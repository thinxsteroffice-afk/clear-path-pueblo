HOW OFTEN TO CHANGE HOT TUB WATER

Drain and refill your hot tub every 90 to 120 days if two people use it a few times a week. If you want a number based on your actual tub instead of a generic one, use this: gallons ÷ (3 × average daily bathers) = days between changes. A 400-gallon spa used by two people every day comes out to 66 days. A 500-gallon tub used by a family of four on weekends lands around 40 days of real soaking stretched across three or four months. In Pueblo West, push toward the short end of that range — our dry air and mineral-heavy fill water drive total dissolved solids up faster than the owner's manual assumes.

The formula that beats the "every three months" rule

That formula comes out of the sanitizer world and it works because it tracks what actually fouls spa water: body load, not calendar pages. Every bather leaves behind roughly a pint of sweat per 20 minutes at 102°F, plus lotion, deodorant, laundry detergent residue from swimsuits, and skin cells. None of that evaporates. Chlorine oxidizes some of it into dissolved salts that stay in the water forever.

Run the numbers for a few common Pueblo West setups:

  • 350-gallon two-seater, one person daily: 350 ÷ 3 = 117 days. Change quarterly.
  • 400-gallon tub, two people daily: 400 ÷ 6 = 66 days. Change roughly every nine weeks.
  • 500-gallon six-seater, four people daily: 500 ÷ 12 = 42 days. Change every six weeks.
  • 450-gallon tub, two people twice a week: average daily bathers is about 0.57, so 450 ÷ 1.7 = 263 days — but do not go past 180 days regardless of the math, because scale, biofilm, and sanitizer byproducts build on a timeline of their own.

Why Pueblo West water changes the math

Three local conditions shorten the interval here compared to a tub in Seattle or Houston.

Evaporation concentrates everything. At roughly 4,800 feet with single-digit summer humidity and steady wind, an uncovered 400-gallon tub can lose 1 to 2 inches a week — about 8 to 16 gallons. Every gallon you top off adds new minerals while removing none of the old ones. Topping off is not diluting. Over a 90-day cycle you might add 100 to 180 gallons of fresh hard water to a tub that never got emptied, which is a slow mineral loading program.

Fill water starts partway up the scale. Pueblo West Metropolitan District water, drawn off the Pueblo Reservoir system, typically runs moderately hard — plan on 100 to 200 ppm calcium hardness and check the district's current annual water quality report for your exact number. Properties on private wells out toward the Pueblo West fringes and in Beulah Valley can test 300 to 500+ ppm with measurable iron. A well-fed tub starting at 400 ppm hardness is already above the ideal range on day one.

Freeze-thaw swings punish neglected plumbing. A January night at 10°F with a tub that's been drained and not refilled can crack a pump wet end or a PVC union. That one risk should dictate *when* you drain, not whether you do.

The three numbers that tell you to drain early

Test strips tell you pH and chlorine. They don't tell you when the water is finished. These do:

  • Total dissolved solids (TDS). Test your tap water first and write the number on the inside of the cabinet door. Drain when TDS climbs 1,500 ppm above that fill number, or at 2,500 ppm total, whichever comes first. A $15 digital TDS pen pays for itself in one avoided early drain.
  • Calcium hardness. Target 150 to 250 ppm for an acrylic shell. Above about 400 ppm you get scale on the heater element, which drops heating efficiency and eventually kills a $150 to $450 element.
  • Cyanuric acid (CYA). If you shock with dichlor, every 1 ppm of free chlorine you add also adds about 0.9 ppm of CYA, and CYA never leaves except by draining. Past 100 ppm, chlorine is chemically locked up — your test strip reads 3 ppm free chlorine and the water still goes cloudy. People in this situation usually blame the tub. It's the water.

Keep pH at 7.2 to 7.8, alkalinity at 80 to 120 ppm, free chlorine at 1 to 3 ppm or bromine at 3 to 5 ppm. Rinse the filter cartridge every two weeks, chemically soak it at every drain, and replace it every 12 to 18 months at $25 to $60.

> If you're adding chemicals weekly and the water still looks tired, you don't have a chemistry problem. You have water that's past its service life — or a tub that's past its.

The best weeks to drain in Pueblo West

Timing matters more here than in mild climates. A drain-and-fill leaves plumbing empty for 30 to 90 minutes and leaves the water cold for hours.

  • Best windows: mid-April through May, and mid-September through October. Daytime highs in the 60s and 70s, low freeze risk, fast refill.
  • Workable: June through August, early morning. Direct sun on an empty black shell can soft-spot acrylic, so don't let it sit uncovered at 1 p.m. in July.
  • Risky: December through February. If you must, pick a day above 40°F, refill immediately, and don't walk away mid-job. A 400-gallon refill through a garden hose takes 45 to 75 minutes.
  • Reheat time: figure 1 to 2 hours per 10°F of rise with a standard 4 kW heater. Winter tap water at 45°F to a 102°F setpoint is a 12- to 20-hour reheat.

What a drain-and-fill actually costs

Per cycle, for a 400-gallon tub:

  • Water: 400 gallons at typical district rates is roughly $3 to $7, including sewer where applicable.
  • Reheat electricity: about 46 kWh to lift 400 gallons from 55°F to 102°F. At Black Hills Energy residential rates, call it $7 to $10, more in winter.
  • Startup chemicals: $8 to $15 for hardness increaser, alkalinity adjustment, and initial sanitizer.
  • Your time: 2 to 3 hours of actual attention, mostly waiting.

Four changes a year runs about $75 to $130 in water, power, and chemicals. Add monthly chemical maintenance of $20 to $40 and an electric bill contribution of $25 to $45 in summer and $55 to $110 in winter for an older, thinly insulated tub. A 15-year-old spa in Pueblo West can quietly cost $900 to $1,500 a year to run.

When changing the water isn't worth it, and when nobody should hire us

Here's the part that costs us jobs. We remove hot tubs — see [hot tub removal pueblo](/services/hot-tub-removal-pueblo) — and we'd still rather you read this than call us by mistake.

Don't bother with a fresh fill if any of these are true. You're paying to heat water that's leaving the cabinet:

  • The tub loses more than an inch a day with the cover on. That's a leak, not evaporation. Fresh water goes into the ground at roughly 25 gallons per inch on a 400-gallon tub.
  • The shell is crazed, spidered, or has a soft spot you can flex with a thumb. Resurfacing a spa shell costs $1,200 to $3,000 and rarely outlives the rest of the tub.
  • The control pack is dead and the tub is over 12 years old. A replacement pack is $500 to $1,200 installed, a circulation pump is $400 to $900, and a new cover is $350 to $600. Three of those at once on a tub with a $500 resale value is a bad trade.
  • The foam insulation is waterlogged. A full-foam cabinet that has been leaking for a season can hold 100 to 300 pounds of trapped water. Nobody fixes that economically.

Who should not hire us:

  • If the tub works and you just want it on the other side of the patio. That's a spa mover with a dolly system, not a junk removal crew. We'd be the wrong and more expensive answer.
  • If it's a small 110V plug-and-play under about 250 gallons and you own a trailer. Four able people, a reciprocating saw, $40 of demolition blades, and a $30 to $60 transfer station fee gets it done in a Saturday afternoon. You'll save real money doing it yourself.
  • If it's still full of water in January. We can't pump 400 gallons of ice. Drain it on a warm day first — into the street or gravel, not onto a neighbor's foundation, and only after chlorine has dropped below 0.5 ppm if it's going anywhere near vegetation.
  • If you're hoping for a credit. Nobody pays you for a used tub with a bad pack. Removal is a cost, not a trade-in.

The failure modes you should know about before we show up. We'd rather say these out loud than hear about them after:

  • Polyurethane foam dust travels. Cutting a full-foam cabinet makes a yellow confetti that gets into gravel, flower beds, and window screens. We tarp and vacuum, and you will still find flakes for a few weeks.
  • Access can force a second decision. Most intact tubs are 78 to 92 inches wide. If your gate is 36 inches and there's no other route, a fence panel or deck section comes off — see [deck fence removal pueblo](/services/deck-fence-removal-pueblo) — or we cut the tub down in place, which takes 1 to 2 extra hours.
  • We don't do electrical. The 240V whip and GFCI disconnect need a licensed electrician to de-energize and cap, typically $125 to $250. Scheduling that is on you, and it has delayed more of our removals than anything else.
  • What's underneath won't look good. Expect a dead grass rectangle, a stained or cracked concrete pad that stays put unless you want it broken out, and possible truck tire ruts on soft gravel driveways.
  • Recycling claims are overstated industry-wide. On a typical spa, only the steel frame, copper heater element, and motors — roughly 15% to 25% by weight — have a real recycling path. The acrylic shell and foam go to the landfill. Anyone promising a fully recycled hot tub is selling you a feeling.

The practical schedule to put on your fridge

  • Every 2 weeks: rinse the filter, test pH and sanitizer, top off.
  • Monthly: test calcium hardness and TDS. Check the cover for waterlogging — a cover you can't lift one-handed is adding $10 to $25 a month to your winter power bill.
  • Every 90 days (April, July, October, with a warm-day January exception): purge the lines with a biofilm cleaner, drain, wipe the shell, soak the filter, refill, rebalance.
  • Annually: inspect the cabinet floor for standing water and the pad for settling.
  • Every 10 to 15 years: decide honestly whether it's still a tub or a 900-pound planter.
