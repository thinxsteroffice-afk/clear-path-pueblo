SHED PERMIT RULES IN PUEBLO WEST

In Pueblo West, a one-story detached shed of 200 square feet or less, used only for storage, does not require a building permit from Pueblo County. That's the short answer. But three things still apply, and they're what actually sink projects here: you must meet your zone district's setbacks, you need covenant approval from the Pueblo West Metropolitan District (a completely separate office from the county), and any electrical or plumbing run to that shed requires its own permit no matter how small the building is. Go over 200 square feet, add a loft and call it habitable, or pour a foundation for a garage, and you're in full permit territory — plans, fees, and inspections.

Verify current numbers before you build. This reflects how Pueblo County and the Metro District have been handling sheds as of October 2026; fee schedules and adopted codes change.

The 200-Square-Foot Line, and Why People Get It Wrong

The exemption comes from the International Residential Code, which Pueblo County has adopted. Section R105.2 exempts one-story detached accessory structures used as tool and storage sheds, playhouses, and similar uses, not exceeding 200 square feet of floor area.

A few places where homeowners trip:

  • It's 200, not 120. The 120-square-foot number is stuck in everyone's head because it's the threshold in other states and in a lot of national blog content. In Pueblo County, you have more room than that.
  • Floor area is measured to the outside of the walls, not the inside. A 10x20 shed is exactly 200 square feet on the nose — a 10x20 with a 2-foot overhang framed as wall is over.
  • A lean-to counts. Adding a 4x12 covered side bay to a 12x16 shed puts you at 240 square feet and into permit range.
  • "One story" means one story. A 12x16 shed with a floored sleeping loft stops being a storage shed. Once anyone could reasonably sleep or work in it, the exemption logic falls apart.
  • Two 180-square-foot sheds are generally fine; one 360-square-foot shed is not. Splitting the structure is legal, though you'll need the setback room for both.

A quick gut check on where the money lands: a permitted 24x30 detached garage in Pueblo County typically runs a few hundred dollars in permit and plan review fees — plan review alone is commonly about 65% of the building permit fee under standard ICC fee tables — plus engineering if you need a stamped foundation detail. That's real, but it's a rounding error next to a $28,000 garage build. The permit is almost never the reason to shrink the project.

The Pueblo West Layer Nobody Mentions

Pueblo West is an unincorporated metropolitan district, not a city. That means two separate approvals, from two separate places:

1. Pueblo County Department of Planning and Development — building permit (if over 200 sq ft) and zoning compliance, including setbacks. 2. Pueblo West Metropolitan District covenant control — the recorded covenants on your lot, which govern siding, color, roof material, height, placement, and sometimes whether a metal building is allowed at all.

The county can tell you yes and the Metro District can still tell you no. Covenant review is a scheduled process, not a same-day counter transaction — plan on a couple of weeks, longer if your submittal is incomplete. Build first and you can be asked to move or remove a finished structure at your own cost.

> The permit isn't what stops Pueblo West projects. The setback and the easement are.

Pueblo West lots are big — a lot of them sit right around one acre, and plenty are 2.5 acres or more. That generosity hides the problem: those lots carry wide recorded utility and drainage easements, frequently 10 feet along side and rear lines and more along major roads. You can own the dirt and still be barred from putting a permanent structure on it. If the district needs to dig up a waterline, a shed sitting on the easement gets moved, and you pay.

Setbacks vary by zone district. Typical residential patterns in the county run something like 25 feet front, 5 to 10 feet side, and 10 to 25 feet rear, with different numbers for accessory versus primary structures. Call with your parcel number and get your actual figures — this is a five-minute phone call that prevents a $4,000 mistake.

Electrical and Plumbing Change the Answer Completely

This is the single most-missed item. In Colorado, electrical permits in most county jurisdictions are issued by the State Electrical Board under DORA, not by the county building department. Run a single 20-amp circuit to a 10x12 shed for a light and an outlet and you need that state electrical permit and inspection — even though the shed itself is permit-exempt.

Same logic for a hose bib with a shutoff inside, or a floor drain: that's the State Plumbing Board's jurisdiction. "No building permit" never means "no permit."

Two practical consequences:

  • Unpermitted shed wiring shows up at resale. An inspector flagging a buried extension cord feeding a shed is an easy repair request, and underground extension cord runs are a genuine fire hazard — they're not rated for burial.
  • Insurance claims get examined. If a shed fire spreads to the house and the wiring was never inspected, expect the adjuster to spend time on it.

What the Site Actually Has to Survive

Pueblo West isn't a mild site. Even on an exempt shed, build for conditions or you'll be rebuilding:

  • Wind is the real load here. Design wind speeds across Pueblo County land in roughly the 105–115 mph ultimate range. A big unanchored shed is a sail. Ground anchors or a slab are not optional.
  • Frost depth in this area is commonly taken at about 30 inches, which is why a 24x30 garage needs real footings and a 10x12 shed can sit on a gravel pad.
  • Ground snow loads around Pueblo generally sit near 30 psf — low by mountain standards, high enough that a flimsy 2x4 flat roof on a 16-foot span is a bad idea.
  • Expansive soils are common in this corridor. Sheds on slabs placed over untested clay crack. If the pad matters, pay for a soils look before you pour.

If your pad needs to be cut, leveled, or built up before anything goes on it, that's grading work — see [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) and [skid steer services](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo). A 20x24 pad on a sloped lot can mean moving 15 to 30 cubic yards of material.

When Hiring Us Is the Wrong Call

Most shed questions reach us because there's an old shed in the way of the new one. Here's where you should not pay us, stated plainly:

  • Small, empty, wood-framed, and you own a truck. A 8x10 wood shed with nothing in it is a 4-to-6-hour weekend job with a sawzall, a pry bar, and two or three pickup loads. Tipping fees are charged by weight — typically in the $40–$70 per ton range with a minimum — so you may be out $50 to $80 total instead of a few hundred. If your time is worth less than that spread, do it yourself.
  • You only have a half-load of debris. Junk removal pricing is volume-based with a minimum charge. Paying a minimum to haul one-eighth of a trailer is poor value. Stack it, wait until you have a real pile, or combine it with a [garage cleanout](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) so one trip earns its keep.
  • The shed has transite or asbestos-cement siding or roofing. Common on structures built before the mid-1980s. We will not demo it, and neither should anyone without certification — that requires a licensed abatement contractor, and Colorado has notification requirements. Expect $1,500 to $5,000+, and expect us to decline the job and hand you a referral.
  • You want the slab saved intact. Mechanical demolition over a slab chips and spalls it. If you plan to set a new building on that exact pad, hand demolition is slower and costs more, and we'll tell you the slab may still need patching.
  • You're hoping we'll pull your permit. We haul and demolish. We don't submit covenant applications or building permits on your behalf, and we can't tell you your setbacks — only the county can, for your parcel.
  • The structure is partially collapsed with an unstable roof. That's a safety call, and sometimes the answer is a licensed demolition contractor with different equipment and insurance, not a junk crew.

What Happens If You Build Without the Permit

Not nothing, and not a catastrophe either. The usual sequence: a complaint comes in, a notice follows, and you apply after the fact. Expect to expose work for inspection — which can mean cutting open finished walls or digging to show footings — and jurisdictions commonly charge an investigation fee equal to the permit fee, effectively doubling it. Permits also expire; 180 days with no inspection activity is a typical lapse window, so pulling one and stalling isn't a fix.

The more expensive failure is quieter. Unpermitted square footage surfaces during a sale, lenders and appraisers won't count it, and buyers use it as leverage. A 480-square-foot unpermitted garage can turn into a closing-table negotiation for far more than the few hundred dollars the permit would have cost in the first place.

The Five-Minute Version

  • Shed 200 sq ft or less, one story, storage only → no county building permit.
  • Still required: zoning setbacks, easement clearance, Pueblo West Metro District covenant approval.
  • Any wiring or plumbing → state permit, regardless of shed size.
  • Over 200 sq ft, a loft, or a garage → full permit, plans, inspections, and probably footings to 30 inches.
  • Call before you buy the materials. Two phone calls — Pueblo County Planning and Development for setbacks, the Metro District for covenants — cost nothing and settle the whole question.
