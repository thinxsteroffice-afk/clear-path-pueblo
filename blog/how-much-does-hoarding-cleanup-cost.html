HOW MUCH DOES HOARDING CLEANUP COST

A hoarding cleanup in Pueblo West typically runs $1,200 to $9,500, with the average three-bedroom home landing between $3,000 and $5,500. Priced by volume, that's roughly $450–$750 per full truckload (about 15 cubic yards, or what fits in a 16-foot box truck). Light-to-moderate clutter in a 1,400 sq ft house is usually $1,500–$2,800 and one working day. A packed home with blocked hallways, floor-to-ceiling storage, and an outbuilding or two runs $6,000–$12,000 over three to five days. Anything involving rodent nesting, human or animal waste, or mold adds $800–$3,500 for containment, PPE, and specialized disposal. Get a walkthrough quote — remote estimates on hoarding jobs are wrong more often than they're right.

The Four Things That Actually Move the Price

Most quotes get built from four inputs, and understanding them lets you argue with a number instead of just accepting it.

  • Volume. The single biggest driver. A 15-cubic-yard truckload is the standard unit. A Level 3 hoard in a 1,600 sq ft home usually produces 4–7 loads. A Level 5 in the same square footage can produce 12–18.
  • Weight. Volume pricing breaks down when the contents are dense. Books, magazines, canned goods, and water-damaged textiles hit weight limits long before they fill a truck. Disposal at the Pueblo-area transfer station runs roughly $45–$65 per ton, and a single pickup bed of soaked paper can weigh 1,800–2,200 lbs. Expect a weight surcharge on paper-heavy or wet jobs.
  • Labor difficulty. A crew moving boxes down an open hallway clears 25–40 cubic yards per person per day. A crew crawling over a four-foot debris layer, hand-passing items through a single navigable path, clears 8–15. Same house, roughly triple the labor cost.
  • Sorting. This is the quiet budget killer. "Just haul it" is one price. "Sort every box for photos, documents, jewelry, and cash" adds 40–70% to labor hours because a crew stops being movers and becomes an assembly line. On a $4,000 haul-only job, full sorting pushes it to $5,600–$6,800.

Real Pricing Bands by Severity

Cleanup professionals use the ICD Clutter–Hoarding Scale, Levels 1 through 5. Here's what each translates to in Pueblo West dollars.

  • Level 1–2 (cluttered but functional): $900–$2,400. One crew of two, 5–8 hours, 2–3 truckloads. All exits usable, no odor, no pest activity. Honestly, this is often closer to a heavy [garage cleanout](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) than a hoarding job, and you should not be paying hoarding rates for it.
  • Level 3 (visible clutter outside, one blocked room, light odor): $2,600–$5,500. Crew of three, 1.5–2.5 days, 4–7 loads.
  • Level 4 (structural concerns, pest evidence, unusable kitchen or bathroom): $5,500–$9,500. Crew of three to four, 3–4 days, 8–12 loads, plus PPE and containment.
  • Level 5 (no working utilities, human/animal waste, severe infestation): $9,500–$22,000+. Crew of four plus, 4–7 days, and often a licensed remediation subcontractor for the biohazard scope. At this level the property itself may need work that has nothing to do with hauling.

What Isn't in the Quote

Six line items surprise people after the fact:

  • Appliance and mattress disposal fees. Refrigerators and freezers carry a $35–$55 refrigerant recovery fee each. Mattresses run $25–$45 apiece, and hoarding homes routinely have four to nine of them.
  • Hazardous materials. Paint, solvents, pesticides, propane tanks, and old ammunition can't go in the truck. Pueblo County's household hazardous waste collection handles residential quantities, but a crew staging and transporting them adds $150–$400.
  • Tires. Common in Pueblo West outbuildings. $8–$20 each for disposal.
  • Flooring removal. Carpet and pad in a urine-saturated home is a separate scope — figure $1.20–$2.50 per square foot including haul-off.
  • Post-cleanup deodorizing. Ozone treatment or enzyme application runs $300–$900.
  • A second visit. Roughly one in four hoarding jobs generates a callback load two to eight weeks later, because items get pulled back out of closets and crawl spaces once the main volume is gone.

> The most expensive hoarding cleanup is the one that gets done twice. Budget for the follow-up before you need it, or don't start.

When You Should NOT Hire Us — Or Anyone

This section costs us work. It should still exist.

Don't hire a cleanup crew if the person living there hasn't agreed to it. Forced cleanouts, arranged by adult children or a spouse over the objections of the person who lives there, have a brutal record. Clinical literature on hoarding disorder consistently finds that clean-outs without the resident's participation see re-accumulation within 12 to 24 months in the majority of cases, and they frequently damage the family relationship permanently. You will spend $5,000 and lose your parent's trust. If the resident is not on board, spend the first $500 on a therapist experienced with hoarding disorder instead. That's a better use of the money, and we'd rather tell you now than take your deposit.

Don't hire out a Level 1–2 job. If you can walk every room, every door opens, and there's no odor, you're looking at a weekend and a rented container. A 20-yard roll-off in Pueblo West runs about $390–$560 delivered for a 7-day rental with a 3-ton allowance. Two people can fill it in two days. That's real money saved versus a $2,400 service call. We'd only push back if there are stairs, if you're over 65, or if the volume is genuinely more than 20 yards — dumpster overage fees of $55–$85 per ton erase the savings fast.

Don't pay a "biohazard" premium when there's no biohazard. Dust, cobwebs, and general filth are not biohazards. Human waste, blood, hypodermic needles, and heavy rodent droppings are. If a quote carries a four-figure remediation line and the home has none of those, ask which specific condition triggered it and what disposal manifest you'll receive. A company that can't answer plainly is padding.

Don't expect insurance to pay. Standard Colorado homeowners policies cover damage from a covered peril — a burst pipe, a fire. They do not cover the cost of removing accumulated possessions. The one common exception is when hoarding conditions caused a covered loss and the debris removal becomes part of the claim, and even then carriers usually cap debris removal at 5% of the dwelling limit. Assume you're paying out of pocket.

Don't start if you're on a hard deadline you can't move. A closing date 10 days out, an eviction lockout, or a code compliance hearing turns a manageable job into an expensive one. Rush scheduling and weekend crews add 20–35%. If you're facing a deadline on a property you're selling or clearing after a death, look at [estate cleanout](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) scoping instead — different problem, different pricing.

Don't assume everything valuable gets saved. Even careful crews miss things. Cash in book pages, deeds in cereal boxes, coins in coffee cans. If specific items must be recovered, either be on site or accept the sorting surcharge. There's no cheap version of both.

Pueblo West Specifics That Change the Math

Local conditions genuinely affect what you pay here versus Colorado Springs or Denver.

  • Lot size. Most Pueblo West parcels run 0.5 to 2.5 acres, and outdoor accumulation — vehicles, pallets, scrap metal, sheds — is common. Outdoor scope frequently equals or exceeds indoor scope, and it's often the part that triggered the complaint in the first place. Metro District covenant enforcement and Pueblo County nuisance codes typically give 10 to 30 days to correct.
  • Septic and well. A large share of Pueblo West homes are on septic. Standing waste inside a home with a failed or overloaded septic system is not a hauling problem, and no junk removal company should tell you otherwise. Get a septic inspection first — $250–$450.
  • Dry climate advantage. Low humidity means less mold than comparable jobs in wetter states. That's real savings; mold remediation is often the single largest line item elsewhere. Expect it here mainly under swamp coolers, around leaking supply lines, and in basements.
  • Scrap offset. Steel, appliances, and vehicle parts have salvage value. On outbuilding-heavy properties, scrap recovery has knocked $200–$900 off final invoices. Ask whether your quote credits scrap or pockets it.

How to Get a Number You Can Trust

Insist on an on-site walkthrough — 20 to 30 minutes, free. Ask for the quote broken into haul labor, disposal tonnage, specialty fees, and sorting, so you can cut scope where it matters. Ask whether it's a firm bid or a not-to-exceed estimate; on Level 4–5 jobs a not-to-exceed with a 10–15% contingency is more honest than a firm number nobody can hit. Get proof of liability insurance and workers' comp. And ask what happens to the contents — a company that donates usable goods to local charities is saving you tonnage fees, not doing you a favor for free.

Phasing works. Doing the garage and yard first for $1,800, then the interior six weeks later, spreads cost and lets everyone see whether the change holds. If it doesn't hold after phase one, you've saved yourself the other $4,000 — and that information is worth having.
