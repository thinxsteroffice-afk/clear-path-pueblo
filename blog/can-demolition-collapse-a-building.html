CAN DEMOLITION COLLAPSE A BUILDING

Yes. A building can absolutely collapse during demolition — and when it does, it almost never collapses the way the person swinging the sledgehammer expected. Uncontrolled collapse is the single most common way small demolition jobs turn into ambulance rides. The mechanism is simple: every structure carries its weight down a load path, and demolition is the act of deleting parts of that path. Delete them in the wrong order and the remaining structure sheds its load sideways instead of downward. On a 12x16 Pueblo West storage shed that means a roof on your head. On an attached garage it can mean part of your house. OSHA wrote an entire subpart of federal regulation about this, and the honest version of the answer has more nuance than "be careful."

How a Building Actually Collapses During Demolition

There are four failure modes worth knowing, and they cover the overwhelming majority of what goes wrong on residential and light-commercial demo in Pueblo County.

  • Bearing-element removal. You cut out a stud wall, post, or header that was holding a roof or floor. The roof does not hover. On a 20x20 detached garage with a 4:12 gable roof, you're looking at 3,000 to 5,000 pounds of sheathing, shingles, and framing coming down at once.
  • Unbalanced load. A wall that was stable as part of a closed box becomes a free-standing panel once the walls perpendicular to it are gone. OSHA's rule at 1926.854(b) says no wall section more than one story tall may be left standing without lateral bracing — that rule exists because unbraced masonry walls fall over, and an 8-foot by 20-foot CMU block wall is roughly 180 blocks at 32 to 38 pounds each, about 6,000 pounds of unmortared dominoes.
  • Progressive collapse. One local failure overloads the next element, which overloads the next. The textbook case is Ronan Point in London on May 16, 1968 — a gas explosion in one corner flat cascaded an entire building corner down 22 stories and killed four people. Residential demo sees a smaller version: a joist bay fails, drops debris onto the bay below, and that floor goes too.
  • Undermining. You dig next to a footing, the soil that was providing lateral support leaves, and the wall rotates into the excavation. This is the one homeowners never see coming, and it is a real risk on any [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) done close to an existing structure.

> The collapse rarely happens while you're working. It happens overnight, or over a weekend, after you've stripped a structure down and walked away thinking it looked stable.

That last point deserves its own line because it's the one nobody warns you about. A half-demolished structure left standing Friday afternoon is a structure with no diaphragm, no bracing, and nothing but friction holding it up. Add a Saturday morning gust and it's on the ground — possibly on top of a fence, a neighbor's horse pen, or a kid taking a shortcut across the lot.

The Five Structures Around Pueblo West That Fail Without Warning

Pueblo West was platted in 1969, which means a lot of the original outbuildings are 50-plus years old and were built by owners, not contractors. Patterns we see repeatedly:

  • Stacked-block piers. Sheds set on dry-stacked CMU with no mortar and no anchors. The structure is held in place by gravity and habit. Lift one corner with a strap and the whole thing racks.
  • Unreinforced block walls. Hobby shops and well houses built of 8-inch block with no vertical rebar and no bond beam. They have essentially zero resistance to a lateral push.
  • Rotted sill plates on slab-on-grade. Forty years of sprinkler overspray rots the bottom plate. The walls are no longer connected to the slab at all — they're standing because the roof is holding them together. Cut the roof first and the walls fold outward.
  • Roofs bearing on something that isn't a wall. A lean-to whose outer edge rests on a fence line, a carport post set in 8 inches of dirt, a rafter tail toe-nailed to a neighbor's shared wall. Remove the accidental support and the roof drops.
  • Attached structures. Garages, mud rooms, and sunroom additions that share a wall, a roof plane, or a footing with the house. These are not shed demolition. These are structural work.

What Pueblo West Dirt, Wind, and Elevation Add

Local conditions genuinely change the risk math here, and it's not marketing fluff.

Pueblo County sits on expansive clay and Pierre shale in wide bands, with caliche hardpan layers in Pueblo West specifically. Expansive soil means footings have been lifting and dropping seasonally for decades — structures on this ground frequently have cracked footings and out-of-plumb walls before you touch them. A wall that's already leaning 2 inches out of plumb over 8 feet has consumed most of its safety margin.

Caliche cuts the other way: it's hard enough that a machine pushing on a structure can transfer far more force than expected before anything yields, and then everything yields at once.

Wind is the underrated one. Spring gusts of 40 to 60 mph across the mesa are routine in March through May. Roof design snow loads in this part of Pueblo County run around 30 psf, but there is no design case for "three walls and a tarp." Anything we partially demolish gets finished the same day or gets laid down flat before we leave the site.

The Paperwork That Exists Because Buildings Have Collapsed

Before any structure in unincorporated Pueblo County comes down, three things matter:

  • Demolition permit. Pueblo Regional Building Department handles permitting for Pueblo West. Small accessory-structure demo permits typically run in the low hundreds of dollars; call and confirm current fees rather than trusting a blog.
  • Asbestos inspection. Colorado's CDPHE Regulation No. 8, Part B requires an asbestos inspection prior to demolition of a facility — regardless of whether anyone thinks asbestos is present. Abatement notification and a 10-working-day waiting period kick in when quantities exceed 260 linear feet on pipes, 160 square feet on surfaces, or 35 cubic feet. Single-family residential is treated differently than commercial, but "different" is not "exempt," and anything built before about 1980 should be presumed to contain asbestos in shingles, siding, floor tile, mastic, and joint compound until tested. A survey usually costs $350 to $900. Abatement on a small structure starts around $1,500 and climbs past $6,000 fast.
  • Utility disconnects. Gas, electric, and Pueblo West Metropolitan District water need to be dead and documented. Cutting a live 3/4-inch water line under a slab floods the excavation and undermines the footing you were counting on.

OSHA 1926.850(a) requires a written engineering survey by a competent person before demolition begins, covering the condition of the framing, floors, and walls and the possibility of unplanned collapse. For a commercial job that means a stamped document. For a homeowner it at least means walking the structure and deciding, out loud, what is holding what up.

When You Should Not Hire Us, and When Demolition Isn't Worth Doing

Do not hire us for these jobs. We take down accessory structures — sheds, detached garages, carports, decks, fences, pool decks, barns, and similar. We are not the right call for:

  • Two-story or multi-story structures, and any unreinforced masonry commercial building. Progressive collapse risk on those is a different discipline.
  • Confirmed friable asbestos, or any quantity above the Reg 8 thresholds. That's a licensed abatement contractor's job, and it happens before we arrive, not during.
  • Selective interior demolition in an occupied building where bearing walls are in play.
  • Anything requiring a stamped engineering letter. We can't produce one. Expect $400 to $1,200 for an engineer to look at a small structure and write one.

Cases where demolition isn't worth paying for at all. A sound 8x10 metal shed with no asbestos and no rot is a genuine DIY job. Two people, a socket set, and a Saturday: $0 in labor versus $400 to $1,200 for us. If you own a truck and a trailer and the structure disassembles instead of demolishes, you should keep that money.

Likewise, if the only reason you're demolishing is a sagging roof on an otherwise solid block shop, a $2,000 re-roof often beats a $4,500 demo plus whatever you build next. And if your slab is flat, level, and uncracked, do not pay anyone to break it out — leave it and reuse it. Where our own service has real limits. Weather shuts us down; a 45 mph day is a no-go day on a partial teardown, and that can push a job a week. How a Small Demo Gets Sequenced So It Doesn't Collapse

The order is roughly the reverse of construction, and it's boring on purpose:

  • Disconnect and verify utilities. Survey for asbestos and lead — lead paint was banned for residential use in 1978, so pre-'78 painted wood is suspect.
  • Strip non-structural contents and finishes first, while the box is still a box.
  • Remove roofing and sheathing before touching walls, so you're never working under load.
  • Take walls down in planned panels, pulling away from the structure and away from anyone standing, never toward the machine or the operator.
  • Never stand inside a partially demolished structure, and never leave one standing overnight.
  • Separate wood, metal, concrete, and shingles at the pile — sorted [construction debris](/services/construction-debris-removal-pueblo) hauls cheaper than mixed, since C&D tipping fees run roughly $40 to $60 per ton and clean metal is often worth money instead of costing it.
