IS DEMOLITION CONSTRUCTION WORK?

Yes — demolition falls under construction in nearly every system that classifies work. Federal OSHA regulates it inside 29 CFR Part 1926, the construction standards, under Subpart T (§1926.850–860). The Census Bureau files demolition contractors in NAICS 238910, Site Preparation Contractors, which sits inside Sector 23: Construction. The older SIC system used 1795, Wrecking and Demolition Work — also construction. The Pueblo Regional Building Department issues a demolition permit from the same counter that issues a build permit, and Pueblo County treats the work as construction activity for zoning and stormwater purposes.

So the one-line answer is yes. But the useful answer is messier: demolition is construction for permits, safety law, and licensing, and *not* construction for a lot of insurance certificates, tax treatment, and prevailing-wage rules. That gap is where Pueblo West property owners get burned.

Where Demolition Is Treated As Construction

If demolition is construction, these things follow automatically:

  • OSHA construction rules apply in full. §1926.850(a) requires an engineering survey by a competent person — in writing — before any structure comes down, covering the condition of the framing, floors, and walls, plus the possibility of unplanned collapse. Fall protection kicks in at 6 feet, not the 4 feet used in general industry.
  • A permit is usually required. In unincorporated Pueblo West, the Pueblo Regional Building Department handles the demolition permit. Budget roughly $75–$300 depending on valuation and whether a separate plumbing cap-off or electrical disconnect is pulled. Structures under about 200 square feet that never needed a building permit generally don't need a demo permit — call before you assume.
  • Contractor licensing attaches. Colorado has no statewide general contractor license; it's local. Pueblo's regional department licenses by class, and structural demolition of an occupied-size building typically requires a license a hauling company does not hold.
  • Utility disconnects are sequenced like new construction. Black Hills Energy gas abandonment and the Pueblo West Metropolitan District water shutoff both require scheduling. Allow 5–15 business days; gas line abandonment has been the single most common cause of delay we see on Pueblo West teardowns.
  • Asbestos law treats it as a construction trigger. EPA's NESHAP rule (40 CFR 61 Subpart M) requires notification 10 working days before demolition even when no asbestos is found. CDPHE Regulation No. 8, Part B sets the abatement thresholds at 260 linear feet of pipe insulation, 160 square feet of surfacing material, or 35 cubic feet of other material.

Where Demolition Is *Not* Treated As Construction

This is the half that competitors skip.

Davis-Bacon. Standalone demolition on a federally funded site is generally *not* covered construction unless subsequent construction is planned at that site. DOL has held this line for decades. If you're a Pueblo West church or district bidding a grant-funded teardown with no follow-on build, you may not owe certified payroll at all — a mistake that costs real money in both directions.

Colorado sales and use tax. A construction contractor owes use tax on materials consumed. Demolition consumes essentially no materials, so the economics invert: a demo invoice in Colorado is mostly non-taxable labor plus disposal. Don't let a bidder pad a "materials tax" line onto a wrecking job.

Insurance certificates. Many general liability policies that say "construction" exclude wrecking, blasting, or collapse operations by endorsement. Ask for the COI *and* the exclusions page. The phrase to look for is coverage for "demolition operations." A contractor can be fully licensed, fully insured for construction, and carry zero coverage for the thing you hired them to do.

Your deed of trust. Removing an improvement from mortgaged property can violate the loan agreement, and most carriers will restrict or non-renew a policy on a structure sitting vacant 30–60 days pre-demolition. Both calls take ten minutes and neither is on any permit checklist.

> The regulatory answer is "demolition is construction." The practical answer is that the label changes which law bites you — and nobody at the permit counter is going to warn you about your mortgage or your liability exclusions.

The Three Tiers That Actually Decide Who You Hire

Forget the classification debate for a second. On the ground, Pueblo West jobs sort into three tiers:

1. Structural demolition. Load-bearing walls, foundations, whole buildings, anything over roughly 200 square feet with a footing. Permit required, licensed demo or general contractor required, engineering survey required. Expect $6–$14 per square foot for a residential structure locally, and $4,000–$9,000 for a single-wide mobile home once title retirement and tie-down removal are counted. 2. Non-structural / selective demolition. Interior partition walls, cabinets, flooring, a detached shed, a deck, a fence, a slab. Frequently permit-exempt. This is where a junk removal crew with a skid steer is the right tool and the cheap tool. 3. Removal only. The structure is already down; you need the pile gone. Pure hauling and disposal.

When Hiring Us Is The Wrong Call

Plainly, because it matters more than the rest of this page:

  • If there's any chance of asbestos, don't call a hauler first — call an inspector. Pre-1990 popcorn ceilings, 9x9 floor tile, transite siding, duct wrap, and old pipe lagging are the usual suspects. A Colorado-certified building inspection runs $350–$700 in Pueblo County; abatement runs $8–$25 per square foot. If we touch suspect material, we've made it a regulated asbestos-containing material and multiplied your cost. We will stop a job over this, and we'd rather you not pay us a trip charge to find out.
  • If you're doing slow DIY demo, rent a dumpster instead. A 20-yard roll-off in Pueblo runs about $375–$525 for a week with a 2–3 ton allowance. If you're swinging the hammer yourself over two weekends, that beats paying labor rates for a crew that stands around. We are cheaper than a dumpster when you want it done in one day and you don't want to touch it. We are more expensive when your own time is free.
  • If it's load-bearing, we're not your contractor. Removing a bearing wall or a foundation element needs a licensed contractor and, often, a stamped plan. Not us. We'll tell you that on the phone rather than after we've broken your roofline.
  • If salvage value exceeds removal cost, you should be selling, not hauling. Barnwood, intact cabinets, cast iron tubs, copper, and newer appliances have real resale value in southern Colorado. A clean copper-heavy mechanical room can be worth $200–$600 in scrap. We'll net scrap value against your invoice when it's meaningful, but if the whole structure is salvageable timber, a deconstruction outfit will pay you better than we will charge you.
  • If a buried tank, well, or septic field is involved, stop. Pueblo West has a lot of older septic systems and the occasional abandoned fuel tank. Tank removal is a CDPHE-adjacent process, not a demolition task, and crushing a leach field with a skid steer turns a $900 job into a $9,000 one.
  • If insurance is paying for a rebuild, your GC may be obligated to handle demo anyway. Don't pay us for scope your adjuster already funded inside someone else's contract.

What Classification Means For Your Paperwork In Pueblo West

Three local details that follow from "demolition is construction":

Tell the Pueblo County Assessor when a structure comes down. If you demolish a 900-square-foot outbuilding and never report it, the improvement stays on your assessment and you keep paying tax on a building that no longer exists. The reduction isn't automatic and isn't retroactive very far.

Check Pueblo West Metropolitan District covenants, not just county zoning. Zoning is Pueblo County; covenant and architectural restrictions may come from the district. On the typical 1-acre to 2.5-acre Pueblo West lot, setback and accessory-structure rules decide whether you can rebuild where the old structure stood — worth knowing *before* you demolish, because a nonconforming structure sometimes loses its grandfathered status the moment it's removed.

Erosion control is real here. Disturbing soil can pull in stormwater requirements, and a bare pad on the windward side of a Pueblo West lot moves fast in a 40 mph spring gust. Our [skid steer services pueblo](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo) and [dirt work excavation pueblo](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) crews regrade and seed-prep pads so you aren't fighting dust with your neighbors.

The Short Version

Demolition is construction for OSHA, NAICS/SIC, permits, and licensing. It is frequently *not* construction for Davis-Bacon, Colorado use tax, and liability policy language. Which definition applies determines whether you need a permit (usually yes for structural, usually no under 200 square feet), whether you need a licensed contractor (yes for load-bearing, no for a shed or deck), and whether your hauler's insurance actually covers what they're about to do.
