PUEBLO WEST HOME DEMOLITION COST

A full house teardown in Pueblo West runs about $9,000 to $18,000 for a typical 1,200–1,600 sq ft slab-on-grade ranch, or roughly $7–$15 per square foot. Detached garages and outbuildings land at $1,200–$4,500. A single-wide mobile home is usually $3,500–$7,000; a double-wide, $6,000–$11,000. Interior-only (selective) demo for a remodel runs $3–$8 per square foot. Those numbers assume clean access, no asbestos, and debris hauled to a Pueblo-area landfill at $45–$85 per ton. Add $400–$900 for the state-required asbestos inspection, $100–$400 for the permit, and $800–$2,500 if there's a septic tank to abandon. Below is where the rest of the money actually goes — and the situations where demolition is the wrong call entirely.

What the per-square-foot number leaves out

The "$7–$15 per square foot" figure is what contractors quote to knock a structure down and haul it away. It is not what you pay. On the mesa lots around Pueblo West, the line items that blow up budgets are almost always below grade or off to the side of the house.

  • Debris tonnage. A wood-frame house generates roughly 100–150 lbs of debris per square foot. A 1,400 sq ft house is therefore 70–105 tons — 8 to 12 loaded dump trailers. At $60/ton average tipping, that's $4,200–$6,300 in disposal alone before anyone gets paid. Masonry, stucco, and tile roofs push the high end.
  • Concrete. Slab removal runs $2–$6 per square foot depending on thickness and rebar. A full basement or stem-wall foundation adds $3,000–$8,000 to break out, and then you owe backfill.
  • Import fill and final grade. A basement hole needs 100–250 cubic yards of structural fill at $18–$30 per yard delivered and compacted. That single item is $2,000–$7,000 and is the most commonly omitted line on a low bid.
  • Septic. Most Pueblo West properties outside the Metro District sewer footprint are on an on-site wastewater system. Pueblo Department of Public Health and Environment requires permitted abandonment — pump, crush or fill, backfill. Budget $800–$2,500 and 1–2 weeks of lead time.
  • Wells. If there's a domestic or stock well you're not keeping, Colorado's Division of Water Resources requires proper plugging and abandonment with a filed report. Figure $1,000–$3,000 and a licensed contractor.

The asbestos rule most homeowners in Pueblo County don't know about

Colorado is stricter than most states here, and it catches people. Under CDPHE Regulation No. 8, Part B, an asbestos inspection by a certified building inspector is required before demolition of essentially any structure — including a single-family home you own and live in. There is no "it's my house" exemption for demolition the way there is for renovation.

Practical numbers: the inspection is $400–$900 and takes about a week for lab results. Notification to CDPHE must be filed and the waiting period observed — plan on 10 working days before machines move. If asbestos is found (very common in pre-1985 popcorn ceilings, floor tile, mastic, siding, and duct wrap), abatement by a licensed contractor runs $2,500–$15,000+ depending on quantity and location.

Skipping this is not a paperwork risk. CDPHE penalties for demolishing without inspection or notification start in the thousands per violation, and the landfill can refuse the load. Any legitimate contractor will ask for your inspection report before quoting a firm price. If someone doesn't ask, that tells you what kind of operation you're hiring.

> The cheapest bid on a teardown is almost always the one that hasn't priced the asbestos inspection, the fill dirt, or the tonnage. You'll pay those three things regardless — the only question is whether you found out before or after you signed.

A realistic Pueblo West line-item budget

Here's a working example: a 1,400 sq ft, 1972 slab-on-grade ranch on a one-acre Pueblo West lot with a detached two-car garage, septic, and no basement.

  • Asbestos inspection: $650
  • Abatement (floor tile + mastic, ~400 sq ft): $3,800
  • Pueblo Regional Building demolition permit: $250
  • Utility disconnects — Black Hills Energy gas cut-and-cap and electric disconnect, plus Metro District water shutoff: $0–$800 (varies; schedule 2–4 weeks out)
  • House + garage demo and haul-off: $11,500
  • Slab and footing removal: $4,200
  • Septic abandonment with permit: $1,600
  • Rough grade, compaction, and reseed: $1,900

Total: roughly $23,900, against a "demolition quote" of $11,500. That gap — better than 2x — is the single biggest reason teardown projects stall halfway.

Timeline: 3–6 weeks from first call to finished pad. Machine time is only 2–3 days of that. Everything else is inspection turnaround, the CDPHE waiting period, and utility scheduling.

When demolition is the wrong move — read this before you spend anything

This is the part that costs us work, and it's the part that matters most.

Do not demolish if the structure still has a mortgage on it. Nearly every deed of trust prohibits removing improvements without lender consent. Tearing down collateral can trigger a default clause. Get written lender approval first or don't start.

Colorado's assessment rates can punish you for creating a vacant lot. Residential property in Colorado is assessed around 6.25–6.95% of actual value; vacant land is assessed at 27.9%. Removing the house can move the parcel into the vacant-land class and *raise* your annual property tax even though the property is now worth less. Confirm the reclassification with the Pueblo County Assessor before you decide — on some parcels this quietly costs more per year than the demolition saved.

Do not demolish before the replacement is financed and permitted. Construction lenders in this market want a permitted plan and a builder contract. A bare pad with an expired plan is the worst possible asset: no rental income, no homestead exemption, higher taxes, harder to insure, and a much smaller buyer pool. If your rebuild funding is "probably next spring," wait.

Do not demolish if a new septic permit isn't a sure thing. An existing, functioning on-site system is grandfathered in ways a new one is not. If soil conditions, setbacks, or lot size mean Pueblo DPHE won't approve a new system sized for your planned home, the existing house may be worth more standing than the dirt is worth clean. Ask that question first — it's a free phone call and it has killed more Pueblo West teardowns than cost ever did.

Demolition is often the wrong tool for the actual problem. Cases where we'd tell you to spend less, not more:

  • The building is structurally sound and the real issue is a full house of contents. That's a [hoarding cleanout](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) or [estate cleanout](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo), not a teardown, and it's a fraction of the cost.
  • You want a bigger yard and the obstacle is a deck, a fence, a pool, or a collapsing shed. Handle those individually — [deck and fence removal](/services/deck-fence-removal-pueblo), [above-ground pool removal](/services/above-ground-pool-removal-pueblo), or [shed demolition](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) — for a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars.
  • A historic or well-built structure has salvage value. Deconstruction instead of demolition costs 30–60% more in labor but can return $3,000–$10,000 in reclaimed lumber, fixtures, and appliances, plus a donation receipt. It also takes 2–4 weeks instead of 3 days.
  • You just need the pile gone after someone else did the demo. That's [construction debris removal](/services/construction-debris-removal-pueblo), billed by volume, and it's dramatically cheaper than a full-service teardown.

And plainly: full structural teardown of an occupied-size home is heavy-equipment, licensed, insured work. If your project is a two-story house with a basement, you want a demolition contractor carrying the right general liability and pollution coverage — not a junk hauler. How to compare bids without getting burned

Ask every contractor these five questions and write the answers down:

  • Is disposal included, and is it capped or billed by actual tonnage? (Uncapped tonnage is where "fixed" bids grow 20–40%.)
  • Does the price include the slab and footings, or does it stop at grade?
  • Who pulls the permit and who files the CDPHE notification?
  • Is import fill, compaction, and final grade included, or is the deliverable a hole?
  • Can you show a current certificate of insurance naming the property?

A bid that's 35% under the others isn't a deal — it's a different scope. Line them up item by item and the low bid usually turns out to be missing three of the five above.

The short version

Budget $7–$15 per square foot for the structure, then plan on another 50–100% on top for asbestos, permits, foundation, utilities, septic, fill, and grade. Give the project 3–6 weeks. Verify the property tax reclassification and your septic permit path *before* the first swing. And if what you actually need is a pile hauled off, a slab broken out, or a shed taken down, buy that instead — it's a tenth of the price.
