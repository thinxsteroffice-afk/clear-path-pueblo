HOW MANY SHEDS ALLOWED ON PROPERTY

Pueblo County zoning does not cap the number of sheds on your property. There is no rule anywhere in the county code that says "two outbuildings, maximum." What actually limits you is four separate things: total lot coverage, setbacks, the size of each individual structure, and — if you're inside the Pueblo West Metropolitan District boundary — the covenants. On a typical 1-acre Pueblo West lot, that math usually lands somewhere between three and five modest outbuildings before you hit a wall, and the wall you hit first is almost always covenant review, not the county. So the honest answer is: count square feet and property lines, not sheds. Confirm current numbers with Pueblo County Planning & Development and Pueblo Regional Building Department before you build or tear anything down.

Why "How Many Sheds" Is the Wrong Question

People call us after a neighbor complaint or a covenant letter, and the first thing they ask is some version of "I thought you were only allowed two." That number comes from HOAs in other states and from municipalities with small lots. Pueblo West isn't either of those.

Pueblo West is unincorporated Pueblo County. Lot sizes here run roughly 0.5 acre on the older interior streets up to 2.5 and 5 acres out toward the county line. A half-acre is 21,780 square feet. A full acre is 43,560. If your residential zone allows something in the neighborhood of 30% lot coverage — the figure commonly used for single-family residential districts, and the one to verify for your specific zone — here's what that means in practice:

  • 1-acre Pueblo West lot, 30% coverage: 13,068 square feet of allowable footprint. A 1,900 sq ft house plus a 624 sq ft attached garage uses 2,524. You have roughly 10,500 square feet left. Coverage will never be what stops you.
  • 7,000 sq ft in-town Pueblo lot, 30% coverage: 2,100 square feet allowed. A 1,400 sq ft house plus a 440 sq ft garage leaves 260 square feet — two 10x12 sheds and you are finished forever.

That gap is the whole story. On Pueblo West acreage, square footage is not the binding constraint. Setbacks and covenants are.

The 120-Square-Foot Line, and the Trap Inside It

Under the International Residential Code, a one-story detached accessory building used as a tool or storage shed generally doesn't require a building permit below a floor-area threshold. That threshold was 120 square feet under older code cycles and was raised to 200 square feet in the 2021 IRC. Pueblo Regional Building Department has used the 120 figure for years; which number applies to you depends on the code edition currently adopted, so make the call before you buy materials.

A 10x12 shed is exactly 120 square feet. A 10x16 is 160. That is not a coincidence — the big-box and local shed builders size their inventory right up against that line on purpose.

Here's the trap, and it's the thing most pages on this topic never mention:

> Permit-exempt does not mean rule-exempt. A shed that needs no permit still has to meet every setback, every coverage limit, and every covenant — and it still counts toward your total footprint.

Four permit-free 120 sq ft sheds equal 480 square feet of structure that nobody ever reviewed. One 480 sq ft permitted building would have gone through plan review, gotten its setbacks checked, and been documented. The four sheds are, functionally, an unreviewed 480 sq ft building scattered across your yard. That's how people end up with a structure 3 feet off a property line and a surveyor's letter in the mail.

Two more things void the exemption regardless of size: - Running electrical to the shed requires an electrical permit, full stop, even on a 10x10. - Plumbing or any sleeping or cooking setup moves you out of "accessory structure" and into dwelling-unit territory, which is a completely different approval path.

Setbacks: The Limit That Actually Bites in Pueblo West

Setback requirements vary by zone district, and this is where you need your actual zoning designation rather than a blog number. What matters is understanding how they interact:

  • Setbacks are measured to the structure, including eave overhang in many interpretations — not to the wall. A 2-foot overhang eats 2 feet of your clearance.
  • Front setbacks are the largest, commonly in the 25-foot range for residential county zones. Side and rear setbacks for accessory buildings are usually much smaller, often 5 to 10 feet.
  • Pueblo West lots are frequently deep and narrow, which means your usable shed zone is a band down the back, not the whole yard.
  • Easements are separate from setbacks. Pueblo West has extensive utility and drainage easements, some 10 to 20 feet wide, and you can meet every setback while sitting squarely on an easement the district has the right to excavate.
  • Wind matters here. Pueblo County design wind speeds run in the 105 to 115 mph range depending on the mapping edition. An unanchored shed is a liability, not a structure — we've hauled off more than a few that relocated themselves in a spring gust.

Pueblo West Covenants: The Rule People Actually Get Caught By

The Pueblo West Metropolitan District enforces recorded covenants separate from and in addition to county zoning. The district has a covenant-enforcement function, and complaints usually arrive as a written notice with a cure window — commonly 30 days — before escalation.

Covenant language in Pueblo West has historically addressed things county zoning doesn't touch at all:

  • Exterior materials and color required to be compatible with the primary residence
  • Restrictions on bare metal or unfinished buildings in residential areas
  • Outbuildings required to sit behind the front plane of the house
  • Limits on outbuilding size relative to the home's footprint
  • Prohibitions on structures used for commercial activity or long-term parking of inoperable vehicles

Covenant text differs by filing and has been amended over time. Do not take a neighbor's word for what your filing says. Pull your own recorded covenants, or call the Metro District office and ask them to tell you which filing you're in and what it says. That call is free and takes ten minutes. A covenant dispute does not.

When Removing a Shed Is a Waste of Your Money

This is the part we'd rather skip, but you deserve it straight. Several situations where you should not hire us — or anyone:

Your shed is small and empty. An 8x10 resin or thin-gauge metal shed, already cleared out, is a 2-to-4-hour job for two reasonably fit adults with a reciprocating saw and a trailer. Trailer rental runs roughly $40 to $80 a day. Landfill tipping fees in the Pueblo area generally fall in the $40 to $60 per ton range with a minimum charge. You'll be out maybe $100 to $150 total. Our minimum for a service call is going to exceed that. Do it yourself.

Your shed is clean metal. Steel buildings have scrap value. Depending on the market, scrap steel moves in the $60 to $120 per ton range. On a decent-sized metal building, demolition is close to a wash or better if you've got a trailer and a Saturday. We'll tell you this on the phone rather than quietly pocketing the scrap.

The shed is sound and someone else wants it. A structurally fine 10x12 that's merely in the wrong spot is worth money. Local buyers routinely pay $200 to $800 for a used shed they can move themselves, and a free-if-you-haul-it listing will move in a day or two in Pueblo West. That's a better outcome than paying us to landfill it.

Your shed is legally nonconforming. This is the one that costs real money and almost nobody warns you about. If your shed was built before a setback or coverage rule changed, it may be "grandfathered" — legal where it stands, but not rebuildable to the same dimensions in the same spot. Demolish it and you can lose that right permanently. If your shed sits 4 feet off the line and the current requirement is 10, tearing it down may convert a legal structure into 6 feet of yard you can never build on again. Ask the county about nonconforming status *before* you schedule a demo, not after.

You suspect asbestos or had a meth issue. Transite panels, certain old siding and some roofing can contain asbestos. That is abatement work under state regulation, not junk removal. We will decline it, and so should any hauler who doesn't hold the certification. Testing first costs a few hundred dollars and is cheaper than the alternative.

You haven't decided what you want yet. If you're not sure whether to demolish, re-skin, or relocate, don't pay for a haul-off to buy yourself clarity. Re-siding and re-roofing a sound 12x16 frame often runs $800 to $2,000 in materials, against $3,000 to $8,000 or more to build new. Make the call, then hire the trade that matches it.

A Five-Minute Audit Before You Build or Demolish

Do these four things in order. They cost nothing.

1. Find your zone district. Pueblo County's online property and zoning lookup will tell you. Your setbacks and coverage limit flow from it. 2. Call Pueblo County Planning & Development with your parcel number. Ask: coverage limit, accessory setbacks, and whether any existing structure is on record as nonconforming. 3. Call Pueblo Regional Building Department. Ask which code edition is adopted and what the current permit-exemption square footage is — 120 or 200. 4. Call the Pueblo West Metro District. Ask which covenant filing your lot falls under and request a copy.

Four phone calls. Then you'll know your real number — which won't be "two," and won't be the same as your neighbor's.
