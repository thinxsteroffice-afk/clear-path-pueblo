HOW LONG THE SHEDDING PHASE LASTS

The shedding phase — the stretch where stuff is actually leaving the property instead of being reorganized — runs 3 to 8 weeks for most Pueblo West households. A single-bay garage: two weekends. A 3-bed home on a half-acre with a shed and a side yard: 5 to 7 weeks. A house someone has lived in for 25+ years, or an estate: 3 to 6 months, and the shape of it is lopsided — roughly 60% of the volume goes out in the first 3 weeks, and the last 10% can eat two months by itself. If you've been "decluttering" for more than 90 days with no truck, trailer, or dumpster on site, you are not in the shedding phase. You're stuck in the deciding phase. Those are two different problems with two different fixes.

The Four Phases, and Why Only One of Them Has a Clock

Every cleanout we run in Pueblo West moves through the same four stages, and people conflate them constantly:

  • Deciding (open-ended, often 6 months to 5 years) — you know the garage is a problem. Nothing has moved. There is no deadline, so there is no end date.
  • Sorting (1 to 10 days of real labor) — piles get made: keep, donate, sell, trash. This is where most DIY projects stall, because sorting creates *more* visible mess before it creates less.
  • Shedding (3 to 8 weeks typical) — material physically exits. Donation runs, marketplace pickups, landfill trips, a hauler, or some mix.
  • Settling (2 to 6 weeks) — you rearrange what's left, buy shelving, and discover the four boxes you deferred.

The clock only exists in the shedding phase, because shedding is the only phase with a measurable unit: cubic yards out the door. A standard pickup bed holds about 2.5 cubic yards. A 6x12 utility trailer, loaded level, holds roughly 3 to 4. A dump trailer runs 12 to 16. A packed two-car garage in Pueblo West is usually 18 to 30 cubic yards once you count the rafters and the wall hooks. Do that arithmetic and the timeline stops being mysterious: 25 cubic yards divided by 2.5 per truckload is 10 trips, and at a 30- to 45-minute round trip to the landfill plus loading, that's 20 to 30 hours of your life.

> Shedding doesn't take six weeks because the work is hard. It takes six weeks because a pickup truck is a 2.5-cubic-yard tool being pointed at a 25-cubic-yard problem.

Realistic Timelines by Project, Pueblo West Specific

Based on how these actually run out here, not how they run in a dense city with alley bulk pickup:

  • Single room or closet: 1 to 2 days. Rarely worth a hauler unless it's a mattress or a 400-lb armoire.
  • Two-car garage: 2 to 4 weekends DIY. One 4- to 6-hour crew visit otherwise. See [garage cleanout pueblo](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) for how we scope these.
  • Shed teardown plus contents: 3 to 10 days DIY, including the day you realize the floor is rotted and the roof panels have 60 exposed screws. A crew does it in 3 to 6 hours.
  • Half-acre yard with accumulated debris: 2 to 6 weeks DIY, because tumbleweed, elm suckers, and old fence posts regenerate while you work. [Yard debris removal pueblo](/services/yard-debris-removal-pueblo) handles these in one or two pulls.
  • Full estate or 30-year household: 3 to 6 months DIY. 2 to 5 crew days. The gap here is the widest of any project type, and it's the one where DIY timelines break down the hardest.

Pueblo West adds friction that Pueblo proper doesn't. There's no city curbside bulk pickup for most of the Metro District, so there's no free pressure valve — nothing disappears just by sitting at the road. Lots run half an acre to 2.5 acres, which means people store instead of discard; a shed, a carport, and a treeline absorb 15 years of deferred decisions. And the wind does real damage to staged piles. A tarped pile of cardboard and insulation on a 40 mph March afternoon becomes your neighbor's problem in about 20 minutes, which is how nuisance complaints start.

The Other Shedding Phase: Trees, Not Boxes

Plenty of people typing this question out here mean the seasonal one, so here are the dates that matter at roughly 4,800 feet:

  • Siberian elm seed drop: mid-April to early May, about 2 weeks. The single biggest gutter-clogging event of the year.
  • Cottonwood fluff: late May into mid-June, 2 to 3 weeks. Peak nuisance, minimal actual volume.
  • Russian olive and locust pods: August through September, 4 to 6 weeks.
  • Leaf drop: mid-October to mid-November, about 3 to 4 weeks, compressed into 7 to 10 days if an early hard freeze hits.
  • Storm limb fall: no season. One wet spring snow can drop more green wood in 6 hours than a whole autumn of leaves.

Leaf and seed volume is light — 20 bags might weigh 300 lbs. Limb and stump volume is not: a 14-cubic-yard trailer of green cottonwood can run 4,000 to 6,000 lbs, which is why weight-based disposal matters more than cubic yards for woody debris.

When You Should NOT Hire Us

This is the part most junk removal pages leave out, so here it is plainly.

Don't hire a hauler if you're still in the deciding phase. This is the number one way people waste money with us. You book a truck, we show up, and you spend 90 minutes opening boxes while the crew stands there. You pay for labor you're not using, and you make keep/toss calls under time pressure, which produces regret. Sort first — even badly — then call. If you genuinely can't face the sorting, that's a different service and a different conversation; a [hoarding cleanout](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) is paced for it, and a 4-hour garage job is not.

Don't hire us for small, light, single-item loads you can move yourself. If it fits in a pickup and you own a pickup, a self-haul trip to the landfill will cost you the tipping fee plus about 45 minutes. Hiring that out means paying a minimum charge — typically $125 to $200 in this market — for something that costs you under $40 in disposal and gas. We'd rather tell you that than take the job.

Don't hire anyone if you need the money back. Haulers dispose. We don't appraise, consign, or run estate sales. If a household contains real value — tools, firearms, mid-century furniture, farm equipment, coin collections — bring in an estate sale company or auctioneer *before* a hauler. We have hauled pieces worth more than the invoice. That's on the homeowner to catch, and the only way to catch it is to look first.

The real failure modes:

  • Weight surprises. Volume pricing has weight limits. A trailer of concrete, roof shingles, tile, or dirt hits legal axle weight at maybe 25% of visual capacity. Expect [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) to price by the ton, not the yard, and expect a half-full trailer.
  • Access. Soft caliche after rain, a 12-ft gate, septic field you can't drive over, or a 300-ft driveway with no turnaround can turn a 3-hour job into a 6-hour hand-carry. Say this on the phone, not at the gate.
  • Hazardous and refused items. Paint, solvents, pesticides, propane tanks, tires, car batteries, asbestos-era siding and flooring, and medical sharps don't go on the truck. Pueblo County has household hazardous waste channels; plan a separate route for them.
  • Scope creep mid-job. "While you're here" is how a $450 job becomes an $800 job. Legitimate, but it should be re-quoted out loud before the first item moves.
  • The last 10%. The box of photos, the dad's toolbox, the kid's trophies. No crew and no deadline fixes this. Budget 4 to 8 weeks for it and stop pretending it's a logistics problem.

One more honest limit: hiring a hauler compresses the shedding phase, it doesn't eliminate it. Two crew days replaces six weekends. It does not replace the sorting, and it does not replace the settling.

How to Actually Compress It

If you want the shedding phase done in days instead of months, the sequence that works:

1. Set a hard end date tied to something real — a closing, a move-out, a family visit. Open-ended projects run 5 to 10 times longer. 2. Sort to four zones in one pass, 10 seconds per item max. Don't open sealed boxes older than 5 years; weigh them, and if nothing rattles, it goes. 3. Book disposal before you finish sorting. The truck on the calendar is what creates the deadline. 4. Batch donations into one trip, not six. Call ahead on furniture — most local thrift operations refuse mattresses, particleboard furniture, and anything with visible wear. 5. Hand off the heavy, bulky, and awkward: appliances, hot tubs, sheds, decks, above-ground pools. These are where a crew earns its fee, because they're the items that stall DIY timelines for months.

The Short Version

Plan on 3 to 8 weeks for a normal Pueblo West household, 2 to 4 weekends for a garage, and 3 to 6 months for an estate you're handling yourself. Hiring it out collapses the hauling portion to 1 to 5 working days. Expect to spend $150 to $250 for a quarter-load, $400 to $700 for a full dump trailer in this market, and expect weight-heavy material to price separately.
