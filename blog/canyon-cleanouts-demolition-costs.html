CANYON CLEANOUTS & DEMOLITION COSTS

Canyon cleanouts and demolition means hauling junk out of the arroyos, draws and steep drainages on Pueblo West lots, and tearing down the old structures next to them: collapsed sheds, horse corrals, rotted decks and barbed-wire fencing. A small hand-carry job, such as a pickup load of tires and scrap pulled out of a shallow wash, usually runs $250–$600. A full arroyo cleanout with a skid steer, several dump-trailer loads and a small structure to demolish usually lands between $1,500 and $6,000. What decides the price is access more than volume. Debris that a machine can reach costs much less than debris a crew has to carry up a 30-foot bank by hand.

Below you'll find what drives the cost, how the work is done, the permits and hazards specific to Pueblo West, and the cases where you shouldn't pay anyone to do this, including us.

Why Pueblo West has so many "canyon" cleanouts

Pueblo West spreads over roughly 50 square miles of shortgrass prairie, and dry drainages run through it toward the Arkansas River and Lake Pueblo. Many lots are an acre or larger, and many sat empty or lightly watched for decades. Over that time, plenty of property owners used the low spot on their land as a private dump.

The same things turn up in these draws again and again:

  • Tires. Often 20 to 100 of them, sometimes half-buried in silt after years of runoff.
  • Broken concrete and block from old patios, foundations and driveway tear-outs.
  • Old appliances, especially chest freezers and refrigerators from the 1970s through the 1990s.
  • Rusted vehicles and parts: truck beds, axles, a car body now and then.
  • Fencing and corral material: T-posts, woven wire, railroad ties and rotted wood rails.
  • Household trash that blew in or was dumped and has broken down into a layer of glass, plastic and metal.

Demolition usually comes along with it. If the arroyo is full of junk, there's often a falling-down loafing shed, a pole barn missing half its roof, or a deck that has pulled away from the house nearby.

What actually drives the price

Volume matters, but it's the third or fourth factor, not the first. In rough order of impact:

1. Machine access. A compact skid steer needs about 6–7 feet of clear width to get in and a grade it can work safely. As a rule of thumb, crews stop driving machines across slopes steeper than about 25% (roughly 14 degrees). Below that line, one operator can load in an hour what four people would carry in a day. Above it, you're paying for hand labor. 2. Weight. Broken concrete weighs about 2,500–4,000 pounds per cubic yard, depending on how tightly it's packed. A dump trailer that holds 14 cubic yards of household junk might legally carry only 3–4 yards of concrete. Heavy debris means more trips and higher disposal fees, which are charged by the ton. 3. Burial depth. Tires and metal that have been in a wash for 15 or more years are often packed under 6–18 inches of sediment. They have to be dug out before they can be hauled. 4. Special-handling items. Refrigerators and freezers have to have their refrigerant recovered by an EPA Section 608–certified technician before they're scrapped. Tires usually carry a per-tire disposal fee, commonly $5–$15 each for passenger tires and more for truck and tractor tires. Fifty tires can add $250–$750 to a job by themselves. 5. What's being demolished. Tearing down a 10×12 wood shed is a half-day job. Tearing down a 30×40 pole barn with a concrete pad is a multi-day project and costs accordingly.

For budgeting:

| Job type | Typical range | Typical time on site | |---|---|---| | Shallow wash, hand-carry, 1 load | $250–$600 | 2–4 hours | | Machine-accessible arroyo, 2–3 loads | $1,200–$3,000 | 1 day | | Steep draw, hand-carry to a staging point, 3+ loads | $2,500–$6,000 | 2–3 days | | Shed or corral demo added to a cleanout | +$400–$2,500 | +0.5–1.5 days |

These are ballpark figures, not quotes. Every job gets priced on a site walk, because a photo can't show how deep something is buried or how loose the bank is.

How the work gets done

A typical job has four stages.

Site walk (30–45 minutes). We walk the drainage with you, find the access route, mark anything you want to keep, and check for hazards. In Pueblo West, that includes prairie rattlesnakes, which are active roughly April through October and like to den in old tire piles and under sheet metal. Expect us to flip material carefully and wear snake gaiters during those months.

Demolition first, if there is any. Structures come down before the canyon gets cleaned so the demo debris and the arroyo junk can go out in the same loads. That's usually cheaper than two separate trips. Our [shed demolition removal](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) and [deck and fence removal](/services/deck-fence-removal-pueblo) pages describe how those pieces run on their own.

Extraction. Where a machine can reach, a skid steer with a grapple pulls material up to a staging area (see [skid steer services](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo)). Where it can't, the crew hand-carries or uses a rope-and-sled setup up the bank. Concrete and rock get separated out because they go to a different disposal stream than general junk. Our [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) service covers that part.

Sorting and disposal. Scrap metal goes to a recycler, appliances go through refrigerant recovery, tires go to a tire processor, and what's left goes to the landfill. On a typical arroyo job, 30–50% of the weight can be diverted from the landfill because the debris is so metal-heavy.

Permits, drainage rules and the asbestos question

The regulatory side is where people get surprised. Three points matter here.

  • Removing debris is generally not the issue. Changing the drainage can be. Taking trash out of an arroyo doesn't usually need a permit. Regrading the channel, filling it to gain usable yard, or building a crossing is different. Depending on the drainage, that can involve Pueblo County floodplain rules or, for channels that count as federally regulated waters, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. We'll remove junk and leave the channel's shape alone. If you want it reshaped, check with the county first, and then our [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) crew can quote that work.
  • Older structures may contain asbestos. Siding, roofing, flooring and some cement panels made before about 1980 sometimes contain asbestos. Colorado's Department of Public Health and Environment regulates any work that disturbs it, and some demolitions require an inspection before work starts. If your shed has old cement-board siding or tar-paper roofing, plan on getting it tested first. Tests often cost $300–$800 and take a few days.
  • Building demolition permits. Small accessory structures sometimes need nothing. Larger buildings, or anything with utilities connected, can need a demolition permit through the Pueblo Regional Building Department. Confirm before anything comes down, because the rules depend on the structure.

When this ISN'T worth it, and who shouldn't hire us

You won't find this section on most junk removal pages. Here are the situations where paying for a canyon cleanout is the wrong call:

  • The "junk" is mostly brush, tumbleweeds and dead branches. In an arroyo, that plant material slows runoff and holds the banks in place. Pull it all out and the next August monsoon can cut the channel deeper and take sediment toward your neighbor's lot. If dead growth is your only problem, clear just the part that's a fire risk near structures. Our [yard debris removal](/services/yard-debris-removal-pueblo) page explains how we scope that.
  • It's almost all scrap metal. If the draw holds a car body, steel roofing and piles of T-posts, a scrap hauler may take it for free or even pay you, as long as they can reach it. Call one first, then hire someone for whatever they leave behind.
  • The job is under one load and you have a truck. If it's a dozen tires and some lumber within 50 feet of your driveway, you'll spend less doing it yourself over a weekend and paying the per-tire fees. Our minimum charge won't be a bargain for that.
  • You're selling the lot as-is at a discount anyway. Buyers of raw Pueblo West land often plan to clean up and regrade on their own schedule. Spending $4,000 to clean a draw on a lot priced for a buyer to improve may not get that money back. Ask your agent before you spend.
  • There are drums, unknown liquids, or anything that smells like fuel or chemicals. We're not a hazardous materials contractor and won't handle it. Unlabeled 55-gallon drums need an environmental contractor, and the cost goes up sharply. Anyone who tells you otherwise is taking a risk with your liability.
  • The bank is actively eroding or undercut. If the walls are collapsing, putting people and equipment at the edge isn't safe. Sometimes the right answer is to leave buried debris where it is until the slope is stabilized.
  • It's mid-winter, or the week after a big storm. Clay-heavy soil around Pueblo West turns to deep mud. A machine that could work the site in dry weather may rut the access route and damage your lot. Waiting 2–3 dry weeks can save you money and some repair work.

> The cheapest canyon cleanout is often the one you schedule for a dry October morning, after the scrap guy has already taken the steel.

How to get an accurate quote

You'll get a faster and tighter number if you send the following before the site walk:

  • Photos from the top of the bank looking down, plus one from the closest point a truck can park.
  • A rough distance from where a truck can park to the debris, even a guess like "about 100 feet."
  • How many tires you think are there, and whether you've seen any refrigerators or freezers.
  • The approximate age and size of any structure you want torn down.
  • Whether you want the channel left as it is or regraded afterward.

Bottom line for Pueblo West property owners

Canyon cleanouts cost what they cost because of access, weight and what's buried, not because of how much junk you can see from the road. Get the scrap metal hauled off first, test old structures for asbestos before demolition, leave the vegetation that holds your banks together, and don't regrade a drainage without checking with the county. After that, a crew with the right equipment can usually turn a decades-old dump site into clean ground in one to three days.
