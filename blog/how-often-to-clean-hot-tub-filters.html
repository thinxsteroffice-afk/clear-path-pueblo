HOW OFTEN TO CLEAN HOT TUB FILTERS

Rinse your hot tub filter with a hose every two weeks, soak it in filter cleaner overnight every 8 to 12 weeks, and replace the cartridge every 12 to 24 months. In Pueblo West, tighten all three: the wind-blown dust, the single-digit humidity, and the mineral concentration that comes from constant top-offs mean most tubs out here do better on a 10-day rinse, a 6-to-8-week soak, and a new cartridge at around 15 months. If three or more people soak daily, rinse weekly. A standard 50-square-foot pleated cartridge runs $30 to $45, so the entire program costs under $60 a year — less than a single service call, and far less than the pump it protects.

The Pueblo West Maintenance Calendar

Generic advice assumes a tub in a humid, sheltered backyard. That is not what you have at 4,800 feet on the west side of the Arkansas. Our dust and pollen load is seasonal and brutal, and your filter schedule should follow it:

  • March through May: Gusts of 40 to 50 mph push grit under covers and into the skimmer. Rinse every 7 days during wind events, not every 14.
  • Late May into June: Cottonwood seed fluff. It can mat the outer third of a pleated cartridge in 3 to 5 days and will drop your flow rate faster than a season of normal use.
  • July and August: Monsoon thunderstorms kick up fine silt ahead of the rain. Pull and rinse after any storm that leaves a film on your cover.
  • September and October: The calmest stretch. A 14-day rinse interval is genuinely fine here.
  • November through February: Dust drops off, but do not rinse a filter outdoors below 32°F. Water trapped in the pleats freezes, expands, and splits the fabric from the plastic core. Pueblo West sees nights in the single digits in January; bring the cartridge into a utility sink or garage instead.

How To Clean It Without Destroying It

The rinse: pull the cartridge, hold a garden hose at roughly 40 to 60 psi at a 45-degree angle, and work the spray down into each pleat, top to bottom, rotating as you go. Budget 8 to 10 minutes for a 50-square-foot cartridge. Rushing it is the most common mistake — a 2-minute rinse only cleans the outer surface.

The soak: a 24-hour soak in a diluted filter cleaner ($12 to $18 per bottle, good for roughly 6 to 10 soaks) dissolves the body oils and sunscreen that a hose cannot touch. Rinse thoroughly afterward; leftover cleaner will foam your water for days.

Three methods that shorten filter life, and what they actually do:

  • Pressure washer. At 1,800 to 3,000 psi you are not cleaning pleats, you are tearing them. A filter with a 1/8-inch tear passes debris straight to the pump and reads "clean" on every test you run.
  • Dishwasher. Detergent residue plus water near 150°F breaks down the polyester fibers and warps the end caps. It also puts body oil in the appliance you wash plates in.
  • Bleach. A 10% bleach soak looks like it works because the cartridge turns white. It also oxidizes the fibers, and a cartridge that would have lasted 18 months is done in 6 to 8.

Two Diagnostics That Beat Guessing

Most people clean on vibes. Two cheap habits tell you the truth.

Time your heat recovery. Once, with a clean filter and fresh water, note how long the tub takes to climb 10°F — on most 400-gallon tubs with a 4 kW heater, that's roughly 90 to 120 minutes. Write it on the inside of the cabinet door. When that same climb takes 25% longer, flow is restricted, and the filter is almost always the cause.

Weigh it. A dry 50-square-foot cartridge weighs about 1.5 to 2 pounds. Weigh it clean and dry on a kitchen scale, then weigh it dry after a few months. A cartridge holding 8 ounces or more of embedded oil and mineral that a soak won't remove is finished, no matter how white it looks.

Also buy a second cartridge. A wet filter is a dirty filter — biofilm has to dry for about 24 hours before it flakes loose. Rotating two cartridges for an extra $35 to $45 roughly doubles the usable life of both, because neither one ever goes back in damp.

The Real Culprit Out Here Isn't Your Tap Water

Pueblo West's treated supply comes out of Pueblo Reservoir, fed by upper Arkansas snowmelt, and it is not punishing on hardness by regional standards — test your own tap with a strip and you'll likely be pleasantly surprised. The problem is what our climate does to that water after it's in the shell.

At our elevation, in afternoon humidity that routinely sits between 20% and 35%, an uncovered tub loses roughly 1/4 to 1/2 inch of water per day. Evaporation takes the water and leaves every dissolved mineral behind. Then you top off, adding more. Over a single 4-month fill cycle, calcium hardness can climb from under 100 ppm to north of 300 ppm, and total dissolved solids from around 300 to 1,500 ppm or higher. That is when scale starts plating onto the heater element and crusting the pleats into something a soak can't rescue.

> A filter that keeps clogging three weeks after a deep clean isn't a filter problem. It's a water problem wearing a filter costume.

Two fixes, neither glamorous: change the water every 3 to 4 months (divide gallons by bathers-per-day times three for a sharper number — 400 gallons with 3 daily bathers works out to about 44 days), and keep the cover sealed. If you're on a well or cistern on the rural edge of Pueblo West, test for iron too. Well water at 15 to 25 grains of hardness with measurable iron will stain a cartridge orange in weeks, and a pre-fill hose filter at $30 to $40 pays for itself on the first fill.

When Filter Cleaning Won't Save Anything — And When Not To Hire Us

We haul hot tubs. We'd rather tell you the truth than sell you a removal you don't need, so here it is plainly.

Most people reading this page should not hire anyone. A clogged filter is a $35 part and twenty minutes. If your water is cloudy, your flow is weak, and your filter is a year old, buy a new cartridge first. We have shown up to quote removals on tubs that needed a cartridge and a water change.

Where filter cleaning genuinely won't help:

  • Biofilm in the plumbing. Slime in the internal lines keeps reseeding your water no matter how clean the cartridge is. A purge product at $15 to $25 followed by a full drain fixes it; a new filter does not.
  • A failing heater or circulation pump. Element replacement typically runs $150 to $350 installed, a circ pump $250 to $600, a control board $400 to $900. No amount of filter care postpones these.
  • A cracked shell or rotted cabinet frame. Unrepairable in any economic sense.
  • High TDS. Above roughly 1,500 ppm, water resists chemistry. You can dump sanitizer in all week and still read nothing.

And the honest math on removal: if a repair quote lands under $500 on a tub under 10 years old, repair it. New tubs run $5,000 to $12,000. Removal only makes sense when the repair stack exceeds the tub's value, when the previous owner left it with the house and nobody uses it, or when it's been sitting dry for two seasons and the lines are shot.

You can also do it yourself and skip us entirely. A reciprocating saw with demolition blades, three strong helpers, a trailer, and an afternoon will do it. Drain first — 400 gallons of water weighs about 3,340 pounds at 8.34 lbs per gallon — which takes 40 to 50 minutes on a garden hose or about 20 minutes with a $90 submersible pump. A dry shell still runs 600 to 900 pounds, so cut it into four or five pieces. Expect a landfill minimum around $30 to $50 for a pickup load; call ahead for current rates. Honest total: a long Saturday and under $150 if you own the tools.

Where we earn the fee is the ugly cases — a tub set inside a deck that has to come apart, a basement or second-story install, a 30-foot gate-less side yard, or nobody to lift with. Our limitation is worth stating too: acrylic shells and injected foam are not recyclable. We separate the steel frame, pumps, and motors for scrap, but the shell itself goes to the landfill. Anyone promising otherwise is overselling. Removal also doesn't restore the pad underneath — you'll be left with a stained concrete slab or a dead rectangle of gravel, and resurfacing is a separate job.

The Short Version

Rinse every 10 to 14 days in Pueblo West — weekly during spring wind and cottonwood season. Soak every 6 to 8 weeks. Replace at 15 to 24 months. Rotate two cartridges. Change the water every 3 to 4 months, because our evaporation rate concentrates minerals faster than the maintenance guide in your owner's manual assumes. Keep a note of your heat-recovery time so you have a baseline to measure against.

Do that and a $35 filter protects a $5,000 tub.
