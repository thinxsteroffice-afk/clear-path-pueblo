What "Estate Sale Cleanout" Actually Means Around Here

Two different jobs get called the same thing, and confusing them costs people money.

A full estate cleanout is everything: you have keys, a house full of a lifetime's belongings, and no sale has happened. That's our [estate cleanout service](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) — sorting, staging keepers, hauling the rest.

A post-estate-sale cleanout is the residual. The estate sale company ran a two-day sale, moved 60–75% of the sellable inventory, and left you with what didn't sell. This is usually 30–50% of the original volume by cubic feet but only about 5% of the original value. It's the heavy, ugly, unsellable remainder — and it's what most Pueblo West callers actually need.

The second job is cheaper, but not as much cheaper as you'd hope. A sale clears the china cabinet; it doesn't clear the crawlspace.

When You Should NOT Hire Us

This is the part most junk removal sites leave out. Here are the cases where hiring a hauler is the wrong call, and we'll tell you so on the phone.

The estate still has real value in it and no one has looked. If nobody has been through the house with an actual appraiser, do not call a hauler. We have thrown away things that turned out to be worth money — a Pueblo West customer in 2023 had us load a box of "old tools" that an auctioneer later said would have brought $400–$700 at a Colorado Springs tool auction. Get a walkthrough from an estate sale company or an auctioneer first. Most Pueblo-area estate sale companies charge 30–40% commission and will do a free consult. If they decline the job, *then* call us.

You have more than 45 days and a working pickup. If the house isn't under contract and you can make four or five trips to the transfer station yourself, you'll spend around $60–$90 per pickup load in tipping fees. Five trips is roughly $400 and two weekends. We'd charge $700–900 for the same material. If your time is worth less than $30/hour on those weekends, do it yourself. That's honest math.

The house has an active probate dispute. We will not load a truck when two heirs are telling us different things. We've walked off exactly one job for this, and we'd do it again. Get a written authorization from the personal representative or executor. Without it, you're exposing yourself, and frankly us, to a claim.

You need it done tomorrow for under $200. Volume is volume. A 2,000 sq ft house does not fit in a $200 job, and anyone quoting you that over the phone sight-unseen is going to re-quote when they arrive. That's the most common complaint we hear about our competitors, and the fix is simple — make anyone give you a number *after* they've looked.

Things we don't take, period: wet paint, motor oil, solvents, pesticides, propane tanks, ammunition, asbestos-containing materials (common in Pueblo West homes built before 1980 — pipe wrap and some floor tile), and medical sharps. Pueblo County runs household hazardous waste collection events; budget a separate trip. Old paint, if fully dried out with kitty litter, we'll take. Liquid, we won't.

The Paperwork Problem Nobody Warns You About

Estate homes contain 15–40 linear feet of documents: tax returns, medical records, insurance policies, canceled checks, Social Security correspondence. Estate sale companies won't touch it. Heirs don't want to read it. It goes in the truck.

Don't let it. We set paperwork aside in a separate stack rather than loading it, at no extra charge, because identity theft against deceased people is a real and boring crime. You want to either shred it or keep it for seven years for tax purposes. Pueblo has commercial shredding around $1–$2 per pound; a typical estate generates 60–150 lbs. That's $60–$300 and it's worth it.

Same logic applies to anything with a serial number — firearms especially. Colorado transfer rules apply to inherited firearms. We're not an FFL and we won't move one.

What Gets Donated vs. Landfilled

Honest numbers: roughly 35–45% of a post-estate-sale load is genuinely landfill-bound. The rest breaks down as:

  • Donation (20–30%): usable furniture, appliances under 12 years old, linens, kitchenware. Habitat ReStore and Posada take most of it. We provide the donation receipt for your tax records.
  • Scrap metal (10–15%): appliances, bed frames, tools, wire. We recover this; it's part of why our pricing works.
  • Electronics (3–5%): CRT televisions are the problem child. Colorado banned them from landfills in 2013, and recycling a console TV costs us $25–$45. That's why every hauler charges extra for the old Zenith in the basement.
  • Landfill (35–45%): particleboard furniture, mattresses past use, carpet, degraded textiles, anything mouse-damaged.

Anyone telling you they divert 90% of an estate load is telling you a story. The particleboard bedroom set from 1998 has no second life.

Timing It Against Your Closing Date

The realistic sequence for a Pueblo West estate with a sale:

1. Death to appraisal/consult: 1–3 weeks 2. Estate sale company prep and staging: 2–4 weeks 3. Sale weekend: 2–3 days 4. Cleanout: 1–2 days 5. Listing photos to first showing: 3–5 days

That's 6–10 weeks from start to on-market. Compressing it below four weeks means skipping the estate sale entirely and eating the loss on unsold value — which is sometimes correct, if the house is carrying a mortgage payment of $1,400/month plus utilities and insurance. Two extra months of carry is $3,000+. If the estate sale would net $2,200, skipping it is the better financial decision. Run that math before you assume the sale is worth waiting for.

We book 4–8 days out most of the year. February and March are our lightest months and you can often get next-day. June through August is our heaviest and you should give us two weeks. Winter cleanouts in Pueblo West are fine — we work through snow — but unheated garages and shops slow the crew down about 20%.

Straight Answers to the Questions We Get Most

Can you work while I'm out of state? Yes, with written authorization and a lockbox code, and we send photos before and after each room. About 30% of our estate jobs are for out-of-state heirs.

Do you buy anything? No. Haulers who "buy" your items are giving you $50 for something worth $500. If we see something we think has value, we tell you to stop and get it looked at.

What if you find cash or jewelry? It goes to you. It happens maybe twice a year — usually in coat pockets, coffee cans, and freezer containers. Check those yourself first anyway.

Is the donation receipt worth anything on taxes? Only if the estate or you itemize. For most people taking the standard deduction, a $600 donation receipt is worth $0 in actual tax savings. Ask the estate's accountant, don't assume.
