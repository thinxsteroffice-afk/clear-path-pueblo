APPLIANCE STORES OUT OF BUSINESS

The best-known appliance stores that went out of business in the U.S. are Circuit City (liquidated in 2009, 567 stores), Montgomery Ward (closed in 2001, about 250 stores), hhgregg (bankrupt in 2017, about 220 stores), Sears (Chapter 11 in October 2018, now down to a handful of locations), Sears Hometown (bankrupt in 2022), and Conn's HomePlus (Chapter 11 in July 2024, more than 500 stores closing along with its Badcock chain). Colorado had its own loss: Ultimate Electronics, based in Thornton, closed its last stores in 2011. If you own a washer, fridge, or range from one of these chains, the appliance probably still works. The things that died with the store are the warranty desk, the parts counter, and the haul-away service. This guide covers what that means for a Pueblo West household.

The Major Appliance Retailers That Closed, and When

Most of these chains didn't fade out slowly. They went from open to liquidation in a matter of months. Here is the short version:

  • Montgomery Ward (closed 2001): It announced its closure on December 28, 2000, after 128 years in business. About 250 stores and roughly 37,000 jobs went with it. Its "Signature" appliance brand is still in some older Colorado homes.
  • Circuit City (liquidated 2009): It filed Chapter 11 in November 2008 and announced liquidation in January 2009. All 567 U.S. stores closed by March 2009 and about 34,000 employees lost their jobs. It started as an electronics chain but sold major appliances for decades.
  • Ultimate Electronics (closed 2011): The Colorado chain filed for bankruptcy in 2005, came back, and was liquidated in 2011. Front Range shoppers knew it as the home-theater and big-screen store.
  • hhgregg (bankrupt 2017): It filed in March 2017 and closed about 220 stores in the Midwest and Southeast. Appliances were its core business.
  • Sears (Chapter 11, October 2018): Sears Holdings entered bankruptcy with roughly 700 Sears and Kmart stores. Eddie Lampert's ESL bought the remaining assets for about $5.2 billion in early 2019, and the closures kept coming. Today only a few full-line Sears stores are left nationwide.
  • Sears Hometown and Outlet (bankrupt 2022): These smaller franchise-style stores were the last places many rural buyers could get Kenmore. They filed Chapter 11 in October 2022.
  • Conn's HomePlus (bankrupt 2024): It filed in July 2024 and began closing its stores, including the Badcock furniture and appliance chain it had bought less than a year earlier. Conn's had stores along Colorado's Front Range, so some Pueblo County homes have Conn's-financed appliances.

Electronics-first chains also took appliance sales with them when they closed. Fry's Electronics shut all 31 of its stores in February 2021, and RadioShack went through bankruptcy in both 2015 and 2017.

What Actually Happens to Your Appliance When the Store Dies

Ranking pages on this topic usually stop at a list of dead brands. The more useful question is what changes for you, the person with a 2012 Kenmore refrigerator in the kitchen.

The appliance itself is usually fine. Store brands were almost never built by the store. Kenmore is the clearest example: Sears put its label on appliances made by other manufacturers. The first three digits of a Kenmore model number tell you who actually built it:

  • 106 – Whirlpool (refrigerators)
  • 110 – Whirlpool (washers and dryers)
  • 665 – Whirlpool (dishwashers)
  • 253 – Frigidaire/Electrolux (refrigerators)
  • 795 / 796 – LG (refrigerators / laundry)

Once you know the real manufacturer, you can buy parts, find repair manuals, and hire technicians just as you would for a Whirlpool or LG model. A "Kenmore" with a 110 prefix is a Whirlpool washer, and any Pueblo repair shop that services Whirlpool can work on it.

Extended warranties are where buyers get hurt. A protection plan sold at a retailer that later went bankrupt may or may not still be honored. It depends on who the obligor is, meaning the company legally on the hook for the plan. Some retailers ran their plans through third-party administrators, and those plans sometimes survived the bankruptcy. Others ran them in-house, and those mostly died with the store. Find your paperwork and look for the obligor's name. If it's the retailer itself, assume the plan is gone.

Haul-away disappears too. When chains like hhgregg or Circuit City delivered a new appliance, they would usually take the old one for free or a small fee. Many Pueblo West households bought from Sears or Conn's and counted on that service the next time around. It's gone now. Big-box stores like Lowe's and Home Depot generally haul away an old unit only when they deliver a new one you bought from them.

The Age Problem: Most "Dead Store" Appliances Are Near the End Anyway

If you bought from Circuit City, the appliance is at least 17 years old, because the last stores closed in 2009. A Montgomery Ward purchase is at least 25 years old. Even an hhgregg appliance is more than 9 years old. Compare that to typical lifespans:

  • Refrigerators: about 10–13 years
  • Washers: about 10–14 years
  • Dishwashers: about 9–10 years
  • Electric ranges: about 13–15 years

Energy cost matters too. A refrigerator from the 1990s can use 1,000 kWh or more a year, while a current Energy Star model of similar size uses roughly 400–550 kWh. At typical Colorado residential rates, an old garage fridge running all summer in a Pueblo West garage, where it often tops 100°F, can easily add $75–$150 a year to your bill. Keeping it for cold drinks costs more than most people assume.

> A store going out of business doesn't make your appliance worthless. What you lose is the safety net around it: warranty, parts counter, and haul-away. You're the one who has to plan for it now.

When Hiring Us Is NOT Worth It

This is the section most junk removal companies leave out. We'd rather you spend your money wisely than call us for a job you don't need us for.

You're buying a new appliance with delivery. If a retailer is delivering a new washer and will take the old one in the same trip, take that deal. It's almost always cheaper than a separate pickup. Paying anyone else to remove one appliance on delivery day makes no sense.

The appliance still works and is under 8 years old. A working fridge or washer has resale value. Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist buyers in Pueblo and Pueblo West will often pick it up for free, or pay $50–$200 for a clean, working unit. Habitat for Humanity ReStores also take working appliances. Try those first.

You have a truck, a dolly, and a free Saturday. A single appliance is a one-person-plus-helper job. Scrap yards pay by weight, though appliance steel ("light iron") brings only a few cents a pound, so a 250-pound fridge may get you just a few dollars. If you have the equipment and the time, doing it yourself is the cheapest option.

You're hoping we can fix a warranty dispute. We can't. If a Conn's financing balance or a bankrupt store's protection plan is the real issue, call the obligor or the bankruptcy claims administrator. Getting rid of the appliance won't settle the debt. If the unit is part of an open claim, keep it until the claim is resolved, because hauling it away can destroy your evidence.

The repair costs less than half the price of a new unit. A common rule of thumb is to repair when the fix costs under 50% of a comparable new appliance and the unit is still within its expected lifespan. A $180 igniter replacement on a 7-year-old gas range is usually worth doing. Call a repair tech before you call a hauler.

Hiring removal does make sense for:

  • several dead appliances at once
  • units stuck in a basement or a tight laundry closet
  • anything with refrigerant when you have no way to dispose of it properly
  • appliances that are part of a larger [garage cleanout](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) or [estate cleanout](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo)

Refrigerant, Landfills, and the Rules Pueblo West Residents Run Into

Under Section 608 of the federal Clean Air Act, the refrigerant in refrigerators, freezers, and window AC units has to be recovered before the unit is scrapped or landfilled. Older units may contain R-12, which was phased out in the 1990s, or R-134a. That rule explains why many curbside trash haulers won't take a refrigerator at all, or will take it only with a certification tag showing the refrigerant was removed.

Pueblo West has no city-run bulky-item pickup like some Front Range cities, and most households use a private trash contract. Before you drag a dead freezer to the curb, call your hauler. Many charge an extra bulk fee, and many refuse any appliance that holds refrigerant. Also check whether your electric utility is running a refrigerator recycling rebate. Colorado utilities have offered $35–$50 for picking up a working second fridge in past years, but these programs come and go.

A Quick Checklist Before You Get Rid of a Store-Brand Appliance

Before you decide what to do with a Sears, Conn's, hhgregg, or Circuit City appliance, run through these:

  • Read the model number and decode the real manufacturer (the Kenmore prefixes above are a good start).
  • Find the warranty paperwork and identify the obligor.
  • Check the age. If it's past the lifespan ranges above, don't put more than $150 into a repair.
  • Check recall databases for the real manufacturer's model, since store-brand units are often covered by the maker's recalls.
  • Price the alternatives: delivery haul-away, resale, a DIY scrap run, or professional pickup.
