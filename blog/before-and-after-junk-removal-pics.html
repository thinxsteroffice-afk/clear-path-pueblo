BEFORE AND AFTER JUNK REMOVAL PICS

Before and after junk removal pics are only useful if they show you something. In Pueblo West, the useful ones show the same spot, from the same angle, in similar light, with the before shot taken no more than 48 hours before the haul. That way the only thing that changed is the junk. A good set takes about 10 minutes with a phone. It can get you a quote without an in-person visit, back up a security deposit claim, document a donation for taxes, or give an estate executor proof that a property was cleared. Below is how to shoot them, what they should and shouldn't convince you of, and when hiring a crew isn't worth it.

Why most before-and-after junk photos tell you nothing

Look through any junk removal gallery online, ours included, and you'll find plenty of photos that can't be checked. The before is a wide shot of a packed garage at dusk. The after is a close-up of a swept floor at noon. They could be two different garages.

A before-and-after pair only proves something if it has three things:

  • The same anchor point. A door frame, a water heater, a fence post or a window has to appear in both shots. Without it, you can't tell whether it's the same room.
  • The same framing. Stand in the same spot and hold the phone at the same height. Chest height, roughly 4 to 4.5 feet off the ground, is easy to repeat.
  • A time gap you can check. Your phone stores the date and time in every photo. A pair taken 3 hours apart is believable. A pair taken 3 months apart may mean someone else did part of the work.

If you're comparing companies by their galleries, look for those three things. If you're shooting your own pics, follow the same rules.

How to shoot your own "before" photos (so they actually get you a better quote)

Most Pueblo West junk haulers, ourselves included, will look at photos before they send a truck. Good photos get you a tighter price range. Bad photos get you a wide range, or a "we'll need to see it in person."

Here's the setup that works:

1. Take 4 photos per area. Shoot from each corner, or from each wall of a room. One photo hides whatever sits behind the front of the pile. 2. Stand about 10 feet back. You want the whole pile and the floor around it in the frame. Close-ups of individual items don't help anyone estimate volume. 3. Put something for scale in one shot. A 5-gallon bucket, a standard 6'8" door or a person standing next to the pile makes it easy to judge size. 4. Photograph the path out. Take 1 or 2 photos of the route the junk has to travel: stairs, gates, gravel drives, the gap between the RV and the garage. On Pueblo West lots, a long walk from the back of the property to the street is often what changes the labor estimate. 5. Shoot in the morning or on a cloudy day. Midday sun at 4,700 feet throws hard shadows. Inside a garage with the door open, it can turn the back half of the space black in the photo. Shooting before about 10 a.m. or after 4 p.m. also usually beats the afternoon wind that knocks over anything you've staged in the yard. 6. Call out the heavy stuff. A hot tub typically weighs 500 to 1,000 pounds empty. A cast-iron tub, a safe or a pile of broken concrete can weigh more than a full room of furniture. Tell the hauler in writing. Weight affects the price more than volume does for these items.

> A photo of a pile tells us the volume. A photo of the path out tells us the labor. Send both.

What before-and-after pics are actually useful for

Contractor marketing isn't the only reason to take before and after pics. The photos you take of your own property matter more.

Getting your deposit back. Under Colorado law (C.R.S. 38-12-103), a landlord has to return a security deposit within one month after the lease ends. A lease can extend that to no more than 60 days. If you're moving out of a rental in Pueblo West and left junk behind, or the previous tenant did, dated photos are your evidence. Take photos of every room after the haul and before you hand back the keys.

Landlords and property managers. After a [foreclosure or eviction cleanout](/services/foreclosure-eviction-cleanout-pueblo), photos showing the contents before removal protect you if the former occupant later says something valuable was thrown out. Photograph closets, drawers and outbuildings, not just the living room.

Estate cleanouts. Executors often have to show heirs, or a court, what was in a house and what happened to it. A walk-through video before an [estate cleanout](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) takes about 15 minutes. It can head off a family argument that might otherwise last a year.

Donation deductions. If usable furniture or appliances go to a charity instead of the landfill, photograph them first. The IRS requires a written acknowledgment from the charity for any single donation of $250 or more. If your total non-cash donations for the year come to more than $500, you also have to file Form 8283. Photos of each item's condition support the value you claim. The IRS can generally review a return for 3 years, so keep the photos at least that long.

Selling the house. Listing photos of an empty garage do more for buyers than a shot of a garage holding two cars' worth of boxes. A [garage cleanout](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) before your agent's photographer arrives is one of the cheaper pre-listing jobs you can do.

What the "after" photo should show

A good after photo shows more than an empty space. Check for:

  • The floor and corners. Small debris like nails, broken glass and old screws collects along the walls. A floor that's been swept shows clearly in a photo.
  • The ground where heavy items sat. If a shed, hot tub or trampoline was removed, photograph the bare ground. Pueblo West soil often holds a dead rectangle of grass, or leftover pavers and anchors. You want to know whether those got removed.
  • The curb and driveway. Make sure nothing fell off the trailer and nothing was left at the street for the county to deal with.
  • The same anchor point as the before shot. This is the whole point.

Before the crew leaves, compare your before and after photos on your phone. If something you expected to be gone is still in the after shot, now is the time to say so, not after the truck has left.

When junk removal isn't worth it (and who shouldn't hire us)

This is the part most junk removal sites skip. A crew isn't always the right call, and before-and-after photos have real limits.

You have 1 or 2 items and a truck. If it's a single couch or a couple of bags of yard waste and you own a pickup, a hauler's minimum charge (single items commonly run $75 to $150 across the Front Range) is probably more than your gas and the landfill fee. Haul it yourself.

The items are still in good shape. Furniture that still works can often go to a thrift or reuse store for free, and some will pick it up. Hiring a crew to haul something a charity would have collected costs you money for no reason.

You're relying on a photo quote for a hoard or a mixed-weight job. Photos are good for estimating volume. They're poor at showing what's buried. A pile that looks like 6 cubic yards of boxes might be hiding a treadmill, paint cans or a layer of wet drywall. For [hoarding cleanouts](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) and anything with concrete, dirt or roofing, expect the final price to change once the crew sees what's underneath. A photo quote on those jobs can miss by a quarter or more. If a company promises a fixed price from two photos of a packed room, be skeptical. That goes for us too.

You're picking a company from its gallery. Before-and-after galleries are marketing. Every company shows its best 5% of jobs. A gallery tells you the crew can clear a room. It doesn't tell you whether they show up on time, call when they're running late or charge what they quoted. Read reviews that mention the final bill matching the quote.

Hazardous materials. Most junk haulers, including ClearPath, can't legally take certain items, such as wet paint, solvents, pesticides, propane cylinders and asbestos-containing materials. A photo of a garage full of half-used chemical containers doesn't mean they'll be gone after the haul. Ask before you book. Separate those items yourself, because they'll need to go to a household hazardous waste drop-off.

You need it gone today and the job is huge. A full-truckload job (around 15 to 20 cubic yards) typically runs $400 to $700 in this region. Whole-house cleanouts can take several loads and most of a day. If your deadline is a closing in 4 hours, a same-day crew may not physically be able to finish. Sometimes a rented roll-off dumpster and a weekend of your own labor is the better plan.

Typical numbers to expect in Pueblo West

These are rough guides, not quotes. Your photos are what narrow them down:

  • Single bulky item (mattress, recliner, washer): commonly $75 to $150
  • Quarter truckload (a small garage corner or a few pieces of furniture): roughly $150 to $250
  • Half truckload: roughly $250 to $400
  • Full truckload (about 15 to 20 cubic yards): roughly $400 to $700
  • Time on site: 1 to 2 hours for a single room or a partial garage, and 4 or more hours for a full house

Heavy materials like concrete, dirt and shingles are usually priced by weight or by load, not by volume. If you have those, look at our [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) page.
