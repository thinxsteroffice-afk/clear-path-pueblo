HOW LONG DO PLASTIC SHEDS LAST?

A plastic shed — resin, polyethylene, "vinyl," whatever the box calls it — lasts 7 to 15 years nationally. In Pueblo West, cut that: 6 to 12 years for a mid-grade shed, 4 to 7 for a cheap single-wall unit, and 15 to 20 for a heavy double-wall shed that's anchored and sitting on a level pad. The reason is altitude and sun. At roughly 4,700–5,100 feet, UV intensity runs about 20–25% stronger than at sea level, and Pueblo West gets somewhere north of 245 sunny days a year. UV is what eats resin. Everything else — hail, wind, snow load — just finishes what the sun started. Below is how to tell where your shed sits in that curve, and when replacing it is a bad idea.

What actually kills a plastic shed here (and it isn't age)

Resin sheds are high-density polyethylene or polypropylene with UV inhibitors blended into the plastic. Those inhibitors are a consumable. They absorb UV energy and degrade doing it, and once they're spent, the polymer chains start breaking directly. The visible sign is chalking — a dusty white film you can wipe off with your thumb — and it typically shows up on south and west panels in year 3 to 5 in Pueblo West, versus year 7 or 8 in a humid, cloudy climate.

Once chalking starts, the clock speeds up:

  • Panel temperature. Dark resin panels in direct July sun here measure 150°F or hotter at the surface. Hot plastic is soft plastic; soft plastic sags and takes a permanent set.
  • Diurnal swing. Pueblo West routinely swings 35–40°F between afternoon high and overnight low. Every panel expands and contracts daily, and every screw hole gets a little wider — a few hundred micro-cycles a year across the 200 to 400 fasteners in a typical kit.
  • Wind. Gusts of 50–70 mph are not unusual on the mesa. An unanchored 8×10 resin shed is a kite with a floor.
  • Hail. Colorado sits near the top of the national hail-claim rankings, and Pueblo County is squarely in hail alley. Fresh resin flexes and survives 1-inch stones. Six-year-old chalked resin cracks.
  • Snow. Design ground snow load around Pueblo West is modest — call it 20–25 psf — but a single wet March storm dropping 18–24 inches gets you there, and UV-aged roof panels are the first thing to fold.

Real lifespan by shed grade

Spend-to-lifespan tracks closely, which is not true of every product category.

  • Single-wall resin, 4×6 to 6×8, $350–$900. Panels about 2–3 mm thick. Expect 4 to 7 years before roof sag, door misalignment, and brittle corners. These are seasonal storage, not buildings.
  • Mid-grade resin, 7×7 to 8×10, $900–$1,800. Reinforced panels, steel-reinforced frame in better models. 8 to 12 years with anchoring.
  • Double-wall resin, 10×12 and up, $1,800–$3,500. Wall sections 8–10 mm with internal ribbing, steel roof trusses. 15 to 20 years on a slab, and the roof is usually the first thing to go, not the walls.

For comparison in the same yard: a galvanized steel shed runs $700–$1,800 and lasts 10–20 years unless water pools at the base, where it rusts through in 5–8. A stick-built wood shed with a real roof runs $3,500–$8,000 installed and lasts 25–40 years with paint every 6–8 years.

Run the cost per year and the gap narrows more than people expect: a $1,800 resin shed over 10 years is $180/year; a $6,000 wood shed over 30 years is $200/year plus roughly $400 every 7 years in paint. Resin wins on cash today and loses on nothing much — provided you accept that you are buying a thing that will be demolished, not inherited.

> The honest framing: a plastic shed isn't a structure you maintain. It's a 10-year consumable with a disposal bill attached at the end.

When a plastic shed is the wrong purchase

This is the part most shed articles skip. Do not buy a resin shed if any of these describe you.

  • You need to store anything that cares about temperature. Interior air in a closed resin shed in Pueblo West July hits 115–130°F. That ruins latex paint, degrades lithium tool batteries, cooks seed, warps vinyl records, and cracks PVC glue. Resin sheds do not vent well, and the ridge vents on most models move almost nothing.
  • Your pad isn't level and you won't make it level. Resin floors are unsupported plastic panels. Set one on dirt with a 1-inch dip and the floor flexes, the walls rack, the doors stop latching within a season, and the warranty is void. Pueblo West's expansive clay soils move seasonally, which makes this worse, not better.
  • You want it to be an attached workshop, a kennel, or anything you'll be inside of. Resin walls won't reliably hold a shelf bracket, a pegboard, or a bike hook. Anchoring loads go into the frame, and most frames have maybe four or six rated attachment points.
  • You're in a covenant-controlled part of Pueblo West and haven't checked. Pueblo West Metro District covenants and Pueblo County zoning setbacks apply even when a building permit does not. Verify the current permit-exempt square footage with Pueblo County Planning & Development before you buy, not after it's standing.
  • You already own a 10-year-old resin shed and a hailstorm just hit it. Patching chalked resin doesn't work. Fiberglass tape, sheet-metal screws with washers, Flex Seal — all of it buys one to two seasons at most, because the surrounding panel is just as degraded as the hole.

And the part that costs us money: if your dead shed is a 4×6 or 6×8 single-wall unit, you almost certainly don't need to hire anyone. Those break down with a cordless drill and a utility knife in 60 to 90 minutes. Pulled apart, the panels stack flat and fit in a single pickup bed — call it 150 to 250 pounds of material. Haul it to a Pueblo County transfer station yourself and you're looking at a small-load minimum fee, typically in the $30–$60 range (call ahead, rates change). Paying a crew $150–$300 to do that is paying for your Saturday afternoon back, which is a legitimate purchase, but call it what it is.

Where hiring out genuinely pencils: 10×12 and larger, anything on a concrete slab you want broken out, anything that collapsed and is now a pile of jagged resin and loose fasteners in the weeds, anything you physically can't move, or a shed stuffed with 10 years of contents you don't want to sort. How to read your shed's remaining years

Walk out and check four things on the south and west faces.

  • Chalk test. Thumb-wipe a panel. White residue means UV inhibitors are gone. Roughly 3–5 years of structural life left.
  • Flex test. Press a wall panel mid-span. New resin flexes and springs back. Degraded resin feels stiff and makes a faint crackling sound. Crackling means 1–2 seasons before hail or snow puts a hole in it.
  • Door gap. Doors that won't latch mean the frame has racked, which usually means the base isn't level. Fixing the base can add 3–5 years; ignoring it ends the shed.
  • Roof line. Sight down the ridge. More than about 1/2 inch of sag over an 8-foot span and the next wet snow is a coin flip.

Stretching a resin shed to the far end of its range

Cheap moves, real returns in this climate:

  • Anchor it. A manufacturer anchor kit costs $40–$80. Ground anchors into clay or wedge anchors into concrete. This single step prevents the most common total-loss failure here.
  • Put it on a real base. A 10×12 slab at 4 inches is about 1.5 cubic yards of concrete, generally $700–$1,500 poured in the Pueblo area. A compacted gravel pad with a pressure-treated frame runs $200–$450 and gets you most of the benefit.
  • Buy light-colored. Tan and gray panels run 20–30°F cooler at the surface than brown or dark green. That's years of UV life.
  • Site it with afternoon shade if you have any. West-afternoon exposure is the worst load it will take.
  • Prop the roof before a forecast heavy wet snow. A single 2×4 center post under the ridge costs nothing and has saved a lot of roofs.
  • Clear the perimeter. Weeds and [accumulated yard debris](/services/yard-debris-removal-pueblo) against the base hold moisture against the floor panels and invite rodents, who chew resin readily.

When to stop spending on it

Replace rather than repair once any two of these are true: chalking across more than half the panels, a cracked or sagging roof panel, doors that won't latch after you've leveled the base, or visible hail punctures. At that point every dollar you put in is rent, not equity.

Plan the exit the same way you planned the purchase. Budget $150–$400 for disposal of a small-to-mid resin shed and $400–$900 for a large one with a slab or a hoard of contents inside — and remember that most resin panels aren't accepted in curbside recycling, since mixed HDPE/PP with UV additives and embedded metal fasteners is a contamination problem for single-stream facilities. It lands as bulky waste either way.
