A CANNON BEACH HOT TUB AT HOME?

Cannon Beach hot tubs are in Oregon. Cannon Beach is a town on the Oregon coast, roughly 1,250 driving miles from Pueblo West, and it's known for vacation rentals with private hot tubs that look out on Haystack Rock. If you're booking a trip there, look on rental sites, not a junk removal company in Colorado. But many people who search this phrase just got back from a weekend soaking in the salt air and are wondering whether they should put a tub in their own backyard. If you live in Pueblo West, this article is for you. It covers what changes when that tub sits at 4,700 feet on the high plains, what it really costs, and when it's a bad idea.

Why a Cannon Beach soak doesn't carry over to Pueblo West

On the Oregon coast, a hot tub sits in cool, damp air at sea level. Daytime highs there mostly stay between the 40s and the 60s all year, humidity is high, and rain softens the sun. Those conditions are easy on a spa. Pueblo West's conditions are close to the opposite:

  • Elevation: Pueblo West is about 4,700 feet up. Water boils lower, and the UV on your cover and cabinet is noticeably stronger than at sea level.
  • Dry air: The Pueblo area gets around 12 inches of precipitation a year. The coast of Oregon gets more than 70. Low humidity means faster evaporation, so you'll top off the water more often and your chemistry will drift quicker.
  • Wind: Afternoon gusts of 30–50 mph on the open lots west of Highway 50 are normal. A cover without working tie-downs can end up in the neighbor's yucca.
  • Freeze swings: January nights regularly fall into the teens, sometimes below zero, and a sunny afternoon can still hit 50°F. If the power goes out for 12–24 hours in a hard freeze, an unheated tub can crack its plumbing, and a repair bill like that often totals the spa.

None of this means a hot tub can't work here. Thousands do. But the relaxed experience you had on vacation came from a tub that someone else maintained, in a climate that was easier on it.

What a hot tub really costs in Pueblo West

The sticker price is the smallest part of the decision. Here's a realistic budget for a standard 5–6 person, roughly 400-gallon acrylic spa:

  • Purchase: About $5,000–$12,000 new for a mid-range brand. Used tubs on local Marketplace listings often sell for $500–$3,000, but you're taking on unknown pump and heater wear.
  • Electrical: Most full-size tubs need a dedicated 240V, 50-amp GFCI circuit. Expect to pay an electrician $800–$2,000. On Pueblo West's larger lots the cost goes up because the run from your panel to the backyard can be long.
  • Base: A filled 400-gallon tub weighs about 3,300 lbs from the water alone (water is roughly 8.3 lbs per gallon), plus the spa itself and the people in it. A 4-inch concrete pad typically costs $600–$1,500 to pour. An old wood deck often can't carry that weight.
  • Electricity: Using a 4 kW heater and around 16 cents per kWh, plan on roughly $25–$40 a month in summer and $50–$80 a month in a Pueblo winter if the cover is in good shape. A waterlogged cover can push that higher.
  • Chemicals and filters: About $20–$40 a month, plus filter cartridges every 12–24 months.
  • Cover replacement: Every 3–5 years here, because of UV and wind, at $400–$700 each time.
  • Water changes: Drain and refill every 3–4 months, which is about 1,200–1,600 gallons a year.

Spread over the first five years, a "$7,000 hot tub" usually costs $12,000–$15,000 all in.

> A hot tub on vacation costs you nothing to maintain. At home, it's a pool you have to chemically balance, heat, and protect from freezing every week of the year.

When a hot tub isn't worth it, and when you shouldn't pay us to remove one

We make money hauling hot tubs away, so you might expect us to cheer you on toward a new one. Here's the honest version.

Don't buy a hot tub if:

  • You used one twice on a trip and loved it. Vacation use doesn't predict home use. A common pattern is heavy use for the first 2–3 months, then once every few weeks by year two. If you can't see yourself soaking at least twice a week in February, it's an expensive lawn ornament.
  • You're likely to move within 3 years. In Pueblo County, buyers often see an aging tub as something they'll have to remove, not a feature. Most sellers don't recover the cost, and some end up paying to remove the tub before closing.
  • You travel for long stretches in winter. Someone has to check the tub during freezes. An unattended outage can cause $1,000–$3,000 in plumbing damage.
  • Your only placement option is an old deck. Reinforcing it or pouring a pad can add 15–25% to the project cost.
  • You need to check with your HOA or the metro district first. Some Pueblo West neighborhoods have rules on placement, screening, or easements. Find out before you buy, not after delivery.

Don't pay for a removal if:

  • The tub is under about 8 years old and just needs a pump or heater. A replacement circulation pump runs about $300–$700 installed, and a heater element costs less. Fixing it is often smarter than hauling it off.
  • It still works and isn't too far gone. A running tub listed for $300–$800 or "free, you haul" usually gets picked up within a week here. Let the buyer bring the trailer.
  • It's an inflatable spa. Drain it, cut it down, and bag it for regular trash or a landfill run. You don't need a crew.
  • You have a free weekend, a reciprocating saw, and two strong friends. Cutting a tub into sections yourself is messy but doable. Expect 4–6 hours and several loads to the landfill, and you'll pay their tipping fees.

When removal does make sense: the tub is 10–20 years old, the shell is cracked, the cabinet is rotting, the electrical is corroded, or it's sitting in the way of a project you want to start. In those cases, a repair dollar is usually wasted.

How hot tub removal works on a Pueblo West lot

If you're replacing an old tub, or you've decided to reclaim the patio, here's what's specific to removals in this area.

Access is the whole job. Many Pueblo West homes sit on 1/2- to 1-acre lots with gravel or dirt driveways and side gates around 36–48 inches wide. A typical spa is about 7×7 feet and 3 feet tall, so it only fits through a gate on its side, and only if there's a clear path. If it can't go through the gate, it gets cut into sections on site. Cutting usually adds 1–2 hours but rarely needs heavy equipment.

Weight surprises people. Even drained, an older tub can weigh 600–1,000 lbs, especially if the foam insulation has soaked up water over the years. That's a job for a crew of 2–4 people with dollies and straps.

The pad and surroundings are separate jobs. If you're pulling the concrete slab too, that's a [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) job. If the tub sat inside a rotting deck or privacy screen, [deck and fence removal](/services/deck-fence-removal-pueblo) can usually be done the same day. Getting a quote for everything at once often costs less than separate trips.

Before removal day: a checklist

Do these 48–72 hours before the crew arrives:

  • Shut off power at the breaker, not just the spa control panel. If you're not sure the circuit is dead, have an electrician disconnect it.
  • Drain the tub at least 24 hours ahead. A 400-gallon drain takes 2–4 hours with a submersible pump. Send the water away from your foundation and septic field, since many Pueblo West homes are on septic.
  • Clear the path: unlock the gate, move vehicles, and pull back landscaping rock or pavers that might get scuffed.
  • Decide what happens to the pad and wiring. Leaving the conduit capped for a future tub is cheaper than pulling it and running it again later.
  • Take the cover off if it's waterlogged. A soaked cover can weigh 100+ lbs and is often the most awkward piece to carry.

The short version
