ALTERNATIVES TO HOT TUBS: 5 OPTIONS

The best alternatives to a hot tub for a Pueblo West home are a barrel or infrared sauna ($1,500–$10,000), a cold plunge ($100–$7,000), a stock tank soaking pool ($300–$800), an indoor soaking tub ($1,500–$5,000 installed), or a patio and fire pit ($300–$3,000). Which one fits depends on why you wanted the hot tub. A sauna gives you heat and relaxation with no water chemistry, and it handles our dry winters better than an outdoor spa. A plunge or stock tank costs far less to run. An indoor tub gives you the soak without a 4,000-pound shell sitting in the wind. The rest of this page compares real costs and explains which option suits which kind of household. It also covers the situations where keeping your hot tub is the better call.

Why Pueblo West homeowners look for hot tub alternatives

Most people who ask us about alternatives already own a hot tub that has stopped earning its keep. The usual complaints:

  • Running costs. A typical 4–6 person spa costs $30–$75 a month in electricity and chemicals. That comes to roughly $360–$900 a year, and winter months cost the most.
  • Wind and cold. Pueblo West is high, open prairie at about 4,700–5,000 feet. Gusts tear up covers, and a replacement cover usually costs $350–$700. A tub that loses power during a January cold snap can freeze and crack its plumbing.
  • Hard water. Water here is on the hard side. Scale builds up on heater elements and jets, so you drain and refill more often. Most manufacturers recommend doing that every 3–4 months anyway.
  • Low use. A lot of owners use the tub a few times a month after the first year, then keep paying to keep it hot.

If that's you, you're probably not trying to replace the hot tub one-for-one. You want the part you actually liked, whether that's the heat, the soak, the social time outside, or the sore-muscle relief, without the parts you didn't.

The five alternatives, compared honestly

### 1. Sauna (barrel, cabin, or infrared)

  • Upfront: Infrared units run about $1,500–$5,000. Outdoor barrel saunas run about $4,000–$10,000, plus electrical work.
  • Running cost: A sauna heats only when you use it, usually in 15–30 minutes for infrared or 30–45 minutes for a traditional heater. Nothing sits there holding 400 gallons at 102°F all month.
  • Pueblo West fit: Very good. Saunas like dry air, and you never deal with water chemistry, freezing pipes, or draining.
  • Downside: You lose buoyancy and jets. If you used the hot tub to take weight off bad knees or a bad back, a sauna won't do that.

### 2. Cold plunge

  • Upfront: About $100–$300 for a DIY barrel or tub, $1,000–$3,000 for an insulated plunge with a chiller, and $5,000–$7,000 for premium units.
  • Running cost: Chilled units draw much less power than spa heaters. Many people run them for $10–$25 a month.
  • Pueblo West fit: A cold plunge works without a chiller from roughly November through March. In July it needs one.
  • Downside: It's a recovery tool, not a place to relax. Very few people sit in 45°F water with a drink and a friend.

### 3. Stock tank pool

  • Upfront: A galvanized 8-foot round tank costs about $300–$600. A filter pump adds $50–$150.
  • Capacity: About 700–800 gallons at 2 feet deep.
  • Pueblo West fit: It's a cheap summer soak. Set it on a level gravel pad, and with our sun it warms up on its own.
  • Downside: It only works for about 4–5 months a year here. You'll fight algae, and you have to drain it or protect it before the first hard freeze. A stock tank heater turns it back into a hot tub with worse insulation.

### 4. Indoor soaking tub

  • Upfront: Expect $1,500–$5,000 installed for a deep freestanding or alcove soaking tub. If you need plumbing moved or a new water heater, the price climbs fast.
  • Running cost: About what a long bath costs you now.
  • Pueblo West fit: It never freezes, wind doesn't touch it, and you can use it in February without the walk across a frozen deck.
  • Downside: It holds one person, maybe two. You lose the outdoor and social side completely.

### 5. Patio, fire pit, and outdoor seating

  • Upfront: About $300–$3,000, depending on whether you buy a steel fire bowl or build a paver patio with a gas fire table.
  • Pueblo West fit: This is often the right answer for families who mostly used the hot tub as a gathering spot. Check Pueblo West Fire Department and county burn restrictions before you buy wood-burning. Fire bans are common here during dry, windy stretches, and propane units are often allowed when wood fires aren't.
  • Downside: It won't help sore muscles.

> The question isn't "what replaces a hot tub?" It's "which thing did I actually use the hot tub for?" Answer that and the choice is usually obvious.

When an alternative is NOT the right move

Getting rid of a hot tub is our business, so it's in our interest to tell you to do it. Here are the cases where you shouldn't.

  • You use it 3 or more times a week. At that rate, $50 a month works out to about $4 per soak. That's cheaper than any gym and easier than any other option on this list. Keep it.
  • It just needs a repair. A failed heater element, circulation pump, or control board typically costs $150–$600 to fix. A new sauna costs $4,000 or more. If the shell and frame are sound and the tub is under about 10–12 years old, repairing it almost always makes more financial sense than ripping it out.
  • You need hydrotherapy. If a doctor or physical therapist recommended warm-water immersion for arthritis, fibromyalgia, or post-surgery recovery, no sauna, plunge, or patio replaces that. An indoor soaking tub comes closest, but it has no jets and you can't move much in it.
  • You're planning on an inflatable spa. Inflatables cost $400–$1,000. They're popular, and in Pueblo West they disappoint more often than they don't. Most can't safely heat below about 40°F outside, wind pulls them apart, and many last only 2–3 seasons. You'd be swapping one problem for a cheaper version of the same problem.
  • You're about to sell the house. Some buyers want a working hot tub. Others see it as a liability. A newer, well-maintained tub can be a mild selling point, so ask your agent before you remove it. A broken tub almost never helps a sale.
  • It still works and you could sell it. If your tub runs fine and is less than about 8 years old, list it on Facebook Marketplace or Craigslist before you pay anyone to haul it away. Working used spas around here often sell for $500–$2,500, and buyers commonly pick them up themselves. You'll make money instead of spending it. Call us only if it doesn't sell after a few weeks or the buyer won't deal with the move.

Every alternative also has a failure mode of its own. Saunas need a dedicated 240V circuit, and if your panel is full, that can add $500–$2,000 in electrical work. Stock tanks need constant water management. Cold plunges usually get abandoned when people buy them as a trend instead of for a habit they already have. Pick an option based on how you actually lived with the hot tub, not how you hoped to.

What happens to the old hot tub

If you've decided the tub is going, it's usually a bigger job than people expect. An empty 4–6 person spa often weighs 500–900 pounds, and filled it can be over 4,000 pounds. Removal in the Pueblo area typically runs about $300–$700, depending on size, access, and whether it has to be cut apart to fit through a side gate. Have it drained and the power disconnected at the breaker before the crew arrives. Most homeowners can drain it themselves with a submersible pump in 2–4 hours.

Our [hot tub removal Pueblo](/services/hot-tub-removal-pueblo) service covers cutting, hauling, and disposal. Before you book, look at two things:

  • The deck or pad underneath. Tubs on wooden decks often leave rotted joists behind. If that's your situation, it can make sense to have the [deck removed](/services/deck-fence-removal-pueblo) during the same visit and start over with a gravel pad for a sauna.
  • The concrete slab. A slab that's 4 inches or thicker makes a good base for a barrel sauna or cold plunge, so leave it. If you want grass or a patio there instead, ask about [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo).

A simple way to decide

Ask yourself what you used the tub for most often:

  • Mostly for heat and stress relief: sauna.
  • Mostly for workout recovery: cold plunge, or a sauna plus a plunge.
  • Mostly in summer with the kids: stock tank pool.
  • Mostly for a long solo soak: indoor soaking tub.
  • Mostly to hang out outside with people: patio and fire pit.
  • Still several times a week, and it just needs a repair: keep the hot tub.

If you're still unsure, live without the hot tub for one month before buying anything. Shut it off, drain it, and see what you miss. That month costs nothing and keeps you from making a $5,000 mistake.
