5 CAUSES OF LOAD SHEDDING EXPLAINED

Load shedding is a deliberate, controlled power cut. A grid operator or utility switches off power to some customers on purpose so the whole system doesn't collapse. It happens for four main reasons. Demand outruns supply, usually during extreme heat or cold. Power plants trip offline without warning. Transmission lines can't carry enough power to where it's needed. Grid frequency drops below safe limits, around 59.3 Hz on a system designed to run at 60 Hz. In Pueblo West, a fifth reason has become common in recent years: wildfire-driven shutoffs. Utilities cut power ahead of high winds so their lines don't start a grass fire. Each of these works differently, and each means something different for your house.

We're a junk removal company, not a utility, so read this as a neighbor's explainer. We'll also be plain about where our work can help and where it can't.

The Four Grid-Level Causes, Explained Plainly

The North American grid has to match supply and demand almost exactly, second by second. When the two drift apart, the frequency changes. If it falls too far, generators start tripping off to protect themselves, and that can cascade into a wide blackout that takes days to restore. Load shedding sacrifices a few neighborhoods for an hour so the whole region doesn't go dark for a week.

  • Demand exceeds available generation. This is the classic cause. During a heat wave or a deep cold snap, everyone runs air conditioning or electric heat at once. If the utility can't buy or generate enough power, it has to cut load. The August 2020 California rolling blackouts hit roughly 800,000 customers over two evenings, the state's first rotating outages since 2001.
  • Sudden generation loss. A large plant can trip offline in seconds. Close to home, Xcel's Comanche 3 unit in Pueblo is roughly 750 MW, and it spent months offline in 2020 and again in 2023. Losing a unit that size forces the regional system to scramble for replacement power.
  • Transmission constraints. Sometimes there's enough power somewhere, but the lines carrying it are maxed out or damaged. The power exists. It just can't reach you.
  • Under-frequency events. Automatic relays called under-frequency load shedding (UFLS) relays trip blocks of customers when frequency falls past set thresholds. The first stage is commonly around 59.3 Hz. Nobody presses a button. It happens in fractions of a second.

The worst recent example is Texas during Winter Storm Uri in February 2021. ERCOT shed about 20,000 MW of load, and about 4.5 million homes and businesses lost power. Grid operators later said the system came within roughly 4 minutes and 37 seconds of a total collapse that could have lasted weeks or months. That's what load shedding prevents, even when it doesn't feel like it from a dark kitchen.

The Pueblo West-Specific Cause: Wildfire Shutoffs

Most Pueblo West homes get electricity from Black Hills Energy. Some outlying parcels are served by San Isabel Electric, which has its headquarters right here in Pueblo West. Check your bill if you're not sure which you have, because the two have different outage notification systems.

Technically, a public safety power shutoff (PSPS) isn't load shedding, but residents experience it the same way: the power goes off on purpose, sometimes for longer than a rolling blackout. In April 2024, Xcel Energy cut power to about 55,000 customers along the Front Range ahead of a wind event. That was the first large PSPS in Colorado and showed the practice had arrived here. Pueblo West has the conditions that trigger these shutoffs: dry grass, low humidity, and spring wind gusts that regularly top 50 mph.

> A rolling blackout usually lasts an hour or two. A wildfire shutoff can last a day or more, because crews have to physically patrol the lines before they turn the power back on.

The practical difference:

  • Rolling load shedding usually rotates through neighborhoods in blocks of about 15 to 60 minutes each.
  • PSPS events can run 12 to 48 hours or longer, depending on how long the wind lasts and how many miles of line need inspection.
  • Equipment outages caused by storms, ice, or a car hitting a pole aren't deliberate at all. They're simply outages, though people often call them "load shedding."

When Load Shedding Is Most Likely Here

Grid stress on the Front Range follows a fairly predictable pattern:

  • Summer evenings, roughly 4 to 8 p.m., when solar output drops while air conditioning demand is still peaking. Utilities call this the "net peak."
  • Multi-day Arctic cold snaps, when natural gas supply to power plants gets squeezed at the same moment heating demand spikes. That's what broke Texas in 2021.
  • Spring red-flag days, March through May, when PSPS risk is highest in our area.
  • Late summer heat waves that cover the entire western U.S. at once. When every state is hot simultaneously, Colorado utilities can't import cheap surplus power from neighbors, because the neighbors don't have any to spare.

Where Junk Removal Fits In (A Small, Honest Part)

Your household load matters a little at the margin, mostly to your own bill. The biggest hidden load we find in Pueblo West homes is the second refrigerator or chest freezer in the garage.

A refrigerator built in the 1990s can use 1,000 to 1,400 kWh a year. A comparable modern Energy Star model uses about 400 to 450 kWh. Put that old unit in a Pueblo West garage, where summer temperatures can pass 100°F, and the compressor runs even harder. At residential rates in the mid-teens of cents per kWh, an old garage fridge can cost $100 to $200 a year to run. It often holds a case of soda and some freezer-burned venison.

Removing that unit won't keep your neighborhood on during a grid emergency. It does lower your bill every month, and it's one fewer thing pulling power during the 4–8 p.m. peak, when utilities are asking customers to cut back. When You Should NOT Hire Us (Or Anyone Like Us)

This section matters more than the rest of the page, so here it is plainly:

  • If your appliance is newer than about 2014 and works, keep it. The energy savings from replacing a reasonably modern fridge are too small to justify the cost of hauling it away plus a new unit. You'd need a decade or more to break even.
  • If your goal is to "prevent load shedding," junk removal won't do it. Load shedding is caused by things that happen at the grid level, such as plant outages, transmission limits, and weather across the whole region. Hauling one fridge has no measurable effect on whether your neighborhood gets rotated off.
  • If your utility currently offers an appliance recycling rebate, use that first. Utility recycling programs often pick up working fridges for free and sometimes pay you $35 to $75 for them. That beats paying a hauler. Check with Black Hills Energy or San Isabel before you call us. We'd rather lose the job than have you pay for something you could get free.
  • If any debris is touching or near a power line, don't call us. Call your utility. OSHA requires unqualified workers to stay at least 10 feet from overhead lines, and we follow that. A limb draped across a service drop is the utility's job, full stop. Treat every downed line as live.
  • If you're dealing with spoiled food after a long shutoff, your regular trash service is usually cheaper than a junk removal visit for a few bags. Call us when it's the whole freezer, not just what was inside it.
  • If the real problem is outage resilience, you need an electrician or a battery and generator installer, not a hauler. We remove things. We don't install backup power.

Our main limitation is simple: we can lower your energy use at the margin and clean up after storms, but we can't make your power more reliable.

What Actually Helps During a Load Shedding Event

A few things are worth doing before the next red-flag day or heat wave:

  • Sign up for outage alerts from Black Hills Energy or San Isabel Electric so you get PSPS warnings, which typically arrive 24 to 48 hours ahead when the forecast allows.
  • Keep fridges and freezers closed. A full freezer holds food safely for about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24 hours, and a refrigerator about 4 hours.
  • Pre-cool or pre-heat your home before the 4–8 p.m. peak during extreme weather.
  • If anyone in your home depends on powered medical equipment, register with your utility's medical-needs program. It doesn't guarantee your power stays on, but it can get you earlier notice.
  • Clear dry brush and junk piles from within 5 feet of your house. That's the "Zone 0" that Colorado wildfire guidance stresses most. Here, cleaning up actually does reduce wildfire risk.

The Short Version

Load shedding happens when supply can't keep up with demand, a power plant trips offline, transmission lines max out, or grid frequency sags dangerously. In Pueblo West, wildfire shutoffs on dry, windy days are now a cause too. It's a grid problem, solved at the grid level. The only household-level response with a real payback is getting rid of the energy hogs you don't need, and only if they're truly old and your utility isn't offering to take them for free.
