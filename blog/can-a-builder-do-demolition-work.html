CAN A BUILDER DO DEMOLITION WORK?

Yes — in Pueblo West, a general builder can legally perform demolition work, and most of them do it routinely as part of remodels, additions, and teardowns. Colorado has no statewide general contractor license, so the authority comes from the local building department, not the state. What matters is whether your builder holds the right license class with the Pueblo Regional Building Department, whether the job needs a separate demolition permit, and whether asbestos rules apply. The real question isn't legality. It's whether using your builder for demo is cheaper and faster than splitting the work — and for roughly half the jobs we see in Pueblo West, it isn't.

What "builder can do demolition" actually means in Pueblo County

There are three different jobs people call "demolition," and they have different rules:

  • Selective / interior demo — gutting a kitchen, pulling out a bathroom, removing a non-bearing wall. Almost always folded into the builder's existing remodel permit. No separate demo permit in most cases.
  • Structural demo — removing a bearing wall, taking a roof off, cutting back part of a house for an addition. Needs engineering and shows up on the building permit drawings.
  • Full structure demolition — the whole house, the detached garage, the barn. This is its own permit with the Pueblo Regional Building Department, plus utility disconnect verification, plus an asbestos step.

A builder licensed for new residential construction is generally cleared for all three. But "cleared to do it" and "set up to do it profitably" are separate things, and that gap is where your money goes. Confirm the current license class and permit requirements with the Pueblo Regional Building Department before you schedule anything — fee schedules and classifications change.

The asbestos rule that catches Pueblo West homeowners

This is the single most-skipped step, and it's the one that stops a job cold.

Colorado's CDPHE Regulation No. 8 sets abatement trigger levels at 260 linear feet of asbestos-containing material on pipes, 160 square feet on other surfaces, or 35 cubic feet where length and area can't be measured. Hit those, and you need a certified abatement contractor — not your builder, not us, not anyone without the specific CDPHE certification. Full structure demolitions typically require a pre-demolition inspection by a certified asbestos building inspector and a written notification to CDPHE with roughly a 10-working-day waiting period before demo starts.

A pre-demo asbestos inspection in the Pueblo area runs about $400 to $850 depending on square footage and sample count. That's not optional overhead. Pueblo West built out heavily from the mid-1970s through the 1980s, and that era's popcorn ceiling texture, vinyl sheet flooring with felt backing, and HVAC duct tape are the three most common positive hits we see. If your builder says "we'll just knock it out this weekend" on a 1979 ranch, that's a warning sign, not efficiency.

> A builder who can't tell you the asbestos trigger numbers off the top of his head has probably never pulled a full demolition permit in Pueblo County. Ask before you sign.

What a builder genuinely does better

Give the demo to your builder when:

  • The demo and the rebuild are the same wall. If someone has to open a wall, discover the framing condition, and immediately decide how to rebuild it, splitting that across two companies costs you days.
  • There's a structural sequence. Temporary shoring, load transfer, beam installation — that's the builder's engineering, and it should be the builder's liability.
  • The permit is already open. Folding interior demo into an existing remodel permit avoids a second application and a second inspection cycle.
  • The job is small. Under roughly 4 cubic yards of debris, the coordination cost of a second vendor eats the savings.

Where builder-led demolition goes wrong — the part that costs us nothing to admit

Builders subcontract demo and hauling more often than they perform it, and they mark it up. Standard general contractor markup on subcontracted work runs 10% to 20%. On a $3,500 demo-and-haul line item, that's $350 to $700 you're paying for a phone call.

The bigger issue is disposal. On most teardown and cleanout jobs, 40% to 60% of the total cost is hauling and tipping, not breaking things. Area landfills charge by weight — budget roughly $45 to $75 per ton for mixed construction debris, and call ahead, because clean concrete, mixed C&D, and green waste all price differently and some loads get rejected outright at the scale. Builders who don't run their own trailers order a 20-yard roll-off at $425 to $650 per week and eat the overage fees when it fills in two days.

Three failure modes we see repeatedly in Pueblo West:

  • The roll-off sits. A container parked on your driveway for 11 days on a 3-day job. Pueblo West Metro District covenants cover property appearance, and a neighbor complaint mid-project is a real possibility on the larger residential lots.
  • Wind scatter. Spring gusts through Pueblo West regularly hit 50 to 60 mph. An uncovered open-top full of drywall scrap becomes your neighbor's problem in about four minutes.
  • The backfill afterthought. Pull a foundation or a pool and leave the void filled with loose demo rubble, and Pueblo West's expansive clay does the rest over the next two winters. Proper fill and compaction is a separate line item. If it's not written down, it wasn't priced.

When you should NOT hire us — or anyone — for standalone demo

We'd rather lose the job than sell you the wrong one. Don't call us when:

  • The asbestos inspection came back positive above trigger levels. We are not an abatement contractor. Hire a CDPHE-certified abatement firm first. Anyone who offers to "just be careful with it" is exposing you to liability that lands on the property owner.
  • It's genuinely structural. Bearing walls, roof loads, anything needing shoring. That belongs to the builder and their engineer, full stop.
  • The debris fits in a pickup. One load of scrap? The Pueblo transfer station is open to the public. You'll spend $40 and an afternoon instead of a service call minimum.
  • You're mid-remodel with an active builder and a tight schedule. Adding a second vendor to a sequenced job introduces a handoff. Sometimes the markup is cheaper than the delay. Pay it.
  • You want the cheapest possible number and nothing else. A guy with a trailer from a marketplace ad will beat us on price. He also may not carry the $1M/$2M general liability coverage that matters when a bobcat clips a gas line.
