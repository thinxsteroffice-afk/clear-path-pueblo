HOW LONG IN A HOT TUB IS SAFE

Fifteen to twenty minutes is the ceiling for a soak at 102–104°F. At 100°F you can comfortably stay in 30 to 45 minutes. At 104°F — the maximum temperature the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission has warned about for decades — cap it at 10 to 15 minutes, get out, cool down for 10 minutes, and go back in if you want a second round. Water at 104°F raises your core body temperature roughly 1°F every 10 minutes, and once you clear 102.2°F internally you're into dizziness, nausea, and dropped blood pressure. Kids under 5, pregnant soakers, and anyone on blood pressure medication should cut all of those numbers roughly in half.

That's the short answer. Here's the part almost nobody writing about hot tub time accounts for: you're soaking at 4,860 feet.

Pueblo West changes the math

Pueblo West sits at roughly 4,860 feet above sea level, and the air here runs 25–40% relative humidity most of the year. Both of those work against you in a hot tub.

At altitude your resting heart rate is already 5–10 beats per minute higher than it would be at sea level, and hot water adds another 15–30 bpm on top of that. Hot water immersion is a mild cardiovascular load — comparable to a brisk walk — and you're starting from a higher baseline.

The dry air matters more than most people realize. In humid climates, your sweat sits on your skin and you *feel* overheated, which is your body's warning system working correctly. In Pueblo West, that sweat evaporates off your shoulders and face almost instantly. You feel fine. You feel great, actually. Meanwhile you're losing 12 to 16 ounces of fluid in a 20-minute soak and getting none of the normal "I'm cooking" feedback.

Practical adjustments for this market:

  • Drink 16 oz of water before you get in, and keep another 16 oz within arm's reach. Not beer. Alcohol plus 104°F water is how people fall asleep in hot tubs.
  • Set a timer on your phone. Your own sense of elapsed time in hot water is unreliable, and here the usual physical cues are muted.
  • Run 100–102°F in July and August instead of 104°F. A 104°F soak on a 96°F Pueblo West evening gives your body nowhere to dump heat afterward.
  • Expect to top off 1–3 inches of water per week in summer from evaporation alone. That's not a leak. That's 4,860 feet and single-digit dew points.
  • Get out immediately if you feel lightheaded, your ears start ringing, or your heartbeat feels "loud." Sit on the step with your legs in the water for two minutes before standing up fully.

The other "how long" — how long a hot tub lasts here

Plenty of people searching this phrase aren't asking about soak time. They're asking how long the tub itself is going to last, usually because something just broke.

A quality acrylic shell with a full foam cabinet lasts 15 to 20 years. An entry-level tub from a big-box store or a Craigslist handoff lasts 7 to 10 years, and in this climate often less. Pueblo West is brutal on hot tubs for three specific reasons:

  • UV intensity. At 4,860 feet you get roughly 20–25% more UV than at sea level. Vinyl covers that are rated for 5–7 years in Seattle start delaminating here in 3 to 4. A waterlogged cover weighing 90 lbs instead of 45 lbs is the single most common "my tub is dying" symptom we see.
  • Freeze-thaw swing. A 40–50°F daily temperature spread in spring and fall is normal here. That cycles seals, unions, and jets thousands of times a year.
  • Hard water. Southern Colorado municipal water runs hard, and scale builds on heater elements fast. A scaled element loses efficiency long before it fails outright, and you pay for that in kilowatt-hours.

On operating cost: a 240V tub kept at 100–102°F year-round in this climate pulls something like 200 to 500 kWh a month depending on cover condition and insulation. At roughly $0.13–$0.15 per kWh, that's $26 to $75 a month, with January running double July. If your bill jumped $40 and nothing else changed, check your cover before you call a technician.

Repair or remove? Run the numbers before you call anybody

Honest repair pricing in this market, parts and labor:

  • Cover replacement: $350–$650
  • Heater element: $200–$450
  • Circulation pump: $400–$900
  • Control board: $500–$1,100
  • Shell crack repair: $300–$800, and it rarely holds past a few years
  • Full foam leak hunt: $400–$1,200, because somebody has to cut insulation out to find it

A new mid-tier tub runs $6,000–$12,000 installed; premium models go to $16,000 and up.

> If repair quotes total more than 40% of a replacement tub on a unit older than 12 years, you're financing someone else's problem. That's the line we'd draw if it were our own backyard.

Removal and disposal of a standard 6–8 person tub runs $350–$650 around Pueblo West, including cutting it down, hauling it out, and tipping fees. A swim spa or an 8x8 unit buried inside a custom deck runs higher. That's the actual comparison: $650 to make it disappear versus $900 for a pump on a 14-year-old shell that's going to need a $500 cover next spring.

When removal is NOT worth it — and who should skip us entirely

This is the part that costs us jobs, and we'd rather say it than not.

Don't pay for removal if your tub works. A functional 6-year-old tub with a good cover is worth $800–$2,500 used in this market. List it. Make the buyer handle the move. We have watched people pay us $500 to haul away a tub that would have sold in a week.

Don't pay for removal if the only problem is the cover. A $450 cover fixes "it costs too much to run," "it won't hold temperature," and "the water's always cloudy" more often than any other single part. Replace the cover first. If the tub is still broken in two months, call us then.

Skip us if you own a truck, a reciprocating saw, and a free Saturday. A dry hot tub shell weighs 500–900 lbs, but a sawzall with a demolition blade cuts an acrylic-and-foam shell into four manageable pieces in 90 minutes to 3 hours. Add $60–$120 in tipping fees at the county landfill and a $40 trailer rental, and a confident DIYer does this for under $250. We are selling labor and disposal access, not magic. If you have both, keep your money.

Where DIY stops making sense: if the tub is above grade on a second-story deck, framed inside a permanent gazebo, or in a fenced backyard with a gate narrower than 36 inches, the job stops being a saw-and-haul and becomes a disassembly problem. Same story if there's a concrete pad underneath that also needs to go — that's a different job with different equipment, and we'd point you toward [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) rather than pretending a pickup handles it.

What we won't do: we don't disconnect electrical. A hot tub is on a 50–60 amp 240V GFCI circuit, and that breaker needs to be off and the whirlpool disconnect handled before anyone touches the cabinet. We ask you to have it killed at the panel before we arrive. If you're not sure, an electrician charges $100–$200 for the visit and it is not optional.

What we can't promise: almost none of a hot tub gets recycled. The acrylic shell, the polyurethane foam, and the ABS base are landfill-bound in practice. We pull the steel frame, the pumps, and copper where it's accessible — typically 40–80 lbs of recoverable metal out of a 700 lb tub. Anybody telling you a hot tub is 90% recyclable is selling you something.

Draining takes longer than you think

If you're removing a tub yourself or prepping for a crew, budget real time. A 6-person tub holds 350 to 500 gallons. The built-in drain valve moves it at roughly 2–5 gallons per minute, which is 2 to 4 hours. A $70 submersible pump cuts that to 30–45 minutes.

Dump the water on landscaping only if the chlorine or bromine is fully dissipated — stop sanitizing 3–4 days ahead and test to under 0.5 ppm. Pueblo West lots with xeriscaping and gravel handle 400 gallons better than a lawn does; don't put it all in one spot, and keep it away from your foundation and your neighbor's fence line.

Let the shell sit open 24 hours after draining. A "drained" tub still holds 20–40 gallons in the plumbing lines and footwell, and that water finds its way onto your patio the moment the shell gets tipped.

The honest summary

For soaking: 15–20 minutes at 102–104°F, 30–45 at 100°F, half that for kids and anyone pregnant or on cardiac medication — and tighten all of it because of our altitude and dry air.

For the tub itself: 15–20 years if it was built well, 7–10 if it wasn't, with covers and heater elements as the usual first casualties.
