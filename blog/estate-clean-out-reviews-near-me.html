How to Actually Read Estate Cleanout Reviews (Most People Read Them Wrong)

A 4.9-star average across 200 reviews tells you almost nothing about estate work, because 90% of a junk removal company's reviews come from single-item pickups — a couch, a mattress, a hot tub. Those jobs are 45 minutes long. An estate cleanout is 14 to 30 crew-hours with the family's grief sitting in the next room. Different skill entirely.

Filter for these instead:

  • Reviews that mention a specific date and a specific price. "They quoted $1,250 and charged $1,250" is worth 50 vague five-stars. Estate work is where quotes drift — a review that confirms the number held is the single strongest signal.
  • Reviews from out-of-state family members. Search the text for "flew in," "from Denver," "from Texas," "my brother handled it." Roughly 1 in 3 Pueblo West estate calls comes from an heir who lives elsewhere. If a company handles remote clients well, those reviews say so explicitly, usually mentioning photos or video.
  • Reviews that mention something being *found*. Cash in a coat pocket, a coin collection, a title, savings bonds, a firearm. If nobody in 200 reviews ever mentions the crew flagging something valuable, either the crew isn't looking or the customers never found out.
  • The 3-star reviews specifically. Skip the 1-stars (often scheduling flukes) and the 5-stars (often the easy jobs). The 3-stars tell you what happens when a job goes sideways.
  • Response time to negative reviews. A company that answers a bad review in under 48 hours with a specific factual reply is a company that keeps records.

> The most useful estate cleanout review isn't the one that says "great job." It's the one that says "they told me the china cabinet wasn't worth what I thought it was."

When an Estate Cleanout Service Is a Bad Purchase

This is the section most companies won't write. Here are the cases where you should keep your money.

1. The estate has real resale value and you're in a hurry. If the deceased collected tools, firearms, coins, vintage furniture, or farm equipment, a junk removal crew is the wrong first call. We are paid to make things disappear, not to appraise them. A $400 Snap-on tool chest and a $40 Harbor Freight one look identical on a trailer at 4pm. Get an estate sale company or an auctioneer in first — they typically take 30–40% commission but can net a family $3,000–$12,000 on a well-stocked Pueblo West garage and shop. Call us *after* the sale, for the 20% nobody bought. That's a $500–$800 job instead of a $2,000 one, and you're thousands ahead.

2. Probate isn't settled and you're not the personal representative. If the property is in probate and you have not been appointed PR by the Pueblo County District Court, do not have anything hauled. We've had two jobs in the last three years where a sibling authorized a cleanout and another sibling had a legitimate claim to the contents. That is a legal problem we cannot fix and neither can you once the trailer is emptied. Wait for the Letters Testamentary. In Colorado that's typically 2 to 6 weeks after filing. We'd rather lose the booking than be a deposition exhibit.

3. You have time, a truck, and fewer than ~15 yards of stuff. A $70 U-Haul pickup rental for 4 hours plus $110 in tipping fees clears a small apartment or a single bedroom for under $200. If you're physically able, have a helper, and aren't emotionally wrecked, DIY genuinely wins below about one trailer load. Above two loads the math flips hard — you'll spend 3 weekends and a strained back to save $600.

4. The property is a hoarding situation with biohazard. Standard estate cleanout crews are not remediation contractors. If there's animal waste saturating subfloor, rodent infestation, mold past 10 square feet, or a death that wasn't discovered for days, you need a licensed biohazard remediation firm, not us. We'll walk it and tell you straight. Sometimes we do the non-contaminated 70% and a remediation company does the rest. See [hoarding cleanout](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) for where that line sits.

5. You're hoping the donation value offsets the cost. It generally won't. Realistically 10–20% of an average estate's contents are donatable — the rest is worn upholstery, pressboard furniture, and 30-year-old electronics. A typical donation receipt from a Pueblo West estate is worth $300–$900 in deductible value, which at a 22% federal bracket is $66–$198 of actual tax savings. Nice, not decisive. And you only benefit if you itemize, which about 10% of filers now do post-2017.

The Failure Modes — What Actually Goes Wrong

  • The quote-to-invoice gap. The single most common estate complaint industry-wide. It happens because the walk-through missed a crawlspace, an attic, or a shed. Fix: insist the estimator physically opens every outbuilding and gets in the attic. A quote given over the phone from photos is wrong 40% of the time on estates — not because anyone is lying, but because families genuinely don't know what's in the back of the shed.
  • Something meaningful gets thrown out. Photo albums, military discharge papers, a wedding ring in a pill bottle. This is the failure that actually hurts. Fix: do one pass yourself — every drawer, every coat pocket, under every mattress, inside every book. Budget 4–6 hours for a 3-bedroom house. Nobody can do this for you, and no crew, ours included, should be trusted to.
  • Scheduling slip. Estate jobs get bumped when the crew ahead of you finds a shed. Ask what happens if your date moves. Our policy: if we move you, the quoted price drops 10%. Most companies have no policy at all, which is the tell.
  • "Free estimate" that isn't free. Some outfits charge a $75–$150 trip fee waived only if you book. Ask directly.
  • Dumping. If a price is dramatically below the numbers above, ask where the load goes and ask for the scale ticket. Illegal dumping on the mesa and along the Arkansas is a real Pueblo County problem, and the fine follows the property owner sometimes, not just the hauler.

What to Ask Before You Book

Five questions, and how a good answer sounds:

  • "Are you insured, and can you email the certificate today?" Good answer: a COI arrives in under an hour showing at least $1,000,000 general liability. Estates mean carrying heavy things down stairs in a house you don't own.
  • "Do you do the walk-through yourself or send a salesperson?" On estate work, the person quoting should be on the truck. Quotes from someone who won't do the labor drift high.
  • "What's your donation partner and will I get a receipt?" A specific name, not "we donate what we can."
  • "How do you handle anything of apparent value?" The right answer is a stop-and-call policy, in writing.
  • "What's the price if it's 30% more than we thought?" Get the per-yard overage rate up front. Ours is $45/yard beyond the quoted volume.

When It Clearly *Is* Worth Paying For

Three profiles where hiring out is obviously correct:

  • You live more than 200 miles away. Two round trips from Denver is ~$180 in fuel, two hotel nights at $120–$160, and 3–4 PTO days. That's $900+ before you've lifted a couch. Hiring out is cheaper and finishes in 2 days.
  • There's a closing date or a listing date. A Realtor needs the house empty and swept to photograph. Missing a listing window in a market where Pueblo West homes average 45–70 days on market costs more than the cleanout.
  • You're over 60, or grieving, or both. This is the real one. The physical work is genuinely hard — a full-size console TV is 180 lbs, a sleeper sofa is 250 lbs — and doing it while sorting through a parent's belongings breaks people down. There's no medal for doing it yourself.
