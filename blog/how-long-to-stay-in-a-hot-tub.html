HOW LONG TO STAY IN A HOT TUB

Fifteen to twenty minutes is the right soak at 102–104°F, and 30 to 45 minutes is fine if you drop the water to 98–100°F. That's the whole answer for a healthy adult. Cut it to 10 minutes or less if you're pregnant, over 65, on blood pressure medication, or you've had anything to drink. Kids under 5 shouldn't be in a 104°F tub at all — and under 12, cap it at 5 minutes with the thermostat at 98°F. If you live in Pueblo West, subtract about 5 minutes from whatever number you'd use at sea level, because our elevation and 20% humidity dehydrate you faster than the manual assumes. Get out, cool down for 10 minutes, and you can go back in.

We haul hot tubs out of Pueblo West garages, patios, and side yards most weeks of the year, and a surprising number of them died from being used wrong — not worn out. So here's the usage side, then the honest part about when the tub is finished.

Why Pueblo West Changes the Soak Math

Pueblo West sits at roughly 4,700–4,900 feet. Two things follow from that, and almost no hot tub article written for a national audience accounts for either.

First, dry air. Relative humidity here regularly runs 15–30% outside of a monsoon afternoon. Hot water plus dry air means evaporative water loss off your skin and lungs is far higher than it would be in Houston. You can lose a noticeable amount of fluid in a 20-minute soak, and you won't feel thirsty until you're already behind. Drink 12–16 oz of water before you get in and the same after. Don't substitute beer — alcohol dilates your blood vessels at the same time the hot water does, which is exactly how people end up on the deck seeing spots.

Second, sun. Pueblo County averages around 245 sunny days a year, and UV intensity climbs roughly 4–5% per 1,000 feet of elevation. A 2 p.m. July soak with no shade is a heat-stress event plus a sunburn, not relaxation. Soak at 7 a.m. or after sundown in summer. In July, when the ambient air is 95°F+, drop the tub to 96–98°F — a 104°F tub in 95°F air gives your body nowhere to dump heat, and 20 minutes becomes genuinely unsafe.

A Soak Schedule You Can Actually Use

  • 104°F (the CPSC maximum for residential spas): 10–15 minutes, once. This is the ceiling, not the target. No kids, no pregnancy, no alcohol.
  • 102°F: 15–20 minutes. The sweet spot for most adults, most of the year.
  • 100°F: 30 minutes comfortably. Good for sore backs and long conversations.
  • 96–98°F: 45+ minutes. The right setting for summer, for kids, and for anyone with a cardiac history who's been cleared by a doctor.
  • Winter, 20°F outside: 20 minutes at 102°F is plenty, and keep a dry towel within arm's reach. The walk back inside is where people slip on ice, not where they overheat.

Set a phone timer. "I'll just feel when it's time" is unreliable, because the early stage of overheating feels pleasant and slightly dreamy — which is the problem.

> The 20-minute rule isn't arbitrary caution. Your body sheds heat by sweating into air. Submerged in 102°F water, sweating does nothing, so your core temperature climbs with no brake on it. Roughly every 10 minutes at 104°F adds about a degree to your core — and at 102.2°F core, judgment and balance start going before you notice they're gone.

Who Should Soak Less — Or Skip It

This is the part people skim. Don't.

  • Pregnancy. Raising core temperature above about 102°F, especially in the first trimester, is a documented risk. If your OB clears it at all, it's usually under 10 minutes below 100°F. Many say skip it entirely.
  • Heart disease, uncontrolled high or low blood pressure, recent cardiac event. Hot water drops blood pressure hard. Talk to your cardiologist, not to the internet.
  • Diabetic neuropathy. If you can't reliably feel temperature in your feet, you can't tell when the water is scalding you. Check the thermometer, not your toes.
  • Alcohol. The Consumer Product Safety Commission has tied alcohol to a large share of spa fatalities for decades. Two drinks and a 104°F tub is the single most dangerous combination in backyard recreation.
  • Soaking alone after dark. Overheating causes fainting. Fainting in 30 inches of water is a drowning. Have someone in the house know you're out there.

Get out immediately for: dizziness, nausea, racing or irregular heartbeat, tingling hands, a headache that arrives suddenly, or any confusion about how long you've been in. Sit for 10 minutes before standing fully.

The Maintenance Reality Nobody Mentions

How long you stay in affects how fast the water goes bad, which affects how long the tub lasts. Four adults soaking 20 minutes will burn through most of the free chlorine in a 350–450 gallon tub in one session. Targets: chlorine 1–3 ppm, bromine 3–5 ppm, pH 7.2–7.8. Drain and refill every 3–4 months, more often if you're hosting.

Pueblo West water is hard — scale builds on heater elements, and a scaled element fails early. A replacement heater runs $250–$500 installed; a circulation pump $400–$800. Neglect the chemistry for a season and you're often looking at both.

And the local killer: a power outage below 20°F. If a tub loses power for 12–24 hours in a January cold snap and nobody drains it, the plumbing can split in a dozen places inside the cabinet. That's not a repair — that's the moment a $7,000 tub becomes a 900-pound fiberglass object in your side yard. Half the [hot tub removals](/services/hot-tub-removal-pueblo) we do start with exactly that story.

When It's Not Worth It — And When You Shouldn't Pay Us

We'd rather tell you this than sell you a job you don't need.

Don't call us if the tub still works and you just stopped using it. A functioning 2015-or-newer tub has real resale value — $800 to $2,500 on local listings. Clean it, take honest photos, list it, and let the buyer handle the move. You come out $1,500 ahead of paying someone to haul it off.

Don't call us if the only problem is one dead component. A $300 repair on a tub with a sound shell beats a $350–$650 removal plus thousands for a replacement. Get a diagnostic first. If a tech says the shell is cracked or the foam is saturated, then it's over.

You can genuinely DIY this. A hot tub is fiberglass over foam over a plywood or composite frame. With a reciprocating saw, a few blades, eye protection, and a strong helper, a determined homeowner can cut a tub into quarters in 3–5 hours and make two or three trips to the transfer station. Disposal fees typically land in the $60–$150 range. You will spend a Saturday, you will be itchy from fiberglass dust, and you will need to deal with the electrical disconnect properly — but it's real money saved, and we won't pretend otherwise.

Where paying someone makes sense: the tub is on a second-story deck or behind a fence with a 32-inch gate; it's still full of 400 gallons of water; there's a concrete pad or pergola coming out with it; you're on a closing deadline; or you physically shouldn't be swinging a saw for four hours. Those are the jobs where a crew, a trailer, and sometimes a [skid steer](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo) earn the fee.

Our limitations, plainly: we don't do electrical work — a licensed electrician needs to kill and cap the 240V circuit before we touch it, and we'll tell you to schedule that first. We don't repair tubs. We don't buy them. If the pad underneath needs to come out too, that's [concrete removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) and a separate line on the quote. And if you're hoping someone will pay you for the tub as scrap: the shell has no scrap value. The motor and a few pounds of copper might offset $20–$40 of a haul. That's it.

The Bottom Line

Twenty minutes at 102°F, water in hand, timer on, not alone, not after drinking — and in a Pueblo West summer, cooler water and earlier hours. Treat the chemistry like it matters and drain it in a hard freeze, and a decent tub gives you 12–18 years.
