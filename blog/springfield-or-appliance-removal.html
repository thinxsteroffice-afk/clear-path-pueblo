What Appliance Removal Actually Costs in Pueblo West

These are typical ranges for the Pueblo and Pueblo West market. Your final price depends on the item, how hard it is to reach and whether we're picking up anything else on the same trip.

  • Single standard appliance (washer, dryer, electric range, dishwasher): usually $75–$150 if it's on the ground floor with a clear path out.
  • Refrigerator, freezer, window AC unit or dehumidifier: add about $25–$50 to cover refrigerant recovery.
  • Second-story or basement removal: expect $25–$75 more, depending on how many stairs there are and how tight the turns are.
  • Washer and dryer pair: often $125–$225 together, which is less than booking them one at a time.
  • Appliances added to a bigger cleanout: an appliance on a larger load is usually priced by how much truck space it takes, so the extra cost can be lower than a standalone pickup.

The single biggest factor is whether the appliance is the only thing on the truck. Most of what you pay for a one-item pickup covers the drive, fuel and two people's time, not the dump fee. If you have a dead chest freezer in the garage plus a broken treadmill and a stack of old tires, book everything as one trip. Why Refrigerators and Freezers Cost More

Under Section 608 of the Clean Air Act, refrigerant has to be recovered by a certified technician before a cooling appliance is scrapped. Older units can contain R-12 (Freon), which was phased out of new equipment in the U.S. around 1994. Newer units usually use R-134a or R-600a. In every case, the appliance has to be documented and handled before any metal recycler will accept it.

This affects you even if you haul the appliance yourself. Many scrap yards will turn away a fridge or freezer unless the refrigerant has already been removed and the unit is tagged. If you load a 250-pound side-by-side into your truck and drive it to a scrap yard, you may have to bring it back home.

> Most of what you pay for a fridge pickup isn't for carrying it. It covers the paperwork and refrigerant handling that let a recycler legally accept it.

How a Pickup Works

The process is simple, but a few details can save you an hour of waiting or a second trip.

1. Disconnect before we arrive, or tell us you haven't. We can unplug an electric appliance and cap a washer's water supply valves. We do not disconnect gas lines. If you're getting rid of a gas range or dryer, have a licensed plumber or your gas utility shut off and cap the line first. Expect that to cost roughly $75–$150, and plan it at least 2–3 days before your pickup. 2. Defrost freezers 24 hours ahead. A freezer that's still frozen leaks water across your floor and into our truck. Unplug it a full day early with a towel underneath. 3. Empty everything. We can't take food waste with an appliance, and a fridge still full of food can add 40–60 pounds of weight plus smell. 4. Measure the tightest doorway. Most modern refrigerators are 30–36 inches wide. If a fridge was installed before a remodel narrowed a hallway, the doors and hinges may have to come off. Tell us ahead of time so we bring the right tools. 5. Same-day or next-day service is common in Pueblo West, but we can't promise it. Weekends and the end of each month fill up first because that's when moves and rental turnovers happen.

Where Your Old Appliance Ends Up

Appliances are mostly steel, so they're among the most recyclable things in a typical household load. A standard washer weighs about 150–200 pounds, and most of that is recoverable metal. After the refrigerant is handled, cooling appliances are recycled for their steel, copper tubing and compressors.

Appliances that still work are a different case. A working fridge or washer that's under about 10 years old often has resale or donation value. Where we can, we route working units to resale or donation instead of scrap. This is also why we ask whether an item works when you book.

When Appliance Removal Isn't Worth Paying For

This section will cost us some bookings. Paying a hauler is the wrong choice in these situations.

  • You're buying a new appliance anyway. Big-box stores and local appliance dealers usually offer haul-away of the old unit when they deliver the new one, often for $0–$40. That's almost always cheaper than booking us separately, so check the checkout options before you call anyone. The main catches are that the old unit usually has to be disconnected and ready, and some retailers only take the same type of appliance they're delivering.
  • You have a working refrigerator or freezer, and the utility will pay you. Xcel Energy has run appliance recycling programs in Colorado that pay customers (historically around $50) to pick up a working second fridge or freezer. Terms, eligibility and whether the program is running change from year to year, so check Xcel's current offer first. If they'll pick it up and pay you, that beats paying us.
  • You have a truck, a dolly and a non-refrigerant appliance. A dead electric dryer or old water heater is just scrap metal. A Pueblo-area scrap yard will usually take it and may pay you a few dollars, since steel scrap typically pays only a few cents per pound. A 180-pound washer won't make you any real money, but you won't pay anyone either. If you can lift it safely and the trip takes 30 minutes, do it yourself.
  • The appliance works and you aren't in a hurry. A working washer or dryer listed locally for $50–$150 often sells within a week in Pueblo West, and the buyer hauls it away. Donation is another option, and many charities will pick up working appliances for free. Our fee buys you speed and convenience. If you don't need either, you're paying for nothing.
  • You live in Springfield, Oregon. We're not driving 1,200 miles. Search for a hauler based in Lane County, and check whether the county's own transfer sites accept appliances directly. That's often the cheapest route anywhere.

Some failure modes are worth knowing about too, whoever you hire:

  • Gas appliances that nobody disconnected. If the line isn't capped when the crew shows up, the job can't go ahead, and some companies charge a trip fee for that. Get the plumber in first.
  • Built-in units. A built-in fridge, wall oven or trash compactor may need cabinet or trim work to get out. Haulers carry appliances out; they don't do carpentry. If removal means cutting countertops, schedule a contractor first.
  • Quotes given over the phone with no photo. A firm price based only on the words "a fridge" can change on site once the crew sees it's in a basement down a narrow staircase. Send a photo of the item and the path out, and you'll get a quote that holds.

Is It Worth Keeping an Old Fridge Running?

Before you pay to haul away a working second fridge, consider what it costs to keep it. A refrigerator from the early 2000s can use twice the electricity or more of a current ENERGY STAR model, often 700–1,000+ kWh per year compared with roughly 350–450 kWh for a new one. At Colorado residential rates, an old garage fridge can cost $75–$130 a year in electricity just to hold a few cases of drinks. Garage fridges also work harder in Pueblo West's summer heat, when garage temperatures can go well above 90°F.

If that's the fridge you're thinking about, getting rid of it can pay for itself within about 12–18 months, especially if a utility program takes it for free or pays you.
