IS JUNK REMOVAL REALLY PROFITABLE?

Yes, a junk removal business can be profitable, but mostly for the owner who hauls the loads. A well-run one-truck operation in southern Colorado typically keeps 10% to 25% of revenue as net profit once the truck, insurance, dump fees, labor, and slow months are paid for. Gross margin on a single job often looks like 50% to 60%, which is why the business seems like easy money from the outside. The gap between those two numbers is where most new haulers fail. Below are the numbers that decide which side of that line a business lands on. If you're a Pueblo West homeowner wondering why a quote is priced the way it is, this also shows where your money goes.

What One Job Actually Costs to Run

Here's a realistic breakdown for a full 16-foot box truck load (about 15 cubic yards) hauled from a Pueblo West home. The numbers are illustrative ranges for our market, not a promise about any specific job.

  • Customer price: $500 to $700 for a full load in the Pueblo area, depending on weight and what's in it.
  • Disposal fees: $60 to $150. Landfills charge by weight, so a load of couch cushions and a load of broken concrete of the same size have very different dump bills.
  • Fuel: $25 to $45. Pueblo West is spread out, and the round trip from a home near Lake Pueblo to a disposal site and back adds real miles.
  • Labor: A two-person crew for 2 to 3 hours at $18 to $22 an hour, plus payroll taxes, comes to about $90 to $160.
  • Special-handling items: $0 to $75 or more. Refrigerators and freezers need their refrigerant recovered by an EPA Section 608 certified technician. Colorado has banned TVs and computer monitors from landfills since 2013, so those go to paid e-waste recycling.

On a $600 job, that leaves about $250 to $400 before overhead. That's the 50% to 60% gross margin people quote online, and it's accurate for that one job. It's also misleading.

The Overhead That Eats the Margin

Gross margin pays for the business before it pays the owner. A lean one-truck operation still carries fixed costs every month:

  • The truck: A used 16-foot box truck or dump trailer setup costs $25,000 to $50,000. Financed over 5 years, that's roughly $500 to $1,000 a month before repairs.
  • Insurance: Commercial auto plus general liability for a small hauler commonly runs $4,000 to $9,000 a year. Workers' comp is on top of that once you hire.
  • Maintenance and tires: Budget at least $2,000 to $4,000 a year for a truck that makes 10 to 15 landfill runs a week on gravel roads and county dirt lots.
  • Marketing: Google Ads for junk removal terms can cost $15 to $40 or more per click. Getting one booked job from paid ads can easily cost $50 to $120.
  • Phone, software, licensing, and bookkeeping: $150 to $400 a month.

Put that together and a one-truck business needs somewhere around $3,000 to $5,000 in gross profit every month just to break even. At about $300 gross profit per average job, that's 10 to 17 jobs a month before the owner earns anything.

> The truck doesn't care whether it's a busy week. The payment, the insurance, and the registration come due in January the same as in June.

Why Pueblo West Is a Different Market

Most online articles about junk removal profitability assume a dense suburb where a crew can do six small pickups within 3 miles of each other. Pueblo West doesn't work that way, and it changes the math in both directions.

What helps: Lots here are big. Many are half an acre to several acres. That means more sheds, old hot tubs, rotting decks, fencing, trailers, and accumulated yard debris than a typical subdivision has. Jobs tend to be bigger. A single [shed demolition and removal](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) or [hot tub removal](/services/hot-tub-removal-pueblo) can bring in more than three couch pickups in a city, and it takes one drive instead of three.

What hurts:

  • Distance. Homes stretch from the Highway 50 corridor out toward the lake and the far western lots. More drive time between jobs means fewer billable hours per day.
  • Weight. Local jobs involve a lot of dirt, rock, concrete, and landscaping material. Heavy loads fill the landfill scale long before they fill the truck, so disposal costs rise sharply. It's one reason [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) is priced differently from household junk.
  • Seasonality. Demand from roughly April through October can be 2 to 3 times the demand in December through February. A business that sizes its crew for summer loses money all winter. A business that sizes for winter turns away work in May.
  • Wind and weather. Spring wind gusts over 40 mph are common here, and that affects roof-level work, tarping, and trailer hauling. Lost days are lost revenue.

When Hiring Junk Removal Isn't Worth It

This section applies to our own customers. Paying a hauler isn't always the smart move, and it's better to know that before you call.

You probably shouldn't hire us if:

  • It's one or two small items your trash service will take. Many residential haulers offer bulk-item pickup a few times a year for free or for a small fee. Check with your provider before paying $100 or more for a single chair.
  • You own a pickup truck and it's clean, light material. A load of brush or bagged leaves you can lift yourself costs you a trip to the landfill and a gate fee. If you have the time and your back is fine, doing it yourself is cheaper.
  • You're doing a multi-week remodel. A roll-off dumpster you fill slowly over 2 to 4 weeks often beats paying for several separate haul-away visits. We'll tell you that if we see it.
  • The item still has resale value. A working fridge less than 5 years old, a solid wood dresser, or a riding mower in running condition can often sell on Facebook Marketplace within a week. Selling it beats paying to haul it.
  • It's hazardous waste. Paint thinners, pesticides, motor oil in bulk, pool chemicals, and asbestos-containing materials aren't standard junk removal items. Most haulers, including us, can't legally put them in a truck headed to a regular landfill. Look for your county's household hazardous waste program instead.

And here's who should NOT start a junk removal business (the other half of "is it profitable"):

  • Anyone without $15,000 to $30,000 in reserves beyond the truck. The first 6 to 12 months usually don't cover the owner's living expenses, and one blown transmission can end the business.
  • Anyone counting on a franchise to guarantee income. National franchise buy-ins often pass $100,000 all in once the fee, trucks, and startup marketing are counted, and royalties commonly take 6% to 10% of revenue for good. In a market the size of Pueblo West (about 33,000 residents), that's a lot of overhead to recover.
  • Anyone physically unable to lift 100+ pounds repeatedly. Back, knee, and shoulder injuries are the most common way owner-operators get knocked out of the business. If the owner can't work, the one-truck business stops making money.
  • Anyone who hates the phone. Most booked jobs come from a fast response. Leads that wait 2 hours for a callback go to whoever answered first.

The Failure Modes Nobody Advertises

When junk removal businesses fail, it's usually for the same handful of reasons:

1. Underpricing to win jobs. New haulers quote $200 for a job that costs $180 to do and call it a win. After five of those, the truck payment bounces. 2. Ignoring dump weight. Pricing by volume alone while hauling heavy material turns a profitable load into a loss at the scale house. 3. One-truck dependency. If the only truck is in the shop for 10 days in peak season, that can wipe out 15% to 20% of the month's revenue. 4. No winter plan. The businesses that last usually add related work for the slow months, like [estate cleanouts](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo), [foreclosure and eviction cleanouts](/services/foreclosure-eviction-cleanout-pueblo) for property managers, and [skid steer work](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo). That kind of work doesn't depend on homeowners spring cleaning. 5. Treating the owner's labor as free. If a business nets $40,000 but the owner worked 60 hours a week to get it, that's under $13 an hour. That's profitable on paper, but it isn't a good job.

What Profitable Looks Like in Practice

A realistic, sustainable one-truck operation in the Pueblo area might look like this:

  • Revenue: $150,000 to $250,000 a year, which works out to about 8 to 15 jobs a week with an average ticket of $300 to $450.
  • Net profit: 10% to 25%, or roughly $15,000 to $60,000, plus whatever the owner pays themselves as a wage.
  • Break-even timeline: 12 to 24 months to pay back the startup costs if nothing major breaks.

That's a real small business, not a get-rich scheme. The operators who do well price by weight and volume honestly, pick up work that doesn't depend on the season, and keep a second vehicle or a trailer so one breakdown doesn't shut them down.
