10-STEP HOARDING CLEANUP PLAN

Here's the 10-step hoarding cleanup plan in order:

1. Get the resident's consent. 2. Rate the severity. 3. Check for hazards. 4. Set one written goal. 5. Pick the first room by function, not by how bad it looks. 6. Set up four sorting zones. 7. Clear in timed blocks. 8. Send things out the same day. 9. Deep clean and repair. 10. Make a plan to keep it clear.

For an average Pueblo West home at a moderate level, steps 1–4 take about a week. The physical work in steps 5–9 usually takes 2–5 working days with a crew, or 3–8 weekends doing it yourself. Step 10 lasts months. The rest of this guide explains each step, what it costs, and when hiring a crew like ours is the wrong choice.

Step 1: Get Consent Before You Touch Anything

This step decides whether the cleanup lasts. Hoarding disorder affects an estimated 2–6% of adults. It's a recognized mental health condition, not laziness. When family members clear a house without the person's agreement, the result is often a damaged relationship, and the house can fill back up within 6–12 months.

A real agreement means the resident:

  • Agrees on which rooms are being cleared
  • Agrees on the categories that will be discarded without a check (food, trash, broken items)
  • Names the categories they want to review personally (papers, photos, clothing)
  • Picks a signal that means "stop, I need a break"

If you can't get this agreement, stop here. Read the section below on when this isn't worth it.

Step 2: Rate the Severity

Two standard tools help you judge the job instead of guessing:

  • The Clutter Image Rating, a 9-point photo scale. You compare each room to reference photos. A 1–3 is cluttered but livable. A 4–6 is a problem. A 7–9 means rooms can't be used for their purpose.
  • The ICD Clutter-Hoarding Scale, from the Institute for Challenging Disorganization. It has 5 levels. It also covers sanitation, pests, and structural issues, not just how much stuff there is.

The level points you to the right approach. Levels 1–2 are usually a DIY job. Level 3 is borderline. Levels 4–5 nearly always need professionals, and sometimes more than one kind.

Step 3: Do a Hazard Walk-Through

Walk the home once with a flashlight, gloves, and an N95 mask before anyone starts moving piles. Look for:

  • Rodent droppings and nests. This is the big one in Pueblo West. Colorado consistently ranks among the states with the most reported hantavirus cases, and deer mice are common on the large, semi-rural lots here. Outbuildings, sheds, and garages that have been closed up for years are the worst spots. Don't sweep or vacuum droppings. Following CDC guidance, spray them with a 1:10 bleach-to-water solution, let it soak for 5 minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels.
  • Blocked exits and electrical panels. Clear a path to at least one exit and the breaker box first.
  • Floor loading. Stacks of paper weigh roughly 35–40 pounds per cubic foot, and some homes hold thousands of pounds of it. Watch for sagging floors.
  • Biohazards. Human or animal waste, needles, or signs of decomposition. If you find any of these, the job is outside both DIY and standard junk removal. See below.

Step 4: Set One Written Goal

"Clean the house" is not a goal. Good goals look like these:

  • "The kitchen works and the stove is usable by November 1."
  • "Clear paths to every exit, at least 36 inches wide."
  • "The home passes the landlord's inspection on the 15th."

Write the goal down. When someone stalls over a box of old magazines on day three, the goal is what settles it.

Step 5: Choose the First Room by Function, Not Appearance

Most people start with the worst room, which is a mistake. Start with the room that brings back the most daily function for the least effort. That's usually the bathroom or kitchen, since those rooms affect health, hygiene, and whether the person can live there safely. A quick visible win early on helps keep the resident willing to continue. A hard first room often ends the project.

Leave garages and outbuildings until near the end, because those are the most common places for long-term storage on Pueblo West lots. They often need their own approach. Our [garage cleanout service](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) covers that, and so does [shed demolition and removal](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) if the structure itself is beyond saving.

Step 6: Set Up Four Sorting Zones

Set up exactly four zones, clearly labeled:

1. Keep: goes back into the home, but only into a specific assigned spot 2. Donate: bagged and out of the house within 48 hours 3. Recycle/special disposal: electronics, paint, batteries, tires, propane tanks 4. Trash: goes directly into the truck or dumpster

Don't add a "maybe" pile. "Maybe" turns into "keep" in nearly every case.

In Pueblo West, the wind matters here. Afternoon gusts can scatter an outdoor staging area quickly, so sort in the garage or under a weighted tarp, not on the open driveway.

A Colorado rule to know: under a state law in effect since July 1, 2013, most consumer electronics, including TVs, computers, and monitors, can't go to Colorado landfills. Budget a separate trip or a recycling fee for them.

Step 7: Clear in Timed Blocks

Work in blocks of 90–120 minutes, then take a 15-minute break. Decision fatigue is real, and the resident's ability to make choices drops sharply after about 3–4 hours. Expect a resident who is personally reviewing items to get through roughly one box of mixed papers per 20–30 minutes. That's why paperwork-heavy homes take the longest.

To estimate volume, measure. A 10×10-foot room filled to 4 feet deep holds about 400 cubic feet, or roughly 15 cubic yards. That's about one full load for a typical junk removal truck. A moderate three-bedroom home commonly produces 4–10 truckloads.

Step 8: Send Things Out the Same Day

Anything sorted into donate, recycle, or trash should leave the property the same day, or within 48 hours at most. Bags left in the driveway get re-opened and pulled back in.

Your options:

  • Rent a roll-off dumpster. These often run $350–$700 per week in southern Colorado, depending on size and weight limits. You do all the lifting, and overweight charges add up fast with paper and wet materials.
  • Make trailer runs yourself. This is cheapest if you have a trailer and several free weekends.
  • Hire a crew. Typical full-home hoarding cleanouts in this region run about $1,500–$6,000+, depending on volume, access, and hazards. Lighter jobs come in under that. Severe Level 5 homes with biohazards can go well past it. Always get a written, on-site quote. You can see how we handle it on our [hoarding cleanout page](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo).

Step 9: Deep Clean and Repair

Once a room is clear, you'll usually find hidden damage: stained subfloor, chewed wiring, plumbing leaks that went unnoticed, and broken windows. Budget 10–20% of your cleanout cost for cleaning and minor repairs. Get an electrician to check any wiring that rodents have reached before turning circuits back on.

Step 10: Plan for After

Without follow-up, many cleared homes fill back up. The treatment with the strongest evidence for hoarding is cognitive behavioral therapy built specifically for hoarding. Standard treatment programs commonly run about 20–26 sessions. Other practical steps:

  • Schedule a monthly 30-minute walk-through for the first 6 months
  • Cancel catalog mail and recurring subscriptions
  • Set a "one in, one out" rule for the keep categories
  • Take dated photos of each cleared room so progress is visible

> A clean house without a maintenance plan is a temporary result, not a finished one.

When This Isn't Worth It, and When You Shouldn't Hire Us

These are the cases where a junk removal crew is the wrong purchase, or at least the wrong first one.

The resident hasn't agreed. If an adult child is hiring us to clear a parent's home while the parent objects, we will likely say no or pause the job. Forced cleanouts are the most common way these projects fail. Money spent clearing a home that fills back up within a year is mostly wasted, and it can permanently damage the relationship.

The home contains biohazards. Human waste, large amounts of animal waste, blood, or an unattended death need a licensed biohazard remediation company, not junk haulers. We aren't the right company for that part, and anyone who says they can do it on a standard junk removal truck is taking risks with your health. Remediation often costs $1,000–$3,000 per day on top of removal.

It's really a Level 1 or 2. If you can walk through every room and the kitchen works, you probably don't need us. A dumpster rental and two or three committed weekends will cost a fraction of a crew. Our [FAQ](/faq) covers how to tell the difference.

There are valuable items mixed in. If the home has collectibles, firearms, coins, tools, or antiques, bring in an appraiser or estate sale company *first*. Crews move fast, and speed works against careful sorting. For estates, our [estate cleanout service](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) should come after the sale, not before.

The structure isn't safe. If floors are sagging, the roof is failing, or there's fire damage, get a structural inspection before anyone carries weight through the house.

You're expecting one day. A crew can physically empty a moderate home in 1–2 days, but only if decisions are already made. If the resident needs to review every item, crew time turns into waiting time, and you pay for it.

The Short Version

Get consent first, check for hazards second, and save the heavy lifting for the middle steps. Move trash, donations, and recycling out the same day they're sorted, and start the follow-up plan before the last truck pulls away.
