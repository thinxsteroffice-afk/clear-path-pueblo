HOW MUCH DOES JUNK REMOVAL MAKE?

A one-truck junk removal operation in a market the size of Pueblo West typically grosses $90,000 to $180,000 a year, and the owner takes home $35,000 to $70,000 of that after fuel, dump fees, insurance, and the truck payment. Average job here runs $250 to $450. A good full day is $800 to $1,400 in revenue across 3–5 jobs; a slow February day is $0. Multi-truck operations in Pueblo and Colorado Springs clear $400,000 to $1.2M gross, but that's 3–6 trucks, payroll, and a real ad budget. The gap between "revenue" and "money in your pocket" is where most new haulers get hurt, and it's wider in a low-density market like ours than the national averages suggest.

We run ClearPath here. Below are the actual mechanics of the money in this specific market — not franchise brochure numbers from Denver or Dallas.

The Per-Job Math Nobody Publishes

Junk removal is priced by volume, not weight or time. A standard dump-body truck holds roughly 12 to 16 cubic yards. Pricing typically ladders like this:

  • Minimum / single item (one mattress, one recliner): $95–$160
  • 1/4 truck: $180–$260
  • 1/2 truck: $300–$420
  • 3/4 truck: $420–$540
  • Full truck: $550–$750

So the ceiling on a single truckload is around $700. That number matters more than anything else in this business, because it's fixed by physics. You cannot earn $2,000 in one trip no matter how good your sales pitch is. You earn more by cycling the truck faster or by running more trucks.

Here's the part that gets glossed over: a full load isn't a full day. Load time on a typical [garage cleanout in Pueblo West](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) is 60–90 minutes with two people. Drive to the transfer station and back from most of Pueblo West is 20–35 minutes each way. Unload and pay is another 15–20. That's a 2.5 to 3.5 hour cycle for one $550 load. Three cycles is a genuinely hard 10-hour day, and that assumes zero windshield time between customers.

Why Pueblo West Specifically Changes the Numbers

National junk removal income guides assume dense suburbs where you can stack four jobs inside a 6-mile radius. Pueblo West is not that. The community covers roughly 50 square miles with about 35,000 residents, and a large share of lots are one acre or larger.

That cuts both ways, and you should understand both directions:

  • Bigger average tickets. Acre lots mean detached shops, horse property, old outbuildings, and decades of accumulated stuff. Our average ticket runs meaningfully higher than a comparable operator working apartment complexes.
  • Brutal drive time. A job on the far west side of the Pueblo West Metro District and the next one off McCulloch can be a 15-minute drive apart, plus the run into Pueblo to dump. Unbilled driving is the single largest hidden cost here — plan on 35–45% of your working hours being non-revenue windshield and dump time.
  • Seasonality is severe. Spring (March–June) yard cleanup and moving season can run 2–3x the volume of January and February. If you can't float a 60-day dry spell, you will not survive year two.
  • Wind and dry conditions matter. Loose loads have to be tarped properly or you're littering Highway 50. That's not a metaphor for anything; it's a real fine and a real reputation problem in a town where people talk.

What It Actually Costs to Run the Truck

Revenue is the fun number. Here is where it goes, per year, for a single-truck operation working roughly 200 revenue days:

  • Dump and transfer fees: $12,000–$25,000/yr. Budget $45–$90 per load for general waste depending on where you tip and how heavy it is. Call the Pueblo facility for current rates before you quote anything — they change, and appliances, tires, and mattresses carry separate surcharges.
  • Fuel: $6,000–$11,000/yr on a gas dump truck getting 8–10 MPG.
  • Commercial auto + general liability insurance: $4,500–$9,000/yr. Do not try to run this on a personal auto policy. One claim and you're finished.
  • Truck payment or depreciation: a used 2015-era dump-body truck runs $28,000–$55,000; new is $70,000+. Financed, that's $600–$1,100/mo.
  • Maintenance and tires: $3,000–$7,000/yr. Hydraulic dump systems and brakes on a loaded truck are not cheap.
  • Labor: a second set of hands at $18–$22/hr, plus payroll taxes, is $22–$27/hr loaded. Most jobs need two people. Some need three.
  • Marketing: $500–$2,500/mo if you want steady phone calls. Local service ads and Google Business Profile management are the realistic channels.

Run those numbers against $120,000 gross and you land somewhere near $45,000–$55,000 of owner income — for physical labor, on-call weekends, and full liability. That's a real job, not passive income.

There are offsets. Scrap metal at Pueblo-area yards can return $40–$150 per load depending on the mix, and appliance-heavy runs like an [appliance removal job](/services/appliance-removal-pueblo) sometimes cut the net dump cost close to zero. Specialty work pays better per hour than general junk: [hot tub removal](/services/hot-tub-removal-pueblo) prices at $350–$650 because most competitors won't touch the wiring and the cut-up. Same with [shed demolition](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) and [above ground pool removal](/services/above-ground-pool-removal-pueblo). If you build a business here, build it on the jobs the guy with a trailer can't do.

Who Should Not Start a Junk Removal Business in Pueblo West

This is the section that costs us nothing to be honest about, because we'd rather you not waste $40,000 finding out.

Don't start if you need income in the first 90 days. Realistic ramp in this market is 6–12 months to consistent bookings. First-year gross for most solo starts lands closer to $40,000–$70,000, not $120,000. Google Business Profile reviews compound slowly — you need 25–40 reviews before you show up reliably, and that's a year of work.

Don't start if your back is already a problem. You will lift a 260-lb console TV out of a basement. You will carry a waterlogged sleeper sofa up stairs. Workers' comp claims and self-inflicted disc injuries end more of these businesses than competition does.

Don't start if you're planning to compete on price. Pueblo West already has guys with a pickup and a 16-foot trailer charging $150 a load, cash, no insurance, no license. You cannot beat them on price and also carry $6,000 of insurance. You have to be worth more, which means same-day scheduling, real disposal (not dumping it in a gully off Siloam Road), and showing up when you said you would.

Don't start with a $70,000 new truck. The math doesn't support it at the volume this market produces in year one. A $25,000 used truck and a trailer will do the same work.

Don't assume the franchise numbers apply. National franchise systems quote $500k–$1M+ per territory, but those territories include dense metro population, and franchise fees plus royalties run 7–10% of gross on top of a $60,000–$100,000 initial investment. In a 35,000-person community, that model is very hard to make work.

A market this size supports maybe 3–5 full-time haulers. Pueblo and Pueblo West combined are around 150,000 people. That is not an infinite pie.

Of that $400, roughly $60–$90 goes to the transfer station, $50–$70 to labor for two people, $20–$30 to fuel, and the rest covers insurance, the truck, and the fact that we didn't book two other jobs during those three hours.

Where you save money is on the parts you can control:

  • Have everything in one staging area — a driveway pile loads in half the time of a scattered house
  • Separate metal and appliances; those may reduce your total
  • Book in the off-season if the timeline is flexible
  • Combine jobs — one truck, one trip, one minimum
