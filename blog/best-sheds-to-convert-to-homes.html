BEST SHEDS TO CONVERT TO HOMES

The best sheds to convert into homes are tall-wall "cabin" or "studio" style sheds, 12x24 feet or larger, with 8-foot sidewalls, walls framed at 16 inches on center, and pressure-treated floor joists. Barn-style (gambrel) sheds and anything with 6-foot sidewalls or less make poor homes. They look roomy on the lot, but once you add insulation, drywall and a finished floor, most of the space is too low to stand in. For Pueblo West, add two more requirements: a roof built for our high winds and snow, and a lot where water, sewer or septic, and power can reach the shed without a huge trenching bill. If you can't get those, you're better off building new.

What makes a shed worth converting

Most sheds are built to keep a lawn mower dry. A home has to pass inspection, stay warm at 5°F in January, and hold a person, furniture and appliances without the floor sagging. Before you look at brands or styles, check the shell against this list:

  • Sidewall height of 8 feet or more. The residential building code generally requires a 7-foot minimum ceiling in habitable rooms. By the time you add a subfloor, insulation and drywall, a 7-foot sidewall ends up at about 6'8" inside, and it will fail.
  • Walls framed at 16 inches on center, ideally 2x6. Many budget sheds use 2x4 studs at 24 inches on center. That leaves only 3.5 inches of cavity for insulation, about R-13 at best, and nothing sturdy to hang cabinets on.
  • Floor joists at 16 inches on center, ground-contact rated. A floor built at 24 inches on center for storage will bounce under a refrigerator and a bookshelf.
  • At least 70 square feet per habitable room. That's the code minimum. A 10x12 shed is 120 square feet total, so after a bathroom there isn't a legal bedroom left.
  • A real roof pitch and roof sheathing. Look for 7/16-inch OSB or better with actual roofing underlayment. Avoid thin metal panels screwed straight to purlins.
  • Door and window openings you can relocate. Egress rules mean any sleeping room needs an emergency escape opening. Very often the window has to go on a wall the manufacturer framed solid.

The shed styles that convert best (and worst)

Best: tall ranch / studio / "modern" single-slope sheds. These have high walls on at least one side and no wasted attic volume, so they're the easiest to finish. A single-slope roof also lets you mount solar panels facing south, which pays off with Pueblo West's roughly 300 sunny days a year.

Good: lofted cabin sheds (12x32 and larger). The porch and gable roof make them look like a small house, and the loft works for storage or a reading nook. Before you buy, check the sidewall height under the loft. Plenty of "cabins" have 6'6" walls, and the loft is usually too low to count as a legal sleeping area.

Workable: custom-built stick sheds. If a local framer built your shed on a real foundation with 2x6 walls, it may be the best candidate on the property. It's also the least common.

Worst: gambrel barns, metal kit sheds, and resin/plastic sheds. A gambrel's lower roof slope eats headroom along both long walls. Metal kit sheds have no framing to insulate or attach drywall to. Resin sheds can't be converted, period.

What a shed-to-home conversion costs in Pueblo West

These are realistic ranges for a Pueblo County project in 2026. Your numbers will depend on how far the shed sits from utilities and how much of the work you do yourself.

  • Shed shell: $8,000–$14,000 for a new 12x24 tall-wall shed, or $15,000–$30,000 for a 14x40 lofted cabin.
  • Foundation: $4,000–$12,000. Sheds sold on skids or gravel pads usually need a permanent foundation to be approved as a dwelling, and footings in our area generally have to go below the frost line (around 30 inches in Pueblo County; confirm with the Pueblo Regional Building Department).
  • Electrical: $3,000–$9,000, including a subpanel and a buried feed. Trenching alone often runs $15–$40 per linear foot, depending on how rocky the ground is.
  • Plumbing and sewer or septic tie-in: $8,000–$25,000 or more. This is the line item that sinks the most projects.
  • Insulation, drywall, flooring and finishes: $25–$60 per square foot for basic finishes.
  • Heating and cooling: $3,500–$7,000 for a ductless mini-split. That's the most practical option for a small space with our 90°F-plus summer afternoons and cold, windy nights.

Add it up and a livable 288-square-foot studio typically lands between $45,000 and $95,000, or about $155–$330 per square foot. That's cheaper than a conventional addition, but not by as much as the "$10,000 tiny home" videos suggest.

> A shed conversion saves money on the shell. The shell is usually less than 20% of the finished cost. Everything expensive is buried in the ground or hidden in the walls.

When a shed conversion isn't worth it

Some of the people reading this will lose money, and it's better to know that before you buy. Skip the conversion, or at least stop and get permit answers first, if any of these apply:

  • You bought the shed rent-to-own and haven't paid it off. Until the contract is done, the dealer owns it. Pouring a foundation, cutting the walls or wiring it can void the agreement, and you'd be putting $40,000 of finishes into a building you don't own.
  • The shed has sidewalls under 7 feet. Raising the roof on a finished shed often costs more than buying a new tall-wall shell. Don't try to make it work.
  • Your lot is on septic and you're adding a bedroom. In much of Pueblo West, adding a bedroom or a second dwelling can trigger a septic evaluation through the county health department. If the existing system is undersized, a replacement can cost $12,000–$25,000 before you've spent anything on the shed.
  • The shed is more than 150 feet from your water and sewer lines. Long trenches through Pueblo West's caliche and rock get expensive fast. Past a certain distance, the utility runs alone approach the price of a small new ADU.
  • You plan to skip permits. Unpermitted living space causes problems when you sell, insurers can deny claims tied to an unpermitted structure, and the county can order it vacated. Many people assume the 200-square-foot permit exemption for storage sheds covers living space. It doesn't. That exemption covers storage only. Once someone sleeps there, it's a dwelling and needs a permit.
  • You're counting on it to raise your home's value dollar for dollar. Appraisers often give a converted shed much less credit than the money you put in, especially if it's on a separate foundation with no permanent connection to the main house. Treat the cost as what it takes to get usable space, not as an investment you'll recoup.
  • You need a full-time home for more than one person in under 300 square feet. A backyard office, guest room or short stay for a relative can work fine. Two adults living there full-time through a Pueblo winter usually don't last.
  • The shed was built before about 2005 and has water damage, sagging floors or rot at the base. You'll replace so much of it that you're really building new, just with worse framing.

Colorado's 2024 ADU law, HB24-1152, pushed many Front Range communities to allow accessory dwellings. Don't assume it automatically makes your Pueblo West lot eligible. Pueblo West is unincorporated, so zoning goes through Pueblo County and building permits go through the Pueblo Regional Building Department. Call both before spending a dollar. A 20-minute phone call can save you a $10,000 mistake.

Pueblo West conditions that change the shed you should buy

Most conversion guides are written for mild, wet climates. Pueblo West is different:

  • Wind. Spring gusts here often top 50–60 mph. Lightweight sheds with no hurricane ties and roofs attached with only a few nails can lose shingles or whole roof panels. Pick a shell with engineered roof-to-wall connections and budget for a permanent anchoring foundation.
  • Sun and UV. At about 4,700 feet elevation, the sun is harsh on siding and roofing. Wood-look composite siding and dark shingles fade and break down faster than they would in the Midwest. Lighter roofing also keeps an insulated shed noticeably cooler.
  • Big temperature swings. A 40-degree swing between day and night is common. Spray foam or careful air sealing matters more here than adding extra R-value on paper.
  • Expansive soils and caliche. Some Pueblo West lots have clay that swells, while others hit hardpan a foot down. Either one can add a day or two of excavation and a few thousand dollars to the foundation.

The step most people forget: clearing the site

The shed usually isn't the problem. The spot you want to put it on is. Most Pueblo West conversions start with at least one of these:

  • An old shed in the way. If the old shed on your lot is short-walled, rotted or a gambrel, it's rarely worth saving. Our [shed demolition and removal](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) service tears it down and hauls away the whole structure, including the floor, so you're left with a bare pad.
  • Decades of stored stuff. Sheds and garages fill up before anyone plans a project. A [garage cleanout](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) or general [junk removal](/services/junk-removal-pueblo) visit clears out the old tools, paint cans and furniture before the concrete truck arrives.
  • Uneven ground, old concrete or rock. A permanent foundation needs a level, compacted pad. We handle [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) and [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo), so you don't have to schedule a separate grading crew.
  • Construction scrap during the build. Drywall offcuts, insulation bags, packaging and old siding pile up quickly. [Construction debris removal](/services/construction-debris-removal-pueblo) keeps the site safe and keeps your neighbors from complaining.

Doing site prep before the shed is delivered usually saves time. Delivery drivers need a clear, level path, and many dealers charge a return-trip fee of $150–$400 if they can't set the building on the first attempt.

A realistic timeline

If everything goes smoothly, a permitted shed conversion in Pueblo West takes about 3 to 6 months:

1. Weeks 1–4: Zoning check, septic or sewer evaluation, and site plan drawings. 2. Weeks 3–8: Permit review with the Regional Building Department. It can be faster for simple plans and slower in the spring rush. 3. Week 6–9: Site clearing, old shed demolition, grading and foundation. 4. Weeks 8–10: Shell delivery or construction, then utility trenching. 5. Weeks 10–20: Rough-in inspections, insulation, drywall, finishes and final inspection.

Whoever quotes you "done in a month" is either skipping permits or skipping inspections.

Our honest recommendation

If you already have a solid, tall-wall shed on a lot with utilities close by and a clear answer from the county, converting it is one of the cheapest ways to add usable space in Pueblo West. If you're buying a shed specifically to live in, buy a tall ranch or studio-style shell with 8-foot walls, 2x6 framing and 16-inch spacing, and get your permit answers before you sign.

If you don't have those things, you'll get more for your money by tearing down the old shed and starting with a clean pad.
