WHAT PROGRAM JUNK REMOVAL PROS USE

ClearPath Junk Removal runs a five-piece software stack: Jobber for scheduling, estimates, and invoicing; CallRail for call tracking; Square for card payments in the field; CompanyCam for timestamped before-and-after photos; and QuickBooks Online for the books. Routing is plain Google Maps, because Pueblo West to the Pueblo transfer station is a 10-mile run and no algorithm improves on that. Most junk removal companies use one of five programs: Jobber, Housecall Pro, Workiz, Service Autopilot, or ServiceTitan. National franchises run proprietary systems instead, like 1-800-GOT-JUNK's internal dispatch platform and College Hunks Hauling Junk's own crew app. If you asked because you want to know what happens after you hit "request a quote," the answer is below.

What each program actually does on your job

Software in this trade does four things, and everything else is decoration.

  • Takes the job and holds the slot. Your request lands in Jobber as a work request with your address, item list, and photos. It becomes a scheduled visit with a two-hour arrival window, not a "sometime Thursday."
  • Puts a number in writing before we drive. The estimate goes out by text and email with a link. You approve it with a tap. That approval is timestamped, which matters later if anyone remembers the price differently.
  • Documents the load. CompanyCam captures the space before and after, with GPS and a time stamp baked into each photo. For a [storage unit cleanout](/services/storage-unit-cleanout-pueblo) or a [foreclosure or eviction cleanout](/services/foreclosure-eviction-cleanout-pueblo), that photo set is the deliverable as much as the empty room is.
  • Takes payment and closes the file. Square processes the card on site at 2.6% plus 10 cents for tap or dip, 3.5% plus 15 cents if we key it in over the phone. The invoice syncs to QuickBooks Online that night.

That's it. Notice what's missing: nothing in the stack lifts a sectional down a flight of stairs.

The photo-estimate problem nobody advertises

Every modern platform now offers estimate-from-photo, and some bolt AI volume detection onto it. It works well on a garage full of boxes and cardboard. It fails hard on anything weight-driven, and that failure is structural, not a bug.

Volume-based pricing assumes your load weighs somewhere near 300 to 400 pounds per cubic yard, which is about right for household goods. Broken concrete weighs roughly 3,700 pounds per cubic yard. A 15-yard trailer rated for a 7,000-pound payload is legally full at under two yards of concrete while looking 87% empty in a photo. Any app that quotes "a quarter truck" on a concrete photo is quoting a price we cannot honor. That's why [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) and [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) get priced by weight and by machine time, in person, on this end. The same goes for roofing tile, wet soil, and plaster.

Our photo estimates land within one-eighth of a truck about 90% of the time on ordinary household loads. On mixed construction debris, that figure drops closer to 70%, and we say so on the phone instead of after the tailgate is down.

When the software revises the price on site

Roughly 15% of our jobs get a price adjustment at the door. Honest list of reasons, because the app records all of them:

  • The pile grew. Two more mattresses appeared between the photo and the appointment.
  • Access is worse than described. A second-floor walk-out adds 20 to 30 minutes of carry time per load.
  • Weight surprise. A shed floor turns out to be set on a concrete pad. [Shed demolition and removal](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) prices swing hundreds of dollars on that single fact.
  • Restricted material. Freon appliances need certified refrigerant evacuation, tires carry a per-unit disposal fee, and paint, solvents, and pressurized tanks are not going in the truck at any price.

The revision goes on the same estimate screen, you approve it or you don't, and if you don't, we leave and you owe nothing. No program prevents a bad estimate. It only makes the correction visible instead of verbal.

When this isn't worth it and who should not hire us

This is the part that costs us money to write, so read it carefully.

Skip us entirely if you have one item and access to a pickup. The Pueblo transfer station is a 20-minute drive from most of Pueblo West. A single mattress, a couch, or a few bags of yard waste hauled yourself costs you a dump ticket in the $25 to $45 range plus an hour of your Saturday. Our $125 minimum exists because a two-person crew, a truck, insurance, and a dump run cost that much before anyone touches your couch. If your load is genuinely small and you own a trailer, we are the expensive option and we know it.

Skip the dump run and call us anyway if you're 70 years old with a stuck water heater. The math above assumes you can lift it. Most people who call us can't, or shouldn't.

If lowest price is the only criterion, we will lose. There is always a guy with a trailer and a Facebook post who will beat our number by $40 to $80. Sometimes he's great. What you give up is a certificate of insurance, a photo record, a written receipt, and someone who answers the phone in November. For a rental property, a probate sale, or anything where a third party may ask what happened to the contents, that paperwork is the entire product. For clearing your own brush pile, it's overhead you don't need.

Do not book an instant online quote for a hoarding or estate job. The software will happily let you. It shouldn't. A [hoarding cleanout](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) or [estate cleanout](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) needs a walkthrough because the variables are hidden items, biohazard exposure, floor condition, and things family members still want to keep. Self-booking those from a phone photo produces a bad number and a worse day.

If you're an operator reading this to pick software, don't buy the expensive one. A one-truck company paying $9,000 a year for an enterprise platform is buying dispatch boards, technician scorecards, and pricebook features it will never open. Start at $39 a month, run it for a full season, and upgrade only when you have a second truck and a real scheduling conflict. Route optimization is the clearest example of a tool that doesn't pay here: in a service radius under 15 miles with four to six stops, OptimoRoute saves us about 8 minutes a day, which does not cover $39 a month.

Software also can't fix the landfill's hours or the weather. Saturday afternoons and the days after a windstorm are our two chokepoints. No scheduling algorithm creates a slot that doesn't exist.

What we don't use, and why

We don't run a customer-facing mobile app. Two people asked for one in three years, and a texted photo link works on every phone in Pueblo West without a download. We don't use automated review-request drip sequences past a single text 24 hours after the job, because four reminders to rate a dumpster run annoys people. We don't use dynamic surge pricing, which some platforms now support. Charging more on a Saturday because demand is high is legal and it is also a good way to never get a referral in a town this size.
