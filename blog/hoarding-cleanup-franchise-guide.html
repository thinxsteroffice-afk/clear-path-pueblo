HOARDING CLEANUP FRANCHISE GUIDE

If you searched "hoarding cleanup services franchise" from Pueblo West, you probably want one of two things. Either you need a hoarded home cleared and assume a national brand is the safe choice, or you're thinking about buying into a hoarding cleanup franchise. For hiring: the franchise name on the truck tells you less than three things: whether the crew is trained for the home's clutter level, whether they price by volume, and whether they can handle biohazards or will refer that part out. For buying: junk removal and hoarding franchises usually charge a $35,000–$60,000 franchise fee, with total startup costs often $100,000–$300,000 or more. What You're Actually Paying For in a Hoarding Cleanup

Hoarding cleanup is not a bigger garage cleanout. It differs in three ways that affect price and outcome:

  • Volume is hard to see. Rooms packed to the ceiling hide 2–3 times more material than they appear to hold. A 1,400 sq ft Pueblo West ranch home with moderate hoarding can fill 4–10 full truckloads.
  • Sorting takes most of the time. Families usually want cash, documents, photos, firearms and keepsakes pulled out before anything is thrown away. Hand-sorting can take up 50–70% of the labor hours.
  • Condition sets the crew. Mold, rodent droppings, spoiled food and pet waste need protective equipment: respirators, Tyvek suits and gloves. Some of it legally needs a remediation specialist.

Most professionals use the Institute for Challenging Disorganization's Clutter-Hoarding Scale, which has 5 levels. Levels 1–2 are clutter that one crew can usually clear in 1 day. Level 3 brings sanitation problems and typically takes 2–3 days. Levels 4–5 can involve structural damage, blocked exits, failed plumbing or animal waste, and can take 5 days or longer. They often need several contractors.

Typical Costs in the Pueblo West Area

These are realistic ranges for Pueblo County. Your number depends on volume, sorting needs and condition:

  • Level 1–2 (heavy clutter, sanitary): $600–$2,000, usually 1–3 truckloads
  • Level 3 (clutter plus sanitation issues): $2,000–$5,000
  • Level 4–5 (hazards, structural or biohazard concerns): $5,000–$15,000+, and more when remediation is required
  • Per-truckload pricing: most local haulers charge about $450–$700 for a full load, with disposal included

For a Level 3 or higher job, ask for a walkthrough estimate in writing, not a phone quote. Phone quotes on hoarded homes are often off by 30–50% because nobody can see what's behind the front room.

Franchise vs. Local Crew: What Actually Differs

National hoarding and junk franchises have real strengths. They tend to have standard training, call centers that answer around the clock, and sometimes biohazard certification under the same brand. If you're managing a parent's home in Pueblo West from out of state, one national 800-number can feel easier.

The tradeoffs are worth knowing:

  • Territory gaps. Franchise territories are drawn by population. Pueblo West often falls in the edge zone of a Colorado Springs or Pueblo territory, which can mean trip charges of $50–$150 or waits of 1–2 weeks.
  • Pricing set to cover royalties. Franchisees typically pay 6–10% of gross revenue in royalties, plus 1–3% in marketing fees. That cost shows up in your quote.
  • Subcontracted crews. Some franchise locations staff hoarding jobs with day labor. Ask who will physically be in the house.
  • Local disposal knowledge. A crew that works Pueblo County every week knows which items the landfill takes, where donations go, and how to handle tires, paint and appliances without paying extra disposal fees.

Some locally run crews are better than some franchise locations, and some are worse. Judge the crew in front of you.

> The brand on the truck tells you who gets the royalty check. It doesn't tell you who will be in your mother's kitchen deciding what's trash.

When Hoarding Cleanup Is NOT Worth Paying For

This section may cost us jobs. Here are the situations where hiring a crew like ours is the wrong move, or not the first move:

1. The person living there hasn't agreed to it. This is the most common way these jobs fail. Studies of forced cleanouts, often ordered by code enforcement or pushed by family, find that most homes fill up again within 12–24 months when the resident received no therapy or follow-up support. You could spend $6,000 to be back where you started in 2 years, and damage the relationship as well. If the resident is alive and living in the home, start with a therapist who specializes in hoarding disorder, which affects about 2–6% of adults. A crew should come later.

2. There's human waste, decomposition, needles or heavy rodent infestation. A junk removal crew, franchise or local, generally should not be the primary contractor here. You need a licensed biohazard or trauma remediation company, which typically charges $1,500–$10,000+ depending on contamination. Hantavirus is a real concern in rural Colorado: deer mouse droppings in closed sheds and outbuildings are a known risk in our dry climate. If a junk hauler says they'll "just bag it up," find someone else. We'll tell you up front if your job is outside our scope.

3. It's animal hoarding. Live animals in unsafe conditions are a welfare and legal matter first. Contact Pueblo Animal Services before you hire anyone to clear the property.

4. It's really Level 1–2 and you have willing helpers. If the home is cluttered but sanitary, you have 3–4 able-bodied family members and a free weekend, a rented roll-off dumpster may cost $400–$600 and get you most of the way. You'll be paying in sweat and sore backs, but you'll save money.

5. The house will be demolished or sold as-is to an investor. Some cash buyers in Pueblo County will take a property with the contents still inside and price the cleanout into their offer. If you're selling regardless, get one investor quote before paying $8,000 to empty a house they'd have bought full.

6. You want to buy a hoarding cleanup franchise to serve Pueblo West. Be realistic about the math. Hoarding jobs are high-value but rare. Even at the high end of the prevalence estimate, a market this size produces a limited number of homes each year where the family is ready to pay. Most franchisees in this niche survive on general junk removal and fold hoarding work in. If you're weighing a $150,000+ investment, talk to at least 5 current franchisees in similar-sized markets before you sign. Ask how long it took them to break even.

How a Good Hoarding Cleanout Works, Step by Step

Whoever you hire, a responsible cleanout in Pueblo West should look roughly like this:

1. Walkthrough (30–60 minutes). The crew assesses the clutter level, safety hazards, exit paths, utilities and anything needing a specialist. 2. Written plan. This covers which rooms come first, what gets sorted versus hauled straight out, and a "never throw away" list: documents, photos, cash, medications, firearms, keys. 3. Safety setup. Protective gear, a cleared exit route, and sometimes utilities shut off if there's water damage. 4. Sort and remove. Three streams: keep, donate and dispose. Donations go to local charities when the items are usable. 5. Final sweep. The crew removes debris down to bare floors. Deep cleaning or remediation follows if needed. 6. Handoff. You get the recovered valuables, receipts for donations, and a note on any home damage found, such as soft floors, roof leaks or rodent entry points.

A Level 3 home in Pueblo West typically takes 2–3 working days with a 3–4 person crew. Be wary of anyone promising a Level 4 job in one afternoon.

Questions to Ask Any Hoarding Cleanup Company

Before you sign with a franchise or a local crew, get clear answers to these:

  • Will you do a walkthrough before quoting, and will the quote be in writing?
  • Who will physically be on site: employees or subcontractors?
  • What happens if you find biohazards partway through? Do you stop, refer out or charge extra?
  • How do you handle valuables, cash and documents you find?
  • Do you donate usable items, and will I get receipts?
  • What's your trip charge to Pueblo West, if any?
  • Are you insured for work inside an occupied or damaged home?

If a company won't answer those clearly, don't hire them, whether they're a franchise or a local outfit.
