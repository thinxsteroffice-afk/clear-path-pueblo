HOT TUB COVER ALTERNATIVES & COSTS

The best alternatives to a hot tub cover are a floating thermal blanket ($30–$90), new foam cores for your existing cover skin ($100–$250), a DIY rigid foam board lid (about $60–$150 in materials), and a hard aluminum or composite cover ($1,500–$4,500). In Pueblo West, the blanket and the foam-core swap give you the most for your money. The DIY lid works as a stopgap for a few weeks. The hard cover only makes sense if you plan to keep the tub another 10+ years. None of these is a good long-term replacement for a lockable, insulated cover once winter arrives. Our altitude, dry air and wind punish weak solutions quickly. The rest of this article covers what each option costs, where each one fails, and when it makes more sense to stop spending money on the tub.

Why Pueblo West Is Hard on Hot Tub Covers

Most cover advice is written for humid, low-elevation places. Pueblo West is at roughly 4,700 feet, gets about 300 days of sunshine a year, and has relative humidity that often drops below 20% on summer afternoons. That combination does three things:

  • UV breaks down vinyl faster. A standard marine-vinyl cover often lasts 5–7 years in milder climates. Here, 3–5 years is more typical before the skin cracks on the sun-facing side.
  • Evaporation is aggressive. Dry air and wind can pull an inch or more of water out of an uncovered tub in a week. That water takes heat and chemicals with it.
  • Wind is a structural problem. Spring gusts of 50+ mph are common along the Front Range. Lightweight lids end up in the neighbor's yard, and anything not strapped down will get lifted.

Winter matters too. January nights in Pueblo West regularly drop into the teens. A tub held at 102°F loses heat fast when it's 15°F outside, which is why a good insulated cover is worth so much here.

Option 1: Floating Thermal Blanket ($30–$90)

A floating blanket is a sheet of closed-cell foam or heavy-duty bubble material cut to sit directly on the water, under your cover or in place of it.

What it does well: - Cuts evaporation sharply, often by 90% or more when it fits edge to edge - Keeps heat and chemicals from collecting on the underside of your foam cover, which is a major reason covers get waterlogged - Costs less than a single month of heating an uncovered tub in winter

Where it falls short: It has almost no structural strength, it doesn't lock, and it gives zero protection against a child or pet falling in. Wind can flip one right out of the tub. Used alone, it works from late spring through early fall and fails in December.

The best use: put a blanket under your existing cover. That alone can add a year or two to the cover's life.

Option 2: Replace the Foam Cores, Keep the Skin ($100–$250)

This is the option most people never hear about. A hot tub cover is two foam cores (usually tapered 4"-to-2" or 5"-to-3") wrapped in plastic and sealed inside a vinyl shell. When a cover gets heavy and sags in the middle, the vinyl is often fine. The foam inside has absorbed water.

Try the bathroom scale test: a new cover for a standard 7'x7' tub weighs about 40–60 lbs. If yours weighs 80 lbs or more, the cores are waterlogged. Badly saturated covers can top 150 lbs, which also strains the hinge and cover lifter.

If the vinyl isn't cracked, you can unzip the skin, pull the old cores, and slide in new ones wrapped in 6-mil polyethylene. Replacement cores run about $100–$250 depending on size and density. Restoring insulation on a cover with a good skin can cost 30–50% of what a full new cover costs.

Skip this option if: the vinyl is split along the fold or the zippers are corroded. A new core in a leaking skin will soak up water again within a season.

Option 3: DIY Rigid Foam Board Lid ($60–$150)

People who have just thrown out a destroyed cover sometimes build a temporary lid from 2-inch XPS foam board (the pink or blue sheets at the hardware store, around $40–$60 per 4'x8' sheet). They cut it to fit, tape the seams, and cover it with a heavy tarp.

That's a decent 2–6 week bridge while a custom cover is being made. Lead times for custom covers often run 2–4 weeks, sometimes longer in fall when everyone orders at once.

It isn't a real solution. Here's why: - XPS has no vinyl protection and degrades in direct UV in a matter of months - It's light enough that Pueblo West wind will carry it off unless you weight or strap it heavily - It can't hold any weight. A kid or dog stepping on it will go straight through into hot water - Tarps trap moisture, and the tub edge can grow mildew underneath

Option 4: Hard Aluminum or Composite Covers ($1,500–$4,500)

Hard covers use aluminum frames, insulated panels, or composite shells instead of vinyl-wrapped foam. Many can hold several hundred pounds of snow or weight, don't absorb water, and are rated to last 10–15+ years.

For Pueblo West, the strengths are real: they handle UV better than vinyl, they don't get waterlogged, and they don't blow away. Many models open on gas struts, so there's no 60-lb lid to wrestle.

The catch is cost and fit. They need clearance behind the tub to open, often 18–24 inches or more, which rules out tubs tucked against a house wall or under a low pergola. They're also the most expensive option by a wide margin.

Option 5: Enclosures, Gazebos and Pergolas

A roof over the tub isn't a cover, but it does help. Shade reduces UV damage to whatever cover you use, and a windbreak on the north and west sides reduces heat loss. A basic hardtop gazebo runs $1,000–$3,000. Treat it as an addition to a cover, not a replacement. A roof does nothing to stop evaporation from the open water surface.

When These Alternatives Aren't Worth It (and Who Shouldn't Bother)

As a junk removal company, we make money hauling hot tubs away. So here's the honest version, including the parts that cost us work.

Don't remove a working hot tub just because the cover is shot. A $60 floating blanket plus a $450 replacement cover is a fraction of the cost of removal, and far less than buying a new spa later. If the heater, pumps and shell are fine, fix the cover and keep soaking.

The alternatives that are a poor fit:

  • Floating blanket alone in winter. Heat loss through an uncovered surface on a 15°F night can push your electric bill up $50–$100+ per month compared with a good cover, especially at Black Hills Energy residential rates. It's a summer tool.
  • DIY foam lids for households with kids or pets. This is the biggest failure mode on this list. Legitimate safety covers are built to the ASTM F1346 standard and lock. A foam board under a tarp looks solid and isn't. If young children live with you or visit, don't use it for even a weekend.
  • Hard covers on an aging tub. Spending $3,000 on a cover for a tub that's 15+ years old is usually money you won't get back. Most acrylic spas are designed for roughly a 15–20 year life, and pump, heater and control-board failures cluster near the end of it.
  • Core replacement on a cracked skin. Covered above, but it's the most common way people waste $200 on this project.

Who shouldn't buy any alternative at all: if you haven't used the tub in 12 months or more, and the cover is just the latest repair after a heater replacement or a leak, adding up the next round of costs usually tells you what to do.

> If the cover, plus whatever else is broken, comes to more than about $1,500, and the tub is more than 15 years old, you're paying to maintain a backyard fixture instead of using it.

At that point, removal often costs less than one more year of ownership. A tub you don't use still costs money to keep running: chemicals, electricity, water, and sometimes $150–$400 in freeze damage from a single cold snap if the power goes out. Quick Comparison

  • Floating thermal blanket: $30–$90 · 1–3 years · good summer option, unsafe as the only cover
  • New foam cores: $100–$250 · 3–5 years · best value if the vinyl skin is intact
  • DIY foam board lid: $60–$150 · weeks to months · stopgap only, never with kids around
  • New vinyl/foam cover: $350–$900 · 3–5 years in Pueblo West · the standard choice
  • Hard aluminum/composite cover: $1,500–$4,500 · 10–15+ years · only for newer tubs you'll keep
  • Gazebo or pergola: $1,000–$3,000 · 10+ years · an add-on, not a replacement

Making Covers Last Longer in Pueblo West

Whatever you choose, a few habits help in our climate:

  • Use wind straps with metal clips, not the thin factory ties. Check them every March before the spring wind season.
  • Apply a vinyl UV protectant every 4–6 weeks from April through September.
  • Leave the cover open for 10–15 minutes after shocking the water so chlorine gas doesn't eat the underside.
  • Flip or rotate the cover once a season if it's symmetric, so one side doesn't take all the afternoon sun.
  • Brush snow off after storms. Heavy, wet spring snow can bend the center fold and crack the foam cores.
