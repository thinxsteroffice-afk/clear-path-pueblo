DO YOU NEED JUNK REMOVAL INSURANCE?

Yes — and the useful version of that answer is this: Colorado does not license junk removal companies or require them to carry insurance, so nobody is checking on your behalf. Any hauler who walks inside your home, carries a 320-pound chest freezer down basement stairs, or backs a loaded truck across a Pueblo West yard should carry general liability (commonly $1 million per occurrence / $2 million aggregate), commercial auto, and workers' compensation if they have even one employee. If *you're* the one starting a hauling business, Colorado requires workers' comp from your first hire and auto liability of at least $25,000/$50,000/$15,000. Below: what each policy actually pays, what it costs, how to verify it in four minutes, and the jobs where demanding a certificate is a waste of your time.

What Colorado Actually Requires — And What It Doesn't

There is no state junk hauler license. No board, no exam, no registration number to look up. The Colorado Public Utilities Commission permits household goods movers — the people who move your furniture to a new address — but hauling items to a landfill or transfer station isn't a regulated move, so don't expect a PUC permit number from a junk company. Anyone with a $2,500 trailer and a Facebook page can legally quote your garage cleanout tomorrow.

What Colorado *does* require:

  • Workers' compensation for any employer with one or more employees, full- or part-time (C.R.S. 8-40-302). Penalties for going without run up to $500 per day, and up to $1,000 per day for a repeat violation.
  • Commercial auto liability at the state minimum of $25,000 bodily injury per person, $50,000 per accident, and $15,000 property damage. Most commercial accounts are written far above that — $1 million combined single limit is the standard a property manager will ask for.
  • Proper disposal. CDPHE regulates where waste lands, and refrigerant-bearing appliances require certified recovery before scrapping. Illegal dumping in Pueblo County is a real citation, and the *property owner* sometimes catches the complaint when debris traced back to their address ends up in an arroyo.

Pueblo West adds no business license of its own — it's an unincorporated metropolitan district, not a municipality — so there's no district permit to ask for either. The certificate of insurance is genuinely the only paper trail available to you.

The Three Policies, And What Each One Pays

General liability covers the hauler damaging *your* property or injuring a third party. A dresser corner punched through drywall, a dropped washer cracking a tile floor, your neighbor's kid hurt by a swinging tailgate. Typical limits: $1M per occurrence, $2M aggregate.

Commercial auto covers the truck and trailer. This is the big one people skip. A loaded 14-foot dump trailer rolling backward into your garage door is an auto claim, not a GL claim, and a personal auto policy will deny it outright under the business-use exclusion.

Workers' compensation covers the hauler's own crew. If an uninsured helper blows out a disc lifting your hot tub shell, the medical bills look for a payer — and your homeowners liability is the next-nearest pocket.

> A certificate of insurance is not a quality guarantee. It's a receipt proving somebody else pays if we're wrong.

The Coverage Gap Nobody Mentions

Here's the part most "why hire insured haulers" articles leave out: general liability usually excludes damage to property in the hauler's care, custody, or control.

Read that again in practice. If we drop your grandmother's armoire while carrying it out, the GL policy may well decline the claim on the armoire itself — while still covering the stair railing it smashed on the way down. Coverage for the goods being hauled comes from a separate motor truck cargo or inland marine endorsement, often written at $10,000–$50,000, and plenty of small operators don't carry it.

So if you're having us move something you want to *keep* — a piano going to storage, a tool chest to your son's place — ask specifically about cargo coverage, not general liability. Different question, different policy, different answer.

What Insurance Costs a Pueblo Hauler

You're paying for this in your quote, so here are the real numbers a one-truck Pueblo County operation sees in 2025–2026 quotes:

  • General liability, $1M/$2M: roughly $650–$1,800 per year
  • Commercial auto, one 1-ton dump truck plus trailer: roughly $2,400–$6,500 per year
  • Workers' comp, refuse collection class codes: commonly $4–$9 per $100 of payroll — the highest-rated work most small contractors ever buy
  • Cargo endorsement, $25,000 limit: often $400–$900 per year

Fully covered, a single-truck crew carries $5,000–$12,000 per year in premium before a dollar of fuel or tipping fees. On 400 jobs a year that's $12–$30 baked into every job. An uninsured competitor can genuinely underbid by that much, and now you know exactly why their $140 quote beats a $185 one.

When Insurance Verification Isn't Worth It

This section costs us work, and it's the most honest part of the page.

Skip the certificate hunt entirely when:

  • The job is curbside and under about $175. A single mattress at the end of your driveway, a couch on the lawn. Nobody enters your home, nothing comes down stairs, no truck leaves the pavement. You are protecting roughly $150 of exposure with a 20-minute paperwork exercise. Hire the neighbor with a trailer.
  • You own a truck and the load is small. Area landfill and transfer station gate fees run in the neighborhood of $35–$60 per ton with minimum charges, and a U-Haul trailer runs $35–$70 a day. Two hours and $90 beats any quote we'll give you for one dresser and a bag of yard waste. We'd rather tell you that than take the job.
  • You're renting a dumpster anyway. If you already have a 20-yard container in the driveway for a remodel, a labor-only helper at $30–$45 an hour is cheaper than a full-service hauler, and the container company's insurance is the one that matters.

And the limits of insurance itself, plainly:

  • Deductibles swallow small damage. With a $500–$2,500 deductible, a scuffed baseboard or a cracked sprinkler head never becomes a claim. The honest fix is the hauler paying out of pocket — which means the hauler's character matters more than the hauler's paperwork on any damage under $1,000.
  • Claims are slow. Expect 30–90 days from report to payment, longer with a liability dispute. Colorado small claims court caps at $7,500 and often resolves a $600 fence argument faster than an adjuster will.
  • Insured does not mean competent. A $2M policy has never once protected a hardwood floor. Crews with the best paper sometimes have the worst hands. Ask how many hot tubs they've pulled this year, not just whether they're covered.
  • Policies lapse. A certificate dated four months ago proves nothing. Nonpayment cancellations can take effect on as little as 10 days' notice, and the certificate in your inbox doesn't update itself.
  • Some damage is excluded outright. Lawn ruts, septic damage, and driveway cracking from vehicle weight get disputed constantly as wear-and-tear rather than sudden accident.

The Pueblo West Risk Nobody Puts On a Certificate

Local specific, and it's the one we actually worry about: much of Pueblo West is on septic with a leach field, and lots routinely run a half-acre to five acres with long dirt or caliche driveways. A loaded truck grossing 19,000 pounds driven across a leach field can crush lateral lines, and a septic repair here commonly lands between $5,000 and $18,000. No general liability adjuster pays that cheerfully — they'll argue the homeowner directed the truck's path.

So before any heavy equipment or loaded truck leaves your driveway, mark your tank lid, your field, and your well head. That five-minute conversation prevents more loss than any policy limit. It's the first thing we ask about on [hot tub removal](/services/hot-tub-removal-pueblo) and [skid steer work](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo).

How to Verify a Certificate in Four Minutes

1. Ask for the ACORD 25 certificate emailed directly from the hauler's agent or broker, not a photo of a PDF. 2. Check the effective and expiration dates. Today's date must fall between them. 3. Confirm the named insured matches the business name on your quote and invoice. Mismatches are common and they void your recourse. 4. Call the agency phone number printed on the certificate — not a number the hauler texts you — and ask whether the policy is active and whether workers' comp is in force. 5. For rentals, estates, and bank-owned work, request an additional insured endorsement naming you or the property owner. A certificate alone does not give you standing to file.

Where It Genuinely Matters

Demand full documentation, every time, on these:

  • Bank-owned, probate, and tenant-turnover work. Asset managers typically require $1M GL, $1M auto, workers' comp, and an additional insured endorsement before anyone touches the property. See [foreclosure and eviction cleanouts](/services/foreclosure-eviction-cleanout-pueblo).
  • Biohazard or heavily soiled interiors, where crew injury and liability risk are highest — [hoarding cleanouts](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) are the clearest case.
  • Anything structural: [shed demolition](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo), [deck and fence removal](/services/deck-fence-removal-pueblo), and [concrete work](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo), where a misjudged tear-out can take a utility line or a shared fence with it.
  • Estate clearouts with heirs involved. Multiple beneficiaries means somebody will eventually ask who authorized what. Paper protects you. [Estate cleanouts](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) go smoother with it.

The Honest Bottom Line

Insurance is a financial backstop for the 1-in-200 job that goes sideways — not a quality signal, not a skill test, and not worth chasing on a $120 curbside pickup.
