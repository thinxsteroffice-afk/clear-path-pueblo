HOW OFTEN TO REPLACE HOT TUB FILTER

Replace your hot tub filter cartridge every 12 to 24 months — and in Pueblo West, plan on the short end of that: 12 to 15 months for a tub used 3+ times a week. Between replacements, rinse the cartridge with a hose every 2 weeks, give it a chemical soak every 3 months, and swap in a fresh one the moment the pleats stay matted after cleaning. Our hard water, blowing caliche dust, and high evaporation rates age cartridges faster than the generic "every two years" advice you'll find online. A single cartridge runs $25–$90; a four-cartridge set for a larger tub runs $120–$200. That is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy on a $7,000 appliance.

Why Pueblo West Chews Through Filters Faster

Filter life is a function of what's in your water and what blows into your tub. Both work against you here.

  • Water hardness. Published water quality reports for the Pueblo area typically put hardness in the 90–150 ppm range (moderately hard), and some Pueblo West households on private wells test well above 200 ppm. Calcium precipitates onto filter fibers as scale. Scale doesn't rinse off with a hose — it needs an acid-based cleaner, and after two or three scale cycles the pleats lose permeability permanently.
  • Dust and grit. Pueblo West's soil throws fine caliche and clay dust on any windy day, and spring gusts over 40 mph are routine. That grit embeds in the filter media in a way that pollen and leaves don't.
  • Evaporation and chemical concentration. At roughly 4,800 feet elevation with humidity often under 30%, an uncovered tub can lose 1–3 inches of water a week in summer. Every top-off adds more calcium and raises total dissolved solids, which accelerates both scaling and the cyanuric acid buildup that kills your sanitizer.
  • Sunscreen season is long. Body oils and sunscreen are the number one cause of a filter that looks clean but flows badly. Oils glaze the fibers. A hose rinse removes zero percent of it.

The Maintenance Schedule That Actually Holds Up

Here's the calendar that keeps a cartridge alive for a full year or more:

  • Every 10–14 days: Pull the cartridge, hose it from the inside out, fan the pleats with your thumb. Takes 5 minutes.
  • Every 90 days: Overnight chemical soak — 8 to 12 hours in a filter cleaner solution, then a 10-minute rinse to clear the degreaser out of the media. Skipping the rinse causes foaming when you reinstall it.
  • Every 3–4 months: Drain and refill. The standard formula is total gallons ÷ (3 × average daily bathers). A 400-gallon tub with two people soaking daily works out to 66 days between refills. Most Pueblo West owners stretch that to 120 days and pay for it in chemical consumption.
  • Every 12–15 months: New cartridge.

Buy two cartridges and rotate them. A soaked-and-dried cartridge lasts noticeably longer than one that goes back in wet, and you're never tempted to run the tub filter-less while one dries. Two cartridges at $45 each is a better value than one at $45 replaced twice as often.

How to Tell a Filter Is Dead, Not Just Dirty

Dirty filters clean up. Dead ones don't. Check for:

  • Pleats that stay flattened after a soak and a dry. If the fabric won't stand apart when you fan it, surface area is gone.
  • Permanent gray-brown banding that doesn't lighten after a 12-hour soak — that's embedded scale and oil, not stain.
  • A cracked or separated end cap, or a core you can flex with one hand. Water will bypass the media entirely and your "filtration" becomes decorative.
  • Fuzzy, frayed fiber along the pleat edges. Media that's breaking down sheds into your plumbing.
  • Heater or flow faults — FLO, FL1, OH, or similar codes on the topside panel — that clear the moment you remove the filter. That's a flow restriction telling you the cartridge is choked.

What a Clogged Filter Actually Costs You in January

This is the part most articles skip, and it's the reason this matters more here than in Phoenix.

A restricted filter starves the circulation pump. The flow switch or pressure switch opens, the heater locks out, and the tub stops heating — often silently, with only a small code on the panel. If that happens during a Pueblo West cold snap, where overnight lows of 12–18°F are normal in January and February, exposed plumbing in the equipment bay can freeze in under 24 hours even while the main body of water is still warm. A frozen-and-split manifold or pump wet end is not a cheap repair.

Rough numbers for what follows a neglected filter:

  • Heater element: $150–$400 for the part, more with labor
  • Circulation pump replacement: $350–$700 installed
  • Control pack / circuit board: $500–$1,200
  • A freeze-split plumbing manifold with foam excavation: often $800–$1,500, and on older tubs the shop will tell you it isn't worth it
  • Running the heater longer than necessary: at Pueblo-area residential electric rates of roughly $0.14–$0.16 per kWh, a poorly circulating tub in winter can add $20–$40 a month to your bill

> A $45 cartridge is the cheapest part in the entire machine, and it is the only part whose failure can take out the three most expensive ones.

When Replacing the Filter Is a Waste of Money

We haul hot tubs for a living. We'd rather tell you the truth than sell you a removal you don't need — or let you spend $200 on cartridges for a tub that's already finished.

Don't bother with a new filter if:

  • The shell is cracked or the acrylic has delaminated. Structural shell repair on a 15-year-old tub typically runs $800–$2,000 and rarely holds. New filters change nothing.
  • The foam insulation is waterlogged. Lift a corner panel. If the foam is wet, heavy, and dark, you have a leak somewhere inside a sealed cavity. Diagnosis alone can run 4–8 billable hours.
  • The tub is 18+ years old and parts are discontinued. Control packs for pre-2008 systems are often unavailable or cost more than the tub is worth.
  • It hasn't run in two or more years. Pumps seize, seals dry out, and rodents get into the equipment bay. Budget $1,200–$2,500 to recommission a long-dormant tub, with no guarantee.

And don't call us if:

  • Your only symptom is a flow code or weak jets. That's a $45 cartridge and an afternoon, not a $350 removal. Clean the filter, check the water level, and clear the suction grates first.
  • The tub still heats and holds water but you're tired of it. Working tubs have resale value. A functional 6-person tub in decent cosmetic shape sells locally for $500–$2,000. List it before you pay anyone to take it.
  • You're mid-winter and not in a hurry. Frozen ground, ice, and snow on a sloped Pueblo West lot make removal slower and more expensive. Waiting for spring is often the cheaper call.
  • You can handle it yourself. A reciprocating saw, a utility trailer, and two strong helpers plus landfill fees will get a small tub gone for well under what any crew charges. It's typically 4–7 hours of genuinely unpleasant work, and the fiberglass dust is miserable, but it's doable.

Draining and Disposal: The Pueblo West Specifics

Nobody writing nationally will tell you this, and it matters locally.

A large share of Pueblo West homes outside the metro district sewer area run on septic systems. Do not drain 400–500 gallons of hot tub water into a household drain on a septic system. That volume can hydraulically overload a tank sized for daily household flow, push solids into the leach field, and turn a filter question into a $8,000–$15,000 drainfield replacement.

Instead:

  • Stop adding sanitizer and let the tub sit 48–72 hours so free chlorine or bromine drops below 0.5 ppm before draining onto the ground
  • Run the discharge hose at least 50 feet from your leach field, well head, and any neighbor's property line
  • Spread the flow across gravel or an unplanted area — dumping that volume in one spot on Pueblo West's clay-heavy soil creates a pond, then a mud pit
  • Never drain into a storm inlet or arroyo

Old cartridges themselves go in household trash. They aren't recyclable — the media is bonded polyester on a plastic core, and no facility here separates them.

The Bottom Line for Pueblo West Owners

Rinse every two weeks. Soak every three months. Replace at 12 to 15 months, sooner if you're on a well or the pleats won't recover. Keep a spare on the shelf. That's somewhere around $60–$120 a year in filters against a tub that cost you four figures and a repair bill that can hit four figures again.
