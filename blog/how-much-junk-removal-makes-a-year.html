HOW MUCH JUNK REMOVAL MAKES A YEAR

A one-truck junk removal operation in the Pueblo West market realistically grosses $60,000 to $140,000 a year, and the owner-operator takes home roughly $28,000 to $65,000 of that after disposal fees, fuel, insurance, and taxes. Two trucks with paid crews can push gross revenue to $250,000–$450,000, with net margins typically landing between 12% and 22%. National franchise averages you'll see quoted — $500,000 or $1M+ per territory — are Denver, Phoenix, and Dallas numbers. They do not transfer to a community of roughly 35,000–40,000 people spread across 50-plus square miles of one-to-five-acre lots. Density is the single biggest driver of junk removal revenue, and Pueblo West has very little of it.

Here's the honest arithmetic, from a company that runs these routes.

What one load actually pays in Pueblo West

Junk removal is priced by volume, not weight, and the ceiling on any single job is the size of the truck. A standard dump-trailer or 12-foot dump body holds about 10–14 cubic yards. Local pricing in the Pueblo area generally runs:

  • Single item (couch, fridge, mattress): $85–$175
  • Quarter truck: $180–$260
  • Half truck: $300–$425
  • Full truck: $500–$750
  • Heavy specialty work (concrete, dirt, hot tubs, sheds): $450–$1,800 per job

The number people miss is loads per day. In a dense metro, a two-person crew can turn 5–7 jobs in a 9-hour day because the next stop is four minutes away. Out here, a job in the northwest end of Pueblo West, a landfill run down Highway 50, and a second job off McCulloch can burn 95 minutes of pure driving before anyone touches a piece of furniture. Three to four jobs is a strong Pueblo West day. Four full-truck days a week at $450 average is $7,200 a month gross — about $86,000 a year — and that assumes the phone rings consistently, which it doesn't.

The seasonality nobody puts in the revenue projection

Annual income in this market is not twelve equal months. Based on how demand actually moves along the Front Range's southern end:

  • March–June is the peak: yard debris, tumbleweed and dead-limb cleanup after wind season, spring [yard debris removal](/services/yard-debris-removal-pueblo) and garage projects. This stretch can carry 40–45% of the year's revenue.
  • July–September holds steady on remodels, deck teardowns, and pool and hot tub removals.
  • October–November brings estate and rental turnover work.
  • December–February is brutal. Expect 50–65% revenue drops versus May. Frozen ground kills dirt work, and nobody cleans a garage at 22 degrees.

That means a business grossing $95,000 for the year might book $14,000 in May and $3,200 in January. Anyone budgeting on the annual average divided by twelve runs out of cash in February. This is the most common way small hauling operations in southern Colorado die — not lack of demand, but a January they didn't reserve for.

Where the money leaks

Gross revenue is a vanity number in this business. The real figure is what survives disposal.

  • Tipping fees are the largest variable cost. Landfill disposal in the Pueblo area is charged by the ton, and a mixed household load commonly runs $45–$120 per trip. Across a year that's frequently 12–18% of gross revenue — more if you take heavy loads. Verify current rates directly with the facility; they change, and construction debris, tires, appliances with refrigerant, and mattresses often carry surcharges of $15–$40 each.
  • Fuel on a 3/4-ton or one-ton truck pulling a loaded trailer gets 8–11 MPG. At 25,000 annual miles that's roughly 2,500 gallons, or $8,000–$9,500 a year.
  • Commercial auto insurance runs $3,000–$7,000 per truck annually. General liability adds $600–$1,500. Personal auto policies do not cover hauling for hire — a denied claim on a $48,000 truck is a business-ending event.
  • Labor. Colorado's minimum wage is above $14/hour and indexes upward every January; competent help who won't damage a customer's drywall costs $19–$24/hour. Add workers' comp, which for hauling classifications is not cheap.
  • Truck and trailer reserve. A dump trailer eats tires, brakes, and bearings. Budget $4,000–$8,000 a year in maintenance and set aside more. Transmissions on trucks that tow loaded every day fail around 150,000–180,000 miles.
  • Self-employment tax at 15.3% on top of income tax. First-year operators who don't make quarterly payments routinely face a $6,000–$11,000 April surprise.

Run those against $95,000 gross and you land near $34,000 take-home for a solo operator working 45–55 hours a week. That's real money in Pueblo County, where the median household income sits in the $50,000s. It is not passive, and it is not the six-figure story sold in franchise brochures.

> The question isn't how much junk removal makes a year. It's how much it makes per mile — and in Pueblo West, every job carries more miles than the spreadsheet assumed.

When getting into this is a bad idea

We would rather say this plainly than watch someone burn $40,000 finding out.

Do not start a junk removal business in Pueblo West if:

  • You need reliable income within 90 days. Realistic ramp is 8–14 months to consistent booking. Months one through four often gross under $3,000 each. If you can't cover six months of personal expenses, this is the wrong bet.
  • Your capital is under $15,000. A usable truck, a dump trailer, insurance down payment, dump-fee float, and a Colorado business registration is $18,000–$45,000 done properly. Underfunded operators end up hauling at a loss to make truck payments.
  • You have a back injury, a bad knee, or you're over 55 and planning to do the lifting yourself. A steel-frame sleeper sofa on a staircase is 250–300 lbs. Cast-iron tubs run 300–500 lbs. This work injures people. Our own crews use two-person lifts and dollies on anything over 100 lbs for exactly that reason.
  • You want to buy a franchise for this market. Franchise fees of $60,000–$90,000 plus 8–10% royalties on gross are structured for metro population density. On $95,000 of Pueblo West revenue, a 9% royalty is $8,550 — most of a solo operator's annual profit — before you've paid the dump.
  • You're squeamish about the ugly jobs. The highest-paying work is [hoarding cleanouts](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo), [foreclosure and eviction cleanouts](/services/foreclosure-eviction-cleanout-pueblo), and estate clearing after a death. Rodents, biohazards, and grieving families are part of the job. If you'd only take clean garage jobs, your annual revenue drops 30–40%.

Where the ceiling really is: volume-based pricing caps you at truck capacity, so scaling means more trucks and more payroll — and each additional truck needs about $110,000–$130,000 in annual revenue to justify itself here. There is a real gap between one truck and three where you have all the overhead of a company and none of the margin. Plenty of operators stall there permanently. The businesses that clear $300,000+ in a market this size almost always add a second capability — [skid steer work](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo), [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo), or [concrete and rock hauling](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo) — because equipment jobs bill $135–$225 an hour instead of by the cubic yard.

What this means if you're hiring a hauler, not becoming one

If you searched this because a quote felt high, the cost structure above is the answer. On a $400 half-truck job in Pueblo West, roughly $70 goes to the landfill, $35 to fuel and vehicle wear, $45 to insurance and overhead allocation, and $120–$160 to two people's wages for two-plus hours including drive time. What's left is thinner than most people assume.

Practical takeaways:

  • Combine jobs. One full truck at $600 beats three separate $220 quarter-loads by $60, and saves two trips.
  • Book in the shoulder months. February and early March schedules are open; May books out 1–2 weeks.
  • Ask what's included. A quote that excludes dump fees isn't a quote. Ours doesn't work that way — see the [FAQ](/faq) for how pricing is set.
  • Be skeptical of the lowest bid. An operator underbidding by 30% is usually uninsured, and an uninsured hauler who drops a water heater through your subfloor becomes your problem.
  • Separate the metals. Appliances and scrap have salvage value; a hauler who recycles rather than landfills should price accordingly.

The short version

One truck, worked hard, in Pueblo West: $60,000–$140,000 gross, $28,000–$65,000 net. Two trucks with crews: $250,000–$450,000 gross, 12–22% net. Add equipment services and a genuine second revenue line and the ceiling moves; without one, this market's math holds you where it holds you. It's a legitimate living built on 45+ hour weeks, a February with no work, and a body that takes the wear.
