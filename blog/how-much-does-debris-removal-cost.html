HOW MUCH DOES DEBRIS REMOVAL COST

Debris removal in Pueblo West runs $95 to $150 for a single-item or minimum load, $250 to $375 for a half-truck load, and $450 to $650 for a full 15-cubic-yard truck. Heavy material — concrete, dirt, roofing shingles, rock — is priced by weight instead, generally $85 to $140 per ton hauled, because a half-full trailer of concrete weighs more than a packed load of couches. A one-acre Pueblo West lot cleared of tumbleweeds, dead elm limbs, and scattered scrap typically lands between $600 and $1,400. If you do it yourself, budget $32 to $45 per ton in tipping fees at the county landfill plus roughly 40 miles of round-trip driving per load.

Below is how those numbers actually get built, what makes them swing, and — the part most haulers skip — when hiring anyone at all is the wrong call.

What You're Actually Paying For

The dollar figure on a debris quote is not a landfill bill with markup. Roughly:

  • Disposal (25–35%) — tipping fees, which run about $32 to $45 per ton for mixed municipal waste at the Pueblo-area landfill, with surcharges layered on top for specific items.
  • Labor (35–45%) — two people, loading by hand, typically 90 minutes to 4 hours on a residential job.
  • Drive time and fuel (10–15%) — Pueblo West to the disposal site and back is roughly 20 miles each way, or about 45 minutes round trip including scale time.
  • Truck, insurance, and overhead (15–20%) — a dump trailer, a 1-ton truck, and $1M general liability aren't free.

That breakdown matters because it tells you where your money goes and where you can cut it. Sorting your own pile so metal goes to a scrapyard and green waste goes separately can shave real dollars. Stacking debris in the driveway instead of scattered across a back lot cuts labor time, and labor is the biggest line item.

Pueblo West Specifics That Change the Price

This market is not Colorado Springs and it is not Denver, and quotes built on metro-area assumptions will be wrong here.

Lot size. Pueblo West Metro District parcels are commonly 0.5 to 1 acre, some considerably larger. Debris on a 1-acre lot is spread out. A crew walking 200 feet each way with a wheelbarrow moves maybe 60% of the volume per hour it would move from a curbside pile. That difference alone can add $150 to $250 to a job.

No municipal bulk pickup. Pueblo West is unincorporated Pueblo County. There is no city bulky-item day the way Pueblo proper handles some scenarios. Your trash service will not take a hot tub, a shed frame, or 30 bags of yard waste. That's why private hauling exists here at all.

Wind and tumbleweeds. After a spring wind event, fence lines on the west side of Pueblo West collect Russian thistle in volumes that look small and measure large. A packed tumbleweed load is bulky and light — you pay for volume, not weight, and a fence line can easily fill 6 to 8 cubic yards.

Hail. A single storm can put a Pueblo West neighborhood into roof replacement season. If your roofer left shingle tear-off behind, that's 1.5 to 2 tons per 10 squares — weight-priced, usually $180 to $350 for a typical single-story ranch's leftovers.

Distance from the disposal site. Jobs out toward McCulloch Boulevard's west end or the Pueblo Reservoir side add drive time. Expect a $25 to $60 difference versus a job near the Purcell corridor.

Real Price Ranges by Debris Type

Numbers below are typical Pueblo West ranges, all-in, including disposal:

  • Single appliance — $75 to $125. Add $25 to $50 if it contains refrigerant requiring certified recovery. See [appliance removal pueblo](/services/appliance-removal-pueblo).
  • Mattress and box spring — $85 to $130 for the pair. Mattresses carry their own disposal surcharge of roughly $20 to $35 each at most facilities.
  • Garage cleanout — $350 to $900 depending on whether it's a two-car with a decade of boxes or a shop with paint, tires, and a dead riding mower. Tires alone add $5 to $18 each. Details at [garage cleanout pueblo](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo).
  • Yard debris after a storm — $200 to $700. Green waste sometimes diverts to a lower-cost composting stream, which is why brush often prices below household junk per cubic yard.
  • Construction and remodel debris — $400 to $1,200 for a bathroom or kitchen tear-out. Drywall, tile, and cast iron are heavy; a 10x12 bathroom gut runs 1.2 to 2 tons.
  • Concrete, brick, and landscape rock — $110 to $160 per ton, disposed separately at a clean-fill facility. A 10x20 patio slab at 4 inches thick is roughly 6 tons, so $650 to $950 is realistic.
  • Shed demolition and haul — $500 to $1,400 depending on whether it's a 8x10 wood kit or a 12x24 with a slab.
  • Hot tub — $350 to $600, because it must be cut apart before it moves.

Larger, spread-out property work — old fence rows, buried debris, rock piles, a decade of accumulation across an acre — usually moves to hourly equipment pricing at $145 to $195 per hour with a skid steer, which is dramatically cheaper than hand labor once the volume passes about 12 cubic yards. That's covered under [skid steer services pueblo](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo).

When Hiring Us Is the Wrong Move

This section will cost us jobs. It should.

You have one or two items and a pickup truck. If you have a broken dresser and a couple of bags of yard waste, do not pay $95 for a minimum load. Drive it yourself. The landfill will charge you a minimum gate fee in the $15 to $25 range for a small pickup load. You'll spend an hour and about $8 in fuel. Hiring anyone for that is throwing away $60.

You have a lot of debris, no deadline, and time on your hands. A 14-foot dump trailer rents for roughly $70 to $110 per day in the Pueblo area. If you can borrow a 3/4-ton truck and give up a Saturday, a full-acre cleanup that costs $900 professionally might cost you $250 to $400 in rental, fuel, and tipping fees. That's a real savings and we'd rather you know it.

Your debris is mostly clean burnable brush and you qualify for an open burn permit. Pueblo County issues open burning permits for unincorporated areas under specific conditions, with fees typically in the $10 to $30 range. If your pile is dry native brush, no treated wood, no trash, and there's no active fire ban, burning is by far the cheapest option. Check current restrictions with the county before striking a match — permits get suspended during red flag conditions, and Pueblo West sees plenty of those.

Your insurance claim covers debris removal. Many homeowner policies include a debris removal provision after a covered loss — often up to 5% of the dwelling limit. If a hailstorm or a fire caused the damage, call your adjuster before calling us. Paying out of pocket for something your policy already covers is a pure loss.

Your contractor already charged you for haul-off. Read the contract. Roofers, remodelers, and fence installers frequently bill debris disposal as a line item. If they left the pile, that's a phone call to them, not a second payment to a hauler.

We are not the right fit for these:

  • Hazardous materials. We do not take paint, solvents, motor oil, pesticides, asbestos, or propane tanks. Pueblo County runs household hazardous waste collection events — that's the correct and usually free channel. Asbestos-containing material requires a licensed abatement contractor, and the fine for improper disposal in Colorado runs into the thousands of dollars.
  • Anything requiring structural demolition with a permit. Pulling down an attached structure is a different trade with a different license.
  • Very small-budget jobs where price is the only factor. If a guy with a trailer on Facebook Marketplace quotes you $60 and you're fine with the risk, take it. Understand the tradeoff: unlicensed haulers sometimes dump illegally on county land or vacant lots, and Colorado law can trace liability back to the waste generator. That's the actual reason to pay for insured hauling — not because we lift better.

Failure Modes: How Quotes Go Wrong

The volume estimate was made over the phone. A homeowner who says "about a truckload" is wrong more often than right, usually low. Photos fix this. A quote given without photos or a walkthrough should be treated as a range, not a number.

Hidden weight. A pile that looks like brush but has a concrete birdbath and 400 pounds of landscape rock at the bottom will reprice on-site. Anything dense, wet, or soil-contaminated changes the math.

Access. A locked gate, a soft yard after rain, an alley too tight for a dump trailer, or a debris pile 150 feet behind a fence line all add labor. Say so upfront.

Buried or comingled debris. Old homesteads and long-held Pueblo West acreage often have partially buried material. Excavating it is [dirt work excavation pueblo](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) territory and prices at equipment rates, not junk-hauling rates.

Scope creep on cleanouts. "While you're here" is how a $400 job becomes $900. That's fine if you agree to it in writing before the extra work starts. Insist on that.

How to Get an Accurate Number

  • Take 4 to 6 photos from different angles, including one wide shot showing the path from the pile to the driveway.
  • Separate anything heavy — concrete, dirt, rock, shingles — into its own pile so it can be quoted by weight.
  • Pull out metal and appliances; scrap value can offset $20 to $80 on a mixed load.
  • Measure the pile roughly. Length x width x height in feet, divided by 27, gives cubic yards. A standard pickup bed is about 2.5 cubic yards.
  • Ask specifically whether the quote includes disposal fees, surcharges for mattresses or refrigerant, and fuel. A number that excludes tipping fees isn't a quote.
