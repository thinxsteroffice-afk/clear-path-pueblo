HOW OFTEN TO REPLACE HOT TUB WATER

Drain and refill your hot tub every three to four months under normal use. That's the baseline. In Pueblo West, with our dry air, hard fill water, and 300-odd sunny days a year, a lot of tubs need it closer to every 8 to 10 weeks. The more precise rule: divide your tub's gallon capacity by three times the number of people who soak in it daily. A 400-gallon tub used by two people every day works out to 400 ÷ 6 = 66 days. The real trigger, though, is total dissolved solids. When your TDS hits 1,500 ppm, or climbs 1,500 ppm above whatever your tap water started at, the water is spent — no amount of chlorine, shock, or clarifier brings it back.

Why Pueblo West tubs hit the wall faster than the manual says

Your owner's manual was written for a national average. We sit at roughly 4,900 feet with about 12 inches of annual precipitation and afternoon humidity that routinely drops to 20–30%. That combination does something specific to spa water that most hot tub articles skip entirely: it concentrates it.

An uncovered tub out here can lose 1 to 2 inches of water a week — call it 1 to 2 gallons a day in summer with a cover that no longer seals. Evaporated water leaves as pure H₂O. Every mineral, every calcium ion, every bit of dissolved sanitizer byproduct stays behind. Then you top off with a garden hose, and that hose water brings in a fresh load of minerals on top of what's already concentrated.

Run the math on a year of that. Topping off a gallon a day means you've added roughly 365 gallons of new fill water to a 400-gallon tub without ever draining it. You haven't diluted anything. You've nearly doubled the mineral load. That's why tubs here scale up and go cloudy on schedules that make no sense against the manual — the problem isn't bather load, it's evaporation chemistry.

The numbers that actually tell you it's time

Buy test strips or a digital TDS meter. A decent meter runs $15 to $30 and pays for itself the first time it stops you from dumping 400 gallons you didn't need to dump. Here's what to watch:

  • TDS at or above 1,500 ppm, or 1,500 above your fill water reading. Non-negotiable drain point.
  • Calcium hardness outside 150–250 ppm. Below that, water gets aggressive and eats heater elements and seals. Above it, you get scale on the heater and a chalky waterline.
  • pH that won't hold between 7.2 and 7.8 after two or three corrections in a week. Chasing pH is a symptom, not a problem to solve.
  • Total alkalinity drifting out of 80–120 ppm repeatedly.
  • Foam that reappears within an hour of adding defoamer. Defoamer is a cosmetic patch on surfactant-saturated water.

If three of those are true at once, don't buy more chemicals. Drain it.

> Chemicals treat water. They don't un-concentrate it. Once TDS is maxed, every dollar of shock you add is a dollar you're spending to not fix the problem.

Hard water, soft water, and the mistake people make

Pueblo West's district water comes off the Arkansas system, and hardness typically lands in a moderate range — pull your district's annual water quality report for the current number rather than guessing. Whatever it reads, write it down. That's your TDS and hardness baseline, and you can't evaluate your spa water without it.

The mistake we see: running the fill through a whole-house softener. Softened water swaps calcium for sodium, and water with near-zero calcium hardness goes looking for minerals — it pulls them out of your heater element, your pump seals, and the grout on any tile trim. If you're on a softener, fill from an outside bib upstream of it, or fill softened and add a calcium increaser to get back into the 150–250 ppm window.

If you're on a private well, which plenty of Pueblo West acreage still is, get a full water panel done. Iron and manganese that are harmless in a toilet tank will stain an acrylic shell brown in a single fill cycle.

What a refill actually costs you

This is the part that makes people stretch intervals too far, so here are real figures.

  • Water: 400 gallons at typical district rates in the $3–$5 per 1,000 gallons range is a few dollars. Not the expensive part.
  • Reheating: Bringing 400 gallons from about 55°F tap temperature up to 102°F takes roughly 46 kWh. At around 13 cents per kilowatt-hour, that's $6 on paper, $8–$12 in practice once you account for heat loss and heater efficiency. Budget 12 to 24 hours of heat-up time in cold months.
  • Startup chemicals: $10–$20 per fill to rebalance.
  • Time: 2 to 4 hours total, most of it waiting on a garden hose draining at 5 gallons a minute.

So a refill is a $20–$35 event, four times a year. Stretching your interval to save that is not a good trade against a $400–$900 heater replacement from scale damage.

Where you dump 400 gallons matters here

Two Pueblo West-specific cautions almost nobody writes about:

If you're on septic, do not drain the tub into a floor drain, laundry sink, or anything that runs to your tank. Four hundred gallons dropped into a septic system in under two hours is a hydraulic overload. It pushes solids into the leach field and can cost you four figures.

Watch the chemistry before it hits your landscaping. Let sanitizer levels fall below 1 ppm — stop adding chlorine or bromine 48 hours before you drain — and the water is fine on gravel, dirt, or an unplanted area. Dumped hot and chlorinated onto xeriscape plantings or a neighbor's downslope lot, it's a problem you'll hear about. Our lots are big enough that you usually have somewhere to put it. Use that.

And don't do a full drain in January. An empty tub with wet plumbing can freeze in a few hours when it's 15°F outside. If you must drain in deep winter, do it midday and have the refill hose ready before you pull the plug.

A schedule you can actually keep

  • Every week: test pH and sanitizer. Two minutes.
  • Every 2 weeks: rinse the filter cartridge with a hose.
  • Every 2 months: test TDS and calcium hardness, and chemically soak the filter overnight.
  • Every 3 months (or at 1,500 ppm TDS, whichever comes first): full drain, flush the plumbing lines with a purge product, wipe the shell, refill, rebalance.
  • Twice a year: inspect the cover. A waterlogged cover is the single biggest driver of evaporation out here, and evaporation is what's shortening your intervals.

Set a calendar reminder for the first week of January, April, July, and October. If your TDS meter says you need it sooner, believe the meter.
