What "24 hour" actually means in this market — three different promises

Search results for this phrase mix three unrelated services, and the ads rarely tell you which one you're clicking:

  • 24-hour answering. Someone picks up at 11 p.m. and books you for Tuesday. Most "24/7" appliance listings in Pueblo and Colorado Springs are this.
  • Service within 24 hours. A truck arrives inside one calendar day. This is what the overwhelming majority of Pueblo West callers actually need, and it's what ClearPath delivers on appliance removal.
  • True overnight dispatch. A crew at your door between midnight and 6 a.m. This exists for burst water lines and gas leaks. For a dead dryer, it does not exist here at any price worth paying, and any company implying otherwise is selling you an after-hours surcharge — commonly $95–$150 on top of the base rate — for a job that would cost the same at 7 a.m.

The 2 a.m. checklist (do this before you call anyone)

If your refrigerator or freezer quit overnight, the clock that matters is a food-safety clock, not a service clock. USDA guidance is specific and worth following exactly:

  • A closed refrigerator holds safe temperature for about 4 hours.
  • A full freezer holds for roughly 48 hours; a half-full freezer, about 24 hours.
  • Anything perishable held above 40°F for more than 2 hours gets thrown out. Don't taste-test it.
  • Move meat and dairy to a cooler with ice — a $25 bag of ice at the Walmart on Purcell beats a $300 grocery replacement.
  • Pull the plug and check the coils and the drip pan before you assume the compressor is dead. A coil packed with dog hair is a $0 fix with a vacuum, and it's a common cause of a fridge that "stopped cooling" in a Pueblo West garage in July.

That checklist frequently ends the emergency. What's left in the morning is either a repair call or a haul-off.

Repair or replace: the 50% rule, run against real numbers

The rule contractors use is simple — if the repair quote exceeds 50% of replacement cost, replace the unit. Applied to what actually breaks:

  • Dryer heating element: $180–$320 installed. New dryer: $500–$900. Repair it.
  • Washer transmission or main bearing: $400–$650. New washer: $600–$1,100. Coin flip at best. Usually replace.
  • Refrigerator sealed system / compressor: $600–$1,200. New comparable fridge: $900–$2,200. Almost always replace, especially on a unit past 10 years.
  • Water heater tank leaking from the shell: never repairable. A 40- or 50-gallon tank runs 8–12 years in Pueblo West's hard water, and softener-free households land at the low end.

That last point matters locally. Hard water shortens water heater life here, and we pull more prematurely scaled tanks out of Pueblo West garages than most crews would expect for the age of the homes.

Why appliance removal isn't the same as hauling a couch

The refrigerant rule is the reason. Refrigerators, freezers, window and portable AC units, dehumidifiers, and wine coolers contain refrigerant — older units on R-134a, newer ones often on R-600a isobutane. Under EPA Section 608, refrigerant must be recovered by a certified technician before the unit is scrapped, and disposal facilities will refuse or surcharge units that arrive uncertified. That's why a fridge costs more to remove than a dryer of similar size: there's a mandated processing step behind it.

The rest is weight and geometry:

  • Top-freezer refrigerator: 200–250 lb. Side-by-side: 300–350 lb. French-door with ice and water: up to 400 lb.
  • Washer: 150–200 lb. Dryer: 100–125 lb. Electric range: 130–180 lb.
  • Standard interior doorways run 30–32 inches; a counter-depth fridge is 30–36 inches deep. Doors come off, both the appliance's and sometimes yours.
  • Appliances are roughly 60–75% steel by weight, which is why the recycling side works — though at scrap prices near $150–$220 per ton, a 250-pound fridge is worth maybe $12–$25 gross before labor and refrigerant recovery. Anyone telling you they'll haul your fridge free because "scrap pays for it" is not doing the arithmetic.

Before you schedule, check whether Black Hills Energy currently runs a refrigerator or freezer recycling rebate for its Colorado customers — these programs come and go, and when active they've paid $35–$50 for a working second unit, often with free pickup. Confirm with the utility directly. If a rebate program will take it, take the rebate.
