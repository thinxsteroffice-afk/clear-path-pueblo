ILLINOIS CCDD RULES VS. COLORADO

Short answer: "Clean construction or demolition debris" (CCDD) is a legal category that exists only in Illinois. The Illinois Environmental Protection Act (415 ILCS 5/3.160) defines it as uncontaminated broken concrete with no protruding rebar, plus brick, rock, stone, reclaimed asphalt pavement, and soil from construction or demolition work. Under that law, CCDD can go to permitted fill sites, such as old quarries, instead of a landfill. If your project is in Pueblo West, Colorado, none of the Illinois rules apply to you. Colorado has no "CCDD" permit program. What does carry over is the sorting idea behind it, and using it here can lower what you pay to get rid of the debris. The rest of this article covers that.

What the Illinois CCDD Rules Actually Require (and Why You're Seeing Them)

Search engines show Illinois results because "CCDD" is a specific regulatory term, and most of the pages that use it are written for Illinois contractors. Here are the main points of that system:

  • Source certification: Since a 2010 amendment to the Act, soil sent to an Illinois CCDD fill site needs paperwork. Illinois EPA form LPC-663 covers sites with no history of contamination. Form LPC-662 covers sites that might be impacted, and a licensed Professional Engineer or Professional Geologist has to sign it.
  • Load screening: Illinois fill operators check incoming loads for contamination, usually with a photoionization detector (PID), and can turn a truck away at the gate.
  • What's excluded: Wood, drywall, roofing, insulation, and painted or treated material are not CCDD. In Illinois they are general construction and demolition debris and go to C&D recycling facilities. Those facilities have to recycle at least 75% of what they take in.

If your job is in Illinois, call the Illinois EPA Bureau of Land or a contractor based there. A Colorado hauler can't legally or practically handle Illinois disposal for you.

How Colorado Handles the Same Material

Colorado regulates construction waste through the state solid waste regulations (6 CCR 1007-2), which the Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment (CDPHE) administers. Colorado has no CCDD fill-site permit or LPC-style certification form. In practice, though, the materials still fall into two groups that are priced very differently:

1. Clean inert material: concrete, brick, rock, asphalt, and uncontaminated soil. If it's kept separate, it can often be crushed and recycled, reused as fill on site, or taken somewhere that charges less than a landfill. 2. Mixed C&D debris: lumber, drywall, shingles, carpet, fixtures, and packaging. This goes to a landfill or transfer station at the regular rate.

> The Illinois rule that CCDD can't have "protruding metal bars" is a good sorting standard for Pueblo West too. Concrete with rebar cut flush is easier to recycle and costs less to dispose of than slabs with steel sticking out.

The problem is mixing. One sheet of drywall or a stack of shingles thrown into a load of broken concrete can turn the whole load into mixed debris, and you then pay the higher rate on all of it.

The Weight Problem Nobody Warns You About

Most people underestimate this part. Clean demolition debris is extremely heavy:

  • Solid concrete weighs about 4,000 lbs per cubic yard. Broken concrete loosely piled in a truck bed still comes to roughly 2,500–2,800 lbs per cubic yard, because of the air gaps.
  • Soil runs 2,000–2,700 lbs per cubic yard depending on moisture. Our caliche-heavy ground in Pueblo West is on the heavy end.
  • A typical half-ton pickup has a payload rating of about 1,500–2,000 lbs, so it can safely carry only half to three-quarters of a cubic yard of broken concrete per trip.

That's why removing a 12×20 ft patio slab at 4 inches thick is a bigger job than it looks. The slab is about 3 cubic yards of concrete, which is roughly 12,000 lbs of material. Hauling that in a personal pickup takes 6–8 trips and puts a lot of wear on your suspension. It also explains why a lot of dumpster companies limit heavy debris to a 10-yard container filled only halfway. A full 10-yard dumpster of concrete would weigh 12+ tons, which is more than the truck's legal limit.

What It Typically Costs in Pueblo West

Prices change with access, volume, and how well the material is sorted. These are realistic Front Range ranges for budgeting:

  • Heavy-debris dumpster (10-yard, concrete/dirt only): about $350–$650 for a 7-day rental. Overweight fees apply if you go over the limit.
  • Skid steer rental to break up and load material yourself: about $250–$400 per day, plus delivery and attachments.
  • Full-service removal, where a crew breaks, loads, and hauls: usually priced by volume and weight. A small patio or walkway generally lands in the few-hundred-dollar range, and a full driveway runs higher. Ask for a written quote based on square footage and thickness.

Check for Asbestos and Lead Before You Demolish

Clean debris has to actually be clean. In Colorado that comes down to two rules homeowners regularly miss:

  • Asbestos (Colorado Regulation No. 8): On a single-family home, a CDPHE-certified asbestos inspection is required before you disturb more than 32 square feet, 50 linear feet, or one 55-gallon drum's volume of suspect material. Tearing down an entire structure usually needs an inspection and a CDPHE demolition notification no matter how much material is involved. Old floor tile, popcorn ceilings, some stucco, and pre-1980s pipe wrap are the usual suspects.
  • Lead paint: Homes built before 1978 fall under the federal EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting rule. The rule applies to contractors disturbing more than 6 square feet of interior or 20 square feet of exterior painted surface. Painted block or brick from these homes isn't "clean" in any useful sense.

Also check with the Pueblo Regional Building Department about whether your demolition needs a permit. Most full structure removals do. Sheds and small outbuildings may not, but confirm first. When Hiring Us Isn't Worth It

Sometimes paying for removal is the wrong call. Here are the situations where you should skip it:

  • Your project is in Illinois. We serve Pueblo West and the Pueblo area only. We can't haul to an Illinois CCDD site, we don't know local Illinois facility rules, and we can't sign Illinois LPC forms. Hire an Illinois contractor.
  • You have a small amount. A few broken pavers, one old fence footing, or less than about half a cubic yard of concrete fits in your own pickup in one trip. Hauling it yourself to a transfer station will almost always cost less than our minimum charge.
  • Your contractor already includes haul-off. Many remodeling and concrete bids include debris removal. Read the bid before you pay twice.
  • You can keep the material on site. On a 1-to-5-acre Pueblo West lot, clean broken concrete can often become a retaining edge, a drainage swale lining, or backfill. Clean soil can be used to fill a low spot. Free reuse costs less than any hauling. Our [dirt work and excavation](/services/dirt-work-excavation-pueblo) crew can regrade it if needed, but sometimes all you need is a rented skid steer and a weekend.
  • You have a large volume of clean dirt. If you're moving 20+ cubic yards of uncontaminated soil, list it as free clean fill first. Pueblo West regularly has property owners looking for fill, and a dump-truck operator might take it for less than a junk removal crew would charge.
  • The material is contaminated. Asbestos-containing material, lead-painted debris, and soil soaked with oil or fuel all need licensed abatement or special handling. We don't take them, and no honest junk hauler should put them in a regular load. If someone offers to, that's a reason not to hire them.
  • You need it gone today and haven't sorted anything. We'll still take mixed loads, but if you won't separate concrete from wood and drywall, the whole load is priced as mixed waste and you lose most of the savings described above.

A Simple Sorting Plan That Saves Money

If you're doing demolition yourself before calling a hauler, keep three separate piles:

1. Clean inert: concrete with rebar cut flush, brick, rock, asphalt, and clean dirt. 2. Mixed C&D: wood, drywall, shingles, fixtures, and trim. 3. Hold for special handling: anything that might contain asbestos or lead paint, plus fuel cans, paint, and chemicals.

Take photos of each pile before you ask for a quote. A photo with something for scale, like a 5-gallon bucket or a tape measure, makes a remote estimate much more accurate.
