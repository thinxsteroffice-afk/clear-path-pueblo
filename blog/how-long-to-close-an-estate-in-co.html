HOW LONG TO CLOSE AN ESTATE IN CO

Most estates in Pueblo West close in six to twelve months. That's the realistic window for an uncontested Colorado informal probate where the heirs agree, the will is clean, and there's a house to sell. The hard floor is about six months, because Colorado gives creditors four months from the first published notice to file claims, and you can't safely distribute before that clock runs out. Contested wills, missing heirs, or a property that won't sell push it to 18 to 36 months. If the estate qualifies for a small estate affidavit — gross value under roughly $84,000 in 2025, no real property — you can be done in two to four weeks with no court filing at all.

Below is what actually eats the time, and where a junk removal company like ours does and doesn't belong in that timeline.

The Colorado Probate Clock, Step by Step

Pueblo County estates are filed with the District Court in the 10th Judicial District on North Elizabeth Street in Pueblo. Here's the sequence and the real timing on each piece:

  • Death to appointment: You cannot be appointed personal representative until 120 hours (5 days) after death. In practice, filing the application plus the will and getting Letters issued takes 1 to 3 weeks in Pueblo County for informal probate. Filing fee for a decedent's estate is $199, and certified copies of your Letters run about $20 each — order three or four up front, because every bank and title company wants its own.
  • Notice to interested persons: Within 30 days of appointment you must give written notice of your appointment to the heirs and devisees.
  • Notice to creditors: Publication runs once a week for three successive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation in Pueblo County. The four-month claim period starts at first publication, not at death. Known creditors get actual mailed notice.
  • Inventory: Due within three months of appointment to any interested person who requests it.
  • Taxes: The decedent's final Form 1040 is due April 15 of the year following death. If the estate earns more than $600 of gross income, it also files a Form 1041. Colorado imposes no state estate or inheritance tax, and the federal exemption is high enough — $13.99 million in 2025, rising to $15 million in 2026 — that virtually no Pueblo West estate owes estate tax.
  • Closing: Most Colorado estates close informally with a sworn closing statement. The PR's appointment then terminates one year after that statement is filed, which is why your file may look "open" long after the work is done.

Two outer limits worth knowing: claims are barred one year after death regardless of whether notice was ever published, and you generally have three years from the date of death to open probate at all.

Why the House Is Almost Always the Critical Path

The court calendar is rarely what holds an estate open past month six. The house is.

A typical Pueblo West property sits on about one acre, often with a detached shop, a shed or two, a gravel RV pad, and fifteen years of things that got moved outside because the garage was full. Add a well permit or a septic system on many parcels, plus Pueblo West Metropolitan District water and sewer accounts to transfer or close.

The sequence that costs families the most time looks like this: Letters issued in week three, nobody touches the house until the siblings can coordinate a weekend in month four, the cleanout drags across six weekends, the septic inspection comes back needing work in month seven, the house finally lists in month eight, and a 30 to 45 day closing puts the sale in month nine or ten. The four-month creditor period expired five months earlier and was never the bottleneck.

> The probate clock and the property clock run at the same time. Families who waste the four-month creditor window close in seven months. Families who wait for it to end before touching the house close in twelve.

Meanwhile the estate keeps paying. Pueblo County property taxes come due in two halves on February 28 and June 15 (or in full by April 30), and vacant-home insurance, Pueblo West Metro District service, and utilities keep draining the estate account every month the property sits.

Do Not Hire Us Before You Have Letters

This is the mistake we see most, and it's the reason we sometimes turn down a job and tell someone to call back in three weeks.

Before Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration are issued, no one has legal authority to dispose of the decedent's property. A well-meaning adult child who clears the house in week two has, technically, distributed estate assets without authority. If a sibling later disputes the will, or a creditor files a claim that the estate can't cover, that person can be personally on the hook for the value of what left the property. We have watched this turn into an 18-month fight over a disputed $4,000 in tools.

Order of operations that keeps you out of trouble:

1. Get appointed. Hold the Letters in your hand. 2. Walk the property with a list. Photograph every room before anything moves. 3. Get an appraisal or at least a written opinion on anything plausibly worth over $500 — firearms, coins, tools, tractors, jewelry, older furniture. 4. Let heirs claim what the will gives them, in writing. 5. *Then* clear the rest.

When Hiring Us Is the Wrong Call

Plainly, because a page that only sells is useless to a personal representative trying to make a real decision:

  • The estate has no cash yet. If the bank accounts are frozen and the house hasn't sold, you'd be paying us out of your own pocket and seeking reimbursement later. That reimbursement is usually allowable, but if the estate turns out to be insolvent, you may never see it. Wait, or get the court and the heirs on board in writing first.
  • The contents are worth more than the removal. If the house is full of mid-century furniture, a decent tool collection, or farm equipment, call an estate sale company or auctioneer first. A good Pueblo-area estate sale can net $3,000 to $15,000 on a house we'd charge $2,000 to empty. Hire us for the leftovers afterward — that's typically a $500 to $900 job instead of a $2,500 one.
  • It's one or two pickup loads and you have a truck and a free Saturday. Pueblo County disposal is cheap enough that two trips to the transfer station will beat our minimum. We'd rather tell you that than take $200 for forty minutes of work.
  • The buyer is an investor taking it as-is. Plenty of Pueblo West properties sell to cash buyers who price in the cleanout themselves. If you're getting the same net either way, skip us. Ask the agent to run the numbers both ways before you spend estate funds.
  • The heirs are not in agreement. We will not clear a property while two siblings are telling us opposite things. Get a signed authorization from the PR, or a court order, or don't call yet.
  • It's a biohazard situation. Animal waste, serious mold, needles, or decomposition needs remediation specialists with the right certifications and insurance — not a junk crew. Limitations You Should Hear From Us, Not Learn Later

We are a removal crew. We are not appraisers, and we will not tell you what your father's tools were worth — if something looked valuable and you told us to haul it, it's gone. Take photographs and inventory first; that record also protects you from an heir's accusation later.

We do not handle paint, solvents, pesticides, propane tanks, or oil — common finds in Pueblo West shops. Those go to Pueblo County household hazardous waste collection, and you'll need to plan a separate trip.

We can't give legal or tax advice. The timeline above is general Colorado law, not advice about your estate, and statutory dollar thresholds like the small estate figure are indexed and change annually. A Pueblo probate attorney running an informal estate typically bills $2,500 to $6,000 total, which is money well spent on anything with real property or more than two heirs.

And storage units: if the decedent had one, the clock there is tighter than probate. Budget nine months and be pleasantly surprised at seven. Use the four-month creditor window to inventory, clear, repair, and list the property rather than waiting it out. Keep every receipt. Expect roughly $199 in court fees, $100 to $200 in publication costs, $2,500 to $6,000 in attorney fees if you use one, and $1,200 to $3,500 to empty a one-acre property with outbuildings.
