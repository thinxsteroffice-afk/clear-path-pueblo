CAUSES OF HOARDING AND CLUTTERING

Hoarding and cluttering usually come from a mix of brain-based, genetic, and life-event factors. Laziness and messiness are not the cause. Hoarding disorder affects an estimated 2–6% of adults. It runs strongly in families, and twin studies attribute roughly 50% of the risk to genetics. It is closely tied to anxiety, depression, ADHD, and trouble with decision-making. Symptoms often begin in the teen years and get worse with age. Everyday cluttering is more often driven by life transitions such as a death, divorce, or job loss, along with ADHD-style disorganization and plain lack of time. Knowing which one you're dealing with changes what will help, and it changes whether hauling everything away will help at all.

Hoarding vs. Cluttering: Why the Difference Matters

People use the two words interchangeably. Clinicians don't, and you shouldn't either. The difference decides whether a cleanout works.

Hoarding disorder became its own diagnosis in the DSM-5 in May 2013. Before that it was filed under obsessive-compulsive disorder. The core features are:

  • Ongoing difficulty throwing away or parting with possessions, whatever their actual value
  • Real distress at the thought of discarding things, beyond mild reluctance
  • Accumulation that blocks rooms from being used as intended, such as a kitchen you can't cook in or a bed you can't sleep in
  • Excessive acquisition (buying, collecting free items, picking things up) in about 80–90% of cases

Cluttering or chronic disorganization looks similar from the doorway. Underneath, the person can usually let things go once they have the time, help, or energy. The problem is a backlog. The attachment that defines hoarding isn't there.

One quick test: ask the person whether you can throw out a stack of old newspapers. Someone who clutters will usually say "Finally, yes." Someone with hoarding disorder may get visibly anxious, want to check every page, or say no.

The Real Causes of Hoarding

### Genetics and family history

Hoarding clusters in families more than almost any other behavior we know of. Roughly 50% of people who hoard report a close relative who also hoards. A large twin study from the UK attributed about half of the variation in hoarding behavior to genetic factors. That doesn't mean it's inevitable. It means a child who grew up in a hoarded home isn't simply copying a bad example. Some of the vulnerability may be inherited.

### Differences in how the brain makes decisions

Brain imaging research has found unusual activity in regions involved in decision-making and in judging emotional significance, especially when people with hoarding disorder are asked to decide whether to keep or discard their own belongings. Deciding to throw something away feels much harder and more draining for them. Many describe each object as a decision they can't afford to get wrong. "What if I need it?" and "What if I lose the memory?" can feel like real threats.

### Co-occurring mental health conditions

Hoarding rarely shows up alone. About 75% of people with hoarding disorder also have a mood or anxiety disorder, most often major depression, social anxiety, or generalized anxiety. About 20% also meet criteria for OCD. ADHD, especially the inattentive type, is common too. It's associated with trouble organizing, finishing tasks, and staying with a sorting job long enough to make progress.

### Age and the slow build

Hoarding usually isn't sudden. Symptoms first appear around ages 11–15 on average. By the mid-20s they start interfering with daily life, and they tend to get worse with each decade. One population study found hoarding was nearly three times more common in adults aged 55–94 than in adults aged 34–44. That's why so many of the homes we're called to belong to people in their 60s, 70s, and 80s. Forty or fifty years of accumulation adds up.

### Trauma and loss

Stressful or traumatic events, such as the death of a spouse, a house fire, divorce, or childhood deprivation, often come before hoarding gets worse. Researchers are cautious here: trauma seems to speed up hoarding in people who were already prone to it more than it creates hoarding from nothing. The pattern families often describe is "Mom always kept things, but after Dad died in 2019 it got out of control."

What Causes Ordinary Cluttering

Most cluttered homes in Pueblo West aren't hoarding cases. The usual drivers are:

  • Life transitions: inheriting a parent's belongings, merging two households, or downsizing from a larger home
  • Depression and burnout: tidying is often the first thing to go when energy runs low
  • ADHD: "out of sight, out of mind" leads to piles that stay visible because putting things away means forgetting them
  • Space that hides the problem: larger lots, outbuildings, and detached garages let clutter spread for years before anyone notices
  • "Someday" projects: the truck that will get fixed, the lumber for a deck, the furniture that will get refinished

That point about space shows up constantly locally. Pueblo West's larger properties make it easy for accumulation to move outdoors into sheds, carports, and side yards. A house can look fine inside while the property has a dozen junk vehicles, appliances, and scrap piles around it. Outdoor accumulation is often what draws a county nuisance complaint before the family ever realizes there's a problem.

> Clutter is usually a backlog problem. Hoarding is usually a decision-making and distress problem. A truck fixes the first. It rarely fixes the second by itself.

How Serious Is It? A Practical Severity Check

Clinicians often use the Clutter Image Rating (CIR), a set of 9 photos per room ranging from 1 (clear) to 9 (packed floor to ceiling). A rating of 4 or higher in any main living area generally signals clinically significant clutter. The images are free online through the International OCD Foundation, and they're more objective than arguing over whether a room is "that bad."

Safety stakes rise quickly at the high end. A study of Melbourne, Australia fire records found that hoarding-related fires were about 0.25% of residential fires but accounted for 24% of preventable fire deaths. Blocked exits, combustible piles near heaters, and firefighters who can't get through doorways all make these fires deadlier. Rural and semi-rural areas, where emergency response may take longer, have even less room for error.

When a Cleanout ISN'T the Answer

We'd rather lose a job than sell you something that won't work. Here's where hiring a junk removal crew, including us, is the wrong move or isn't enough:

1. The person hasn't agreed to it. This is the biggest failure mode. When family members clear out a hoarded home while the owner is in the hospital or away for the weekend, the space often fills back up, sometimes within months. The owner also experiences the cleanout as a violation, which can damage trust and make later help harder. Clinical guidance is clear that forced cleanouts don't treat hoarding. If your parent is saying no, the next call should be to a therapist, their doctor, or Pueblo County Adult Protective Services if there's real danger. A hauler isn't the right first call.

2. You expect the cleanout to be the treatment. Treatment for hoarding is real work. The best-studied approach is a specialized form of cognitive behavioral therapy, usually around 26 sessions over 6–12 months, including in-home sessions. Even with good therapy, studies show only about a quarter to a third of people reach the point where their symptoms are no longer clinically significant. A cleanout can make the home safe enough to live in. It won't change the thinking that filled it.

3. There's biohazard involved. Animal hoarding, large amounts of rodent droppings or human waste, needles, or significant mold need a certified biohazard or remediation contractor. A standard junk removal crew isn't the right fit, so get the right people in first. If you're unsure, describe the conditions honestly when you call, and we'll tell you whether it's ours or someone else's job.

4. It's light clutter and you have help. If your rooms rate a 2 or 3 on the CIR scale and you have a weekend, a pickup, and a couple of friends, you may not need us at all. Donate what's usable, haul the rest to the landfill yourself, and keep your money.

5. You're selling the house as-is anyway. Investors in the Pueblo market regularly buy homes in as-is condition, contents included. If the plan is a quick cash sale, compare the offer with and without a cleanout before paying for one. Sometimes the price difference doesn't cover what the cleanout costs.

6. The person is in crisis. If someone is talking about self-harm or isn't safe at home, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or Colorado Crisis Services. Belongings can wait.

When Professional Removal Does Make Sense

A cleanout is the right tool when:

  • The owner has agreed, ideally with a therapist or a trusted family member involved in the decisions
  • The home has to be made safe to prevent eviction, a code violation, or an APS case
  • A loved one has died and the family is handling the estate. In that case an [estate cleanout](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) clears the home without the emotional weight of the owner watching
  • The volume is simply too much for a family to haul themselves, which is often the case once you're past 15–20 cubic yards

Done well, a [hoarding cleanout](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) goes at the owner's pace. Items are sorted into keep, donate, and haul. Paperwork, photos, cash, and jewelry get set aside, and these turn up in hoarded homes more often than you'd expect. Nothing leaves without a decision. Large jobs often take 2–5 days instead of a single afternoon, and the slower pace is intentional.

How to Start the Conversation

If you're worried about someone, a few approaches tend to go better than an ultimatum:

  • Talk about safety, not belongings. "I'm worried you can't get out if there's a fire" lands better than "You need to get rid of this junk."
  • Don't touch their things without asking. Even well-meant tidying can end the conversation.
  • Start with one small, clear goal. A clear path to the back door, or a usable bathroom.
  • Bring in a professional early. The International OCD Foundation keeps a directory of clinicians trained in hoarding treatment.
