HOW OFTEN TO CLEAN YOUR HOT TUB

Clean your hot tub on a three-layer schedule: wipe the waterline weekly, rinse the filters every two weeks, and do a full drain, shell scrub, and refill every 60 to 90 days. In Pueblo West, lean toward the 60-day end rather than 90 — our hard water and high evaporation rate concentrate minerals faster than the manufacturer's manual assumes. If you mean the bathtub in your bathroom, that's a weekly wipe-down with a 10-minute scale soak every two to three weeks, which we cover further down. The one-line version: a 400-gallon tub used by two people daily needs draining about every 66 days, and skipping that is what turns a $25 chemical problem into a $900 plumbing repair.

The Drain Formula Nobody Tells You

The real schedule isn't a calendar date, it's a ratio. Divide your tub's gallon capacity by three times the number of people who soak in it per day. That's your days between full drains.

  • 400 gallons, 2 bathers per day: 400 ÷ 6 = 66 days
  • 350 gallons, 4 bathers per day: 350 ÷ 12 = 29 days
  • 450 gallons, 1 bather every other day: 450 ÷ 1.5 = 300 days — but cap it at 120 days regardless, because water chemistry degrades on its own

Most residential tubs in Pueblo West hold 350 to 450 gallons. The "three gallons per bather" number accounts for the dissolved solids a human body adds: sweat, skin oils, lotion, deodorant, laundry detergent residue from swimsuits. None of that filters out. It accumulates until the water stops holding sanitizer no matter how much chlorine or bromine you dump in.

Here's where Pueblo West changes the math. In July, an uncovered or loosely covered tub at our elevation loses 1 to 3 inches of water per week to evaporation — dry air, 300-plus sunny days a year, and strong UV at 4,800 to 5,000 feet. Every top-off adds fresh minerals while the old ones stay behind. Calcium hardness climbs on a ratchet. A tub that would run 90 clean days in Portland runs closer to 60 here.

What Each Cleaning Layer Actually Involves

Weekly (about 2 minutes). Wipe the waterline with a clean microfiber cloth — no household cleaner, the surfactants cause foaming. That gray ring is body oil and it's the seed stock for biofilm.

Every 2 weeks (10 minutes). Pull the filters, spray between every pleat with a hose nozzle. A clogged filter drops flow enough that the heater works harder, which you pay for in kilowatt-hours.

Every 3 months (overnight). Soak filters in a dedicated filter cleaner for 8 to 12 hours, rinse, and dry fully before reinstalling. Buy a second set so the tub isn't down. Replace filters every 12 to 18 months — a set runs $40 to $120.

Every 60 to 90 days (3 to 4 hours of real work). This is the one that matters:

  • Add a plumbing purge product and run the jets for 20 to 30 minutes before draining. This is the step 80% of owners skip, and it's the difference between cleaning a tub and cleaning a shell.
  • Drain fully. Pull the drain plug on the pump if your model has one.
  • Scrub the shell with a non-abrasive pad. Remove the cover and clean its underside — mildew lives there.
  • Refill, then balance in this order: calcium hardness to 150 to 250 ppm, total alkalinity to 80 to 120 ppm, pH to 7.4 to 7.6, then sanitizer.

> Purge the plumbing before you drain, not after. You can scrub a shell spotless and still be soaking in water that just ran through forty feet of biofilm-coated pipe.

The Pueblo West Water Problem

Pueblo West Metropolitan District water comes off the Arkansas River system and tests in the moderately hard range — generally 120 to 190 ppm total hardness, roughly 7 to 11 grains per gallon. Check the district's current annual water quality report for your exact number, because it shifts with source blending.

What that means for your tub: you start a fresh fill already most of the way to your calcium hardness target. Add three months of evaporation top-offs and you'll see scale on the waterline, on the heater element, and inside the jet housings. Scale on a heater element is the quiet killer — it insulates the element from the water, so the heater runs longer to hit the same temperature and eventually trips on high-limit or burns out.

Two local adjustments worth making:

  • Fill through a pre-filter hose attachment ($30 to $50, good for several fills). It knocks hardness and metals down enough that your chemical balancing takes half as long.
  • Check calcium hardness at every 30-day mark, not just at fill. If it's climbing past 400 ppm, drain early — scale removal is harder than scale prevention.

Your cover is also on a Pueblo West clock. UV at this elevation chews vinyl. Covers that last 5 to 7 years in milder climates typically last 3 to 5 years here, and a waterlogged cover is both a heat-loss problem and a mold problem. Replacement runs $350 to $700.

And watch the freeze window. Pueblo West regularly sees nights below 20°F from late November through February. A tub drained and left sitting, or a tub with a failed heater, can freeze and split plumbing in a single night. Never drain in winter unless you're refilling the same day or doing a full winterization with the lines blown out.

What It Costs to Keep One Running

Numbers for a typical Pueblo West setup:

  • Refill water: a 400-gallon fill is well under $5 of metered water at district volumetric rates.
  • Reheating: raising 400 gallons from roughly 55°F tap water to 102°F is about a 47-degree rise — around 46 kWh. At Black Hills Energy residential rates, call it $6 to $8 per drain. A standard 4 kW heater takes 12 to 16 hours to get there.
  • Chemicals: $20 to $40 per month for sanitizer, balancers, and shock.
  • Electricity to run it: $30 to $60 per month for a well-insulated tub with a tight cover. $75 to $120 or more for an older tub with compressed foam and a soggy cover, January being the worst month.
  • Annual all-in: $600 to $1,800, before any repairs.

Against that, a replacement tub installed runs $6,000 to $12,000. Which is exactly why we're about to argue against our own service.

When Cleaning Won't Fix It — And When Removal Isn't Worth Paying For

We remove hot tubs. That's a service we sell. Here's the honest accounting.

If your tub works and you use it twice a week or more, don't call us. Maintenance at $600 to $1,800 a year is cheap next to replacement. Follow the schedule above and the tub will outlast the cleaning argument.

Cleaning cannot fix these, so stop spending money trying:

  • Foam that returns within 48 hours of a full drain and purge. You have entrenched biofilm in the plumbing or saturated foam insulation. A second purge is worth trying once. A third isn't.
  • A cracked or delaminated acrylic shell. Hairline spider cracks around a jet mean the substrate is failing. Patch kits are cosmetic.
  • A seized pump or dead circuit board on a tub older than 15 years. Parts for discontinued models run $400 to $900 and often aren't available at all.
  • Water loss with no visible leak. That's usually a leak under the foam. Diagnosis alone means cutting insulation out, and that's a $300 to $600 service call before any repair.
  • A tub that smells like mildew with the cover off and the water fresh. The cabinet or foam is holding moisture. You're not cleaning that out.

And If You Meant the Bathtub

Fair enough — the phrasing goes both ways.

  • Weekly: wipe and rinse. Once a week is enough for a tub used for showers.
  • After every use: a quick rinse and squeegee of the walls. In our hard water, standing droplets leave spots in under an hour.
  • Every 2 to 3 weeks: a scale soak. Equal parts white vinegar and warm water, spray the tub, wait 20 to 30 minutes, scrub, rinse. Pueblo West hardness makes this non-optional — skip it for two months and you'll be looking at a chalky ring that needs an acid cleaner.
  • Quarterly: pull and clean the drain and overflow cover. Hair and soap scum there cause slow drains people blame on the main line.
  • Never: abrasive powder on acrylic or fiberglass. It micro-scratches the surface, and scratched surfaces hold grime permanently, which makes everything worse from then on.

If you're replacing the tub rather than cleaning it, know what you're lifting. A cast iron tub weighs 250 to 400 pounds and needs two or three people plus a plan for the stairs. Acrylic runs 60 to 70 pounds and one person can manage it. Either way it becomes construction debris, not curbside trash.

Pulling It Together

Weekly wipe, biweekly filter rinse, quarterly filter soak, and a full drain every 60 to 90 days using the gallons-divided-by-three-times-bathers rule. In Pueblo West, shorten the drain interval, fill through a pre-filter, watch calcium hardness, and plan on a new cover every 3 to 5 years instead of every 7.

Then be honest about the tub's condition. A clean schedule keeps a good tub good for a decade. It doesn't resurrect one with a cracked shell, soaked foam, or a dead pump — and continuing to buy chemicals for a tub nobody soaks in is the most expensive version of this whole problem.
