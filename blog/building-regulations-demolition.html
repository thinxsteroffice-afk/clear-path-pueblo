BUILDING REGULATIONS & DEMOLITION

Yes — building regulations apply to demolition, and in Pueblo West they apply in more ways than most property owners expect. The working rule in unincorporated Pueblo County is simple: if the structure needed a permit to build, it needs a permit to tear down. That covers houses, garages, additions, carports, large sheds, and most accessory buildings over the exempt size threshold. On top of the county's demolition permit, Colorado state law layers on an asbestos inspection requirement enforced by CDPHE that applies whether or not a county permit is required, plus utility disconnects, Colorado 811 locates, septic abandonment rules, and — unique to Pueblo West — Metropolitan District covenant review. Small, non-permitted structures like a 10x8 shed or a backyard deck usually skip the permit, but never skip the utility and asbestos steps.

Who actually regulates demolition in Pueblo West

Pueblo West is unincorporated Pueblo County, not a city. That trips people up constantly, because the regulatory map is split between four separate bodies and none of them talk to each other on your behalf:

  • Pueblo County Department of Planning and Development issues building and demolition permits for unincorporated areas. City of Pueblo rules do not apply here, and neither do Colorado Springs' Pikes Peak Regional Building Department rules — don't follow advice written for either one.
  • Pueblo West Metropolitan District controls water and sewer service and enforces the residential and commercial covenants. Removing a structure with a water tap, or leaving a lot vacant and unmaintained, can put you crossways with Covenant Control even when the county is perfectly happy.
  • CDPHE (Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment) enforces Regulation No. 8, Part B — the asbestos rules — and Regulation No. 9, which bans open burning of demolition debris statewide. You cannot burn the old shed. Not in a burn pile, not in a barrel.
  • Pueblo Department of Public Health and Environment handles on-site wastewater (septic) abandonment, which matters on the many Pueblo West acreage lots that were never connected to the sewer main.

Permit fees and thresholds change, so confirm current numbers directly with county Planning and Development before you budget. What follows are the ranges we see in practice on Pueblo West jobs.

The size thresholds that decide whether you need a permit

Most residential codes exempt one-story detached accessory structures below a square-footage cap — historically 120 square feet, raised to 200 square feet in the 2018 and later IRC editions. Which number applies depends on the code edition Pueblo County has currently adopted, so ask before you assume. A 10x12 shed is 120 square feet and sits precisely on the line; a 12x20 shed at 240 square feet is over any version of it.

Three things that are commonly misunderstood:

  • Electrical or plumbing service pulls a structure into permit territory regardless of size. A 10x10 shed with a subpanel run from the house is not an exempt structure anymore. The circuit has to be properly disconnected and, in most cases, that's permitted electrical work.
  • Decks, fences, and above-ground pools are usually exempt from demolition permits but can still trigger covenant notice in Pueblo West, and pool removal leaves an excavation that has to be backfilled and compacted, not just filled with loose dirt.
  • Concrete slabs and foundations are separate from the building above them. You can get a demo permit signed off on a garage and still have an unpermitted 600-square-foot slab sitting there. If you want it gone, that's its own scope — see [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo).

The asbestos rule is the one that bites

Colorado's Regulation 8 trigger levels are 260 linear feet on pipes, 160 square feet on other surfaces, or the equivalent of a 55-gallon drum (roughly 35 cubic feet) of asbestos-containing material. Hit any of those and you're into licensed abatement with a 10-working-day advance notification to CDPHE before demolition starts.

Here's the Pueblo West wrinkle. The community was platted in 1969 by McCulloch Oil, and the first big wave of homes went up between 1970 and 1980. That window is squarely inside the era of asbestos popcorn ceilings, 9x9 vinyl floor tile, black mastic, and cement-board siding — and inside the pre-1978 lead paint window too. A 1974 Pueblo West ranch with a detached garage is not an "old building" by Front Range standards, and owners routinely assume it's too new to matter. It isn't.

A pre-demolition asbestos inspection by a Colorado-certified Asbestos Building Inspector typically runs $350 to $800 for a residential structure in southern Colorado, with lab turnaround of 3 to 5 business days standard or 24-hour rush for a premium. If the samples come back positive above trigger levels, abatement before demolition commonly adds $3,000 to $15,000 depending on material and square footage.

> The inspection is the cheapest line item in a demolition and the one people cut. Skipping it converts a $500 known cost into an open-ended liability the moment a neighbor calls CDPHE.

Utilities, 811, and septic — the steps with real deadlines

  • Colorado 811 locates: Colorado law requires notice at least two business days before excavation. Locate marks are valid for a limited window and expire — if your project slips three weeks, you re-notify. This applies to post holes and slab breakout, not just big digs.
  • Gas and electric: Black Hills Energy serves the Pueblo area. A meter pull and service disconnect needs to be scheduled, and 5 to 15 business days of lead time is realistic. Demolishing around a live service is how people end up on the news.
  • Water and sewer: The Metropolitan District handles tap abandonment. Leaving a live water service capped under rubble is a code problem and a future buyer's inspection problem.
  • Septic abandonment: Required when a dwelling served by an on-site system comes down. The tank gets pumped by a licensed hauler — typically $350 to $600 — then crushed or filled and backfilled, under a county health department permit.
  • Manufactured homes: Colorado requires the title be purged through the County Clerk and Recorder with a Certificate of Destruction before the unit is scrapped. Skip it and the home still legally exists on the tax rolls.

What demolition debris actually costs to get rid of

Disposal is usually the largest single cost, and it's driven by weight, not volume. Mixed construction and demolition tipping fees in the Pueblo area generally run $40 to $70 per ton, and C&D debris is heavy:

  • A 4-inch concrete slab covering 120 square feet is about 1.5 cubic yards and roughly 3 tons.
  • A standard 10x12 wood frame shed with shingles runs 1,500 to 2,500 pounds.
  • A 20-yard roll-off in the Pueblo market typically rents for $375 to $500 for 7 days with a 2-to-4-ton allowance and $50 to $75 per ton in overage charges beyond it.

That overage clause is where DIY demolition budgets break. One shingle roof or one slab puts a 20-yarder over its allowance, and the invoice arrives after the dumpster is already gone. When permits and professional demolition are NOT worth it

This section costs us work, and it should.

Don't hire anyone — including us — for these. A 6x8 wood shed on skids with no power, no slab, and nothing suspect in it is a weekend with a reciprocating saw and a trailer. Same for a 50-foot stretch of wood privacy fence or a small ground-level deck. If you own a truck and have one helper, you'll do it for the cost of gas and a dump ticket. Paying $400 to $700 for a crew to do a four-hour job you can do yourself is a bad trade.

Don't hire us for these — hire someone else. We are a junk removal and light demolition company. We are not a licensed asbestos abatement contractor and we are not a structural demolition contractor. If your project is:

  • A full house or any occupied-structure teardown — that requires a licensed demolition contractor carrying demolition-specific liability coverage and the ability to pull the structural demo permit as contractor of record. Call one.
  • Any confirmed asbestos above trigger levels — that is licensed abatement work, period, and nobody can legally shortcut it.
  • Anything inside 10 feet of a shared property line with an occupied neighboring structure, or a building with known structural failure — engineering controls and sometimes an engineer's sequencing plan are warranted.
  • A commercial building with fire suppression, three-phase service, or a mezzanine — out of our lane.

Failure modes to plan around, named plainly:

  • The asbestos report comes back positive. Your timeline moves by 3 to 6 weeks and your budget moves by thousands. There is no version of this project where that risk is zero on a 1970s structure.
  • Permit review takes longer than quoted. Over-the-counter approvals happen, but 5 to 10 business days is a safer planning assumption, and incomplete applications restart the clock.
  • Stop-work orders. Getting caught demolishing without a required permit generally means a stop-work order, a retroactive permit at a multiplied fee, and in some cases a requirement to expose and re-inspect work already buried. Budget-wise, assume it at least doubles the permit cost and adds weeks.
  • Weather. Pueblo West wind regularly runs 25 to 40 mph in spring. Open demolition in those conditions scatters debris across your neighbors' lots and gets shut down, legitimately.
  • The slab you forgot. Most people budget for the building and not the foundation. Decide up front, because mobilizing equipment twice costs more than doing both in one visit — relevant if you're looking at [skid steer work](/services/skid-steer-services-pueblo).

A realistic sequence for a Pueblo West accessory-structure teardown

1. Measure the structure and confirm with Pueblo County Planning and Development whether it clears the exemption threshold. 2. Check the Pueblo West Metropolitan District covenants for your property class, especially on corner and arterial-facing lots. 3. Order the asbestos inspection on anything built before roughly 1985 with suspect materials — budget 1 week. 4. Schedule utility disconnects and call Colorado 811 at least two business days out. 5. Apply for the demo permit if required; attach the asbestos report. 6. Demolish, haul, and backfill. Keep the dump tickets — they're your proof of lawful disposal and your buyer's inspector will ask. 7. Call for final inspection if a permit was issued. An open permit on the property record is a title headache later.
