DENTAL APPLIANCE REMOVAL CODE D7997

Short answer: If you need the billing code, it's D7997, "appliance removal (not by dentist who placed appliance), includes removal of archbar." Use D8695 for taking off fixed orthodontic braces before treatment is finished, for example when a patient moves or stops paying. Normal debonding at the end of treatment usually isn't billed separately. It's part of the comprehensive ortho fee (D8070–D8090), and retainers are billed under D8680. We're a junk removal company, not a dental biller, so check these against the current CDT manual before you submit a claim. If you got here because you're a Pueblo West dental office clearing out old *physical* equipment like chairs, compressors, cabinetry or X-ray units, the rest of this page is for you.

The dental codes, in plain terms

We don't want to send you off with half an answer, so here are the codes that usually come up. The American Dental Association updates CDT every year, and changes take effect January 1. A code you used in 2024 may have a different descriptor now.

  • D7997: removal of an appliance by a dentist who did *not* place it. Arch bars are included.
  • D8695: removal of fixed orthodontic appliances for reasons other than completing treatment.
  • D8680: orthodontic retention, which covers removing appliances and making and placing retainers.
  • D8696–D8699 and D8701–D8704: repairing, re-cementing or replacing orthodontic appliances and retainers. These are split by maxillary and mandibular arch.
  • D6100: surgical removal of an implant body. This is not an "appliance" code, but people often mix it up with one.

Whether insurance pays depends on the plan. Many plans limit orthodontic benefits to patients under 19 and set a lifetime ortho maximum. Call the carrier before you assume D7997 or D8695 will be paid.

That covers the dental side. The rest of this article is about the other kind of appliance removal, the one we actually do.

When a dental office needs "appliance removal" of a different kind

Pueblo West practices remodel, merge, retire and relocate like any other business. When that happens, someone has to get a lot of heavy, awkward equipment out of a building that was designed around it. These are the jobs we see:

  • Patient chairs. A full chair with its delivery unit usually weighs 250 to 400 pounds. It's often bolted to the floor, with water, air and power lines run up through the base.
  • Compressors and vacuum pumps. Most sit in a mechanical closet and weigh 100 to 300 pounds each.
  • Built-in cabinetry and sterilization centers. Most of a remodel's volume is here. One operatory's casework can fill 2 to 4 cubic yards.
  • Break-room and lab appliances. Refrigerators, freezers, dishwashers and ice machines. Anything with refrigerant needs to go to a facility that recovers it under EPA Clean Air Act Section 608, and we handle that routing.
  • Waiting room furniture, desks, file cabinets and old computers.

For a typical 3- to 5-operatory practice in Pueblo West, a full equipment cleanout runs one to two truckloads. If everything is disconnected and staged when we arrive, the crew usually finishes in 3 to 6 hours. What a junk hauler cannot legally take from a dental office

Most dental office cleanout articles skip this part, and it's where practices get into trouble. Several common items in a dental office are regulated waste. Putting them in a junk truck can create liability for the practice owner, not only for the hauler.

> If it has a radiation registration, mercury in it, or a needle on it, it doesn't belong in a junk truck, ours or anyone else's.

X-ray units. Dental X-ray machines in Colorado are registered with the Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment (CDPHE) Radiation Program under 6 CCR 1007-1. When you get rid of one, you have to update the registration and document where the unit went. Old tube heads also contain lead shielding. Units made before about 1979 can contain dielectric oil that should be tested for PCBs. Call the CDPHE Radiation Program for the current notification requirements, and use a licensed X-ray service company or a recycler that gives you a disposal certificate. After the unit has been properly decommissioned and the tube head removed, we can haul the arm, mounting hardware and cabinetry.

Amalgam and amalgam separators. The EPA dental amalgam rule (40 CFR Part 441) took effect in 2017, and existing offices had to comply by July 14, 2020. Separators have to meet ISO 11143 and capture at least 95% of amalgam solids. Offices have to keep inspection and disposal records for 3 years. A used separator canister, a chairside trap or a container of scrap amalgam must go back through a certified amalgam recycler. Old plumbing traps under operatories can also hold decades of mercury buildup, so have a plumber or recycler deal with them before demolition starts.

Sharps and regulated medical waste. These go through your existing medical waste contract.

Nitrous oxide and oxygen cylinders. Return them to the gas supplier. Even cylinders that look empty are pressurized vessels, and landfills won't accept them.

Chemicals. Old film developer, fixer (it contains silver), disinfectants and lab solvents need hazardous waste disposal or return to the vendor.

Lead aprons and thyroid collars. These can be recycled for their lead. Don't throw them in a dumpster.

Patient records. Paper charts and old hard drives fall under HIPAA. Use a shredding service that provides a certificate of destruction. We can move sealed boxes to a shredding vendor, but we are not a certified destruction company.

When hiring us isn't worth it

We'd rather lose the job than have you overpay or end up with a compliance problem. Here are the situations where a junk removal company is the wrong call or a waste of money:

  • Your equipment still has resale value. A working chair and delivery unit less than about 10 years old can sell for real money to used-equipment dealers, and some dealers remove it for free because they plan to resell it. If your equipment is recent and working, call a dealer before you call us. Paying a hauler to take something someone else would pay you for is the most common mistake we see.
  • You only need the dental code. If you came here looking for D7997, you already have it. Don't book anything.
  • Your equipment is still connected. We don't disconnect plumbing, compressed air, vacuum lines or hardwired electrical. Hire a dental equipment technician or a licensed plumber and electrician first. That usually costs $150 to $500 per operatory, depending on how the room was built. If our crew arrives to find chairs still plumbed in, you pay for a return trip and nothing goes on the truck.
  • Most of the job is regulated waste. If the job is mostly an X-ray unit, a separator and a closet of chemicals, you need hazardous waste and radiation specialists. We would only be hauling the shelving afterward.
  • Your building has a loading dock and a dumpster contract. A 20-yard roll-off might be cheaper for a big remodel if your own staff or contractor can load it. You give up labor and same-day removal in exchange for a lower rate. That trade makes sense when you have labor available.
  • You're leaving equipment for the next tenant. Many dental buildouts in the Pueblo area are leased to another dentist with the plumbing and chairs still in place. That can be worth tens of thousands of dollars to the incoming practice. Check with your landlord and broker before you tear anything out.

A realistic timeline for a Pueblo West practice closing or remodeling

The order of operations matters more than how fast any single step goes. This sequence prevents most delays:

1. 6 to 8 weeks out: Get quotes from used-equipment dealers on anything worth selling. Contact the CDPHE Radiation Program about decommissioning your X-ray equipment. 2. 4 weeks out: Schedule an amalgam recycler, the medical waste vendor's final pickup, gas cylinder returns and records shredding. 3. 1 to 2 weeks out: Have a technician or tradespeople disconnect and cap all utilities at each operatory. 4. Final week: Book the junk removal for everything left over: furniture, cabinetry, appliances, chairs no one bought and general debris. 5. After the haul: Keep your disposal certificates, amalgam records and shredding certificates together. You'll need them if a regulator or landlord asks questions later.

If you do it in the reverse order, with the hauler first and the specialists last, you end up paying for two trips. You may also find out too late that a separator canister went somewhere it shouldn't have.
