HOW OFTEN TO DRAIN A HOT TUB

Drain and refill a residential hot tub every 3 to 4 months under average use — and in Pueblo West, closer to every 6 to 10 weeks if the tub gets used several times a week. The industry shortcut is the water replacement interval formula: tub gallons ÷ (average daily bathers × 3) = days between drains. A 400-gallon spa used by two people every day works out to 67 days. A 500-gallon tub with four daily bathers drops to 42 days. Light use — two adults, twice a week — stretches a 400-gallon tub to four or five months. The local wrinkle is that our dry air and mineral-heavy fill water push most Pueblo West spas toward the short end of every range, and the calendar is a worse guide here than a $12 test strip kit.

The formula is a starting point, not a schedule

That ÷3 math exists because each bather sheds a predictable load into the water: body oils, sweat, lotion, sunscreen, deodorant, laundry detergent residue from a swimsuit. Chlorine or bromine oxidizes most of it, but the byproducts don't leave. They dissolve, and dissolved solids accumulate until your sanitizer is fighting a losing battle against its own waste.

The measurable version of that is total dissolved solids (TDS). Fill a tub and record the TDS of the fresh water — call it 400 ppm. Once the reading climbs 1,500 ppm above that baseline, or passes about 1,500 to 2,000 ppm absolute, the water is done. You'll notice it before the meter does: chlorine demand doubles, pH won't hold, the water looks dull rather than clear, and a faint flat smell appears within an hour of shocking.

Why Pueblo West water forces a shorter interval than Denver or Dallas

Here's the part most national hot tub articles skip. At just under 5,000 feet, with afternoon humidity that routinely drops below 25% in June and July and wind that doesn't quit, evaporation off a spa is aggressive. A well-covered 400-gallon tub in Pueblo West loses roughly 1 to 3 gallons a day. With a cracked, waterlogged, or poorly seated cover, that jumps to 5 to 10 gallons a day.

Every gallon that evaporates leaves its minerals behind. Every gallon you add from the hose brings new ones in.

Run the math over a 60-day cycle at 3 gallons a day: you've added 180 gallons of fresh fill water to a 400-gallon tub and removed exactly zero minerals. That's nearly half a tub's worth of new calcium, magnesium, and dissolved solids stacked on top of what was already there. In a humid climate, that same tub might top off 40 gallons in the same period.

> Your hot tub in Pueblo West isn't just getting dirtier with use — it's concentrating. The dry air does to your spa water what a slow boil does to a stockpot.

Add the source water. Pueblo West Metro District water comes from the Arkansas River system via Pueblo Reservoir and carries real hardness; properties on the rural fringe running domestic wells often see considerably more, plus iron. Look up your Metro District Consumer Confidence Report for the actual annual hardness number rather than guessing — it's published every year and it's free. Then compare: acrylic spas want calcium hardness around 150 to 250 ppm. Above roughly 400 ppm, you get scale on the heater element, cloudy water that no amount of clarifier fixes, and white crust at the waterline.

Two things that matter locally:

  • A hose pre-filter costs $30 to $45 and screws onto your garden hose. On hard fill water it is the single highest-return $40 in spa ownership, because it keeps hardness out instead of chasing it with chemicals later.
  • If your house has a water softener, don't fill the spa from a softened tap. Softened water trades calcium for sodium, and water that soft is corrosive to heater elements and seals, plus it foams badly. Fill from an outside spigot upstream of the softener.

What a drain and refill actually costs here

The water itself is cheap. At roughly $5 per 1,000 gallons, a 400-gallon refill is about $2. The real cost is reheating.

Bringing 400 gallons from a 55°F hose temperature up to 102°F takes about 46 kWh of electricity. At $0.14 per kWh — check your own Black Hills Energy bill for your actual rate — that's $6 to $7 per refill, and 8 to 12 hours of waiting on a typical 4 to 5.5 kW heater. Refilling through a garden hose at 5 gallons per minute takes about 80 minutes.

So a full drain-and-refill cycle runs roughly $10 in water and power, plus half a day of downtime. Six cycles a year is about $60. Chasing bad water with chemicals instead — extra shock, clarifier, defoamer, scale remover, pH down by the jug — easily costs three times that and still leaves you soaking in it.

Other recurring numbers worth budgeting:

  • Filter cartridges: $30 to $80, replaced every 12 to 24 months. Rinse monthly, chemical-soak at every drain.
  • A plumbing purge product: $12 to $25 per use. Run it 20 minutes before you drain, not after you refill. Biofilm living in the jet lines is why some tubs go cloudy three days after a fresh fill.
  • Covers: $350 to $700, replaced every 3 to 5 years. UV at this elevation is brutal on vinyl, and a saturated cover doubles your evaporation and your heating bill at the same time.

Where 400 gallons of used spa water can legally and safely go

This is where Pueblo West differs sharply from in-town Pueblo, and it's the part that causes expensive damage.

  • If you're on septic, do not drain the spa into a floor drain, laundry sink, or anything tied to the leach field. A typical household puts 150 to 250 gallons a day through a septic system. Dumping 400 gallons in 25 minutes hydraulically floods the field, pushes solids out of the tank, and can cost thousands to remediate.
  • Don't drain against your foundation or into a crawlspace window well. On our expansive clay soils, repeated saturation at the foundation is a structural problem, not a landscaping one.
  • Chlorinated water kills plants. Stop sanitizer additions 48 hours before draining, let free chlorine fall below 0.5 ppm, get pH between 7.0 and 8.0, and never discharge water treated with copper-based algaecide onto soil at all.
  • Keep it out of the street and storm drains. Spread it across gravel, xeriscape, or an unplanted area well away from the house, and move the hose every 10 minutes instead of flooding one spot.
  • A submersible pump rents for about $25 a day and empties a 400-gallon tub in 10 to 15 minutes versus 45+ on the drain valve. Worth it if you're draining six times a year.

When draining more often is a waste of money — and when the tub isn't worth saving

We haul hot tubs for a living, so the honest part of this page works against us. Read it anyway.

Draining more often than every 6 weeks is almost always solving the wrong problem. If you're dumping water monthly because it keeps going cloudy, the culprit is usually a filter past its service life, a biofilm colony in the plumbing, or total alkalinity sitting at 40 ppm so pH swings with every use. Fresh water masks all three for about five days. A $45 filter and a $20 purge fix what twelve drains a year won't.

Draining has a real downside at altitude in winter. Pueblo West January average lows run near 16°F. An empty or half-empty spa with power off can freeze and split plumbing in 24 to 48 hours. If you're draining between November and March, do it on a day forecast above 40°F, have the new water going in within the hour, and never walk away from a half-drained tub overnight. If the tub is going unused all winter, a proper full winterization — blow out every line, pull the drain plugs on both pumps, vacuum the heater — is the move, not a casual drain.

Who should not call us: if your tub is under 10 years old, holds temperature, and the only complaint is water quality, you do not have a removal problem. You have a $60 problem. Same if the fix is a heater element at $200 to $450 or a circulation pump at $300 to $600 on an otherwise sound cabinet. And if it's a clean, working, name-brand spa, list it — functional used tubs move locally for $500 to $2,500. Paying someone to haul away an asset is a bad trade.

When removal genuinely is the right call: a cracked acrylic shell, a foam core saturated with water, a failed control pack quoted at $700 to $1,200 on a 15-year-old tub, or a spa that's been sitting empty and frozen for two or more winters. Dry weight runs 350 to 1,000 pounds; full of water it's 2,500 to 4,000 pounds, which is why "I'll just get four friends and a trailer" ends in a trip to the ER or a gouged deck. The quick-reference schedule for a Pueblo West spa

  • Weekly: check sanitizer and pH, rinse the filter if use is heavy, top off evaporation loss.
  • Monthly: test calcium hardness and TDS, deep-rinse the filter, inspect the cover for waterlogging.
  • Every 6 to 10 weeks (regular use) or 3 to 4 months (light use): purge the lines, drain, wipe the shell, refill through a pre-filter, rebalance.
  • Trigger an early drain regardless of date when: TDS reads 1,500 ppm over your fill baseline, chlorine demand doubles, foam persists after defoamer, or hardness clears 400 ppm.
  • Annually: replace the filter, check jet bearings and cover seams, and photograph the equipment bay label so you have the model number when something fails.

Keep records. Three lines in a notebook by the tub — date, TDS, hardness — will tell you your actual interval within two cycles, and that beats any formula on the internet including the one at the top of this page.
