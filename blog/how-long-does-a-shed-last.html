HOW LONG DOES A SHED LAST?

A shed lasts 10 to 30 years, and in Pueblo West the honest range is narrower than the national numbers suggest. A thin-gauge steel kit shed from a big-box store gives you 7–12 years here before the base rusts through and the door track racks. A resin/plastic shed runs 10–15 years before UV makes the panels chalky and brittle. A wood-framed shed with T1-11 siding on a decent foundation goes 20–25 years. A cedar or metal-on-slab building with real trusses will hit 35–50. The single biggest variable isn't the walls — it's the roof and the thing it's sitting on. At 4,700 feet with 12 inches of annual rain and 50 mph spring gusts, our conditions push cheap sheds toward the low end and barely touch good ones.

Why Pueblo West Eats Sheds Faster Than the Box Says

The warranty card on a kit shed was written for Ohio. Here's what actually changes the math at our elevation:

  • UV load. Pueblo County sees roughly 245–285 sunny days a year, and UV intensity climbs about 5–6% per 1,000 feet of elevation. At Pueblo West's ~4,700 feet, your shed is absorbing close to 25% more UV than the same shed in a coastal town. Unstabilized polyethylene and vinyl trim are the first casualties — expect visible chalking and color fade by year 5–7.
  • Wind. Spring and early-summer gusts of 45–60 mph are routine out here, and Pueblo has recorded gusts above 70 mph. Most kit sheds are rated to 70–90 mph *only when anchored to the manufacturer's spec*. Set on bare dirt or loose gravel with four auger anchors, that rating is fiction.
  • Hail. Colorado ranks second in the country for hail claims. A 20-year 3-tab shingle roof on a shed realistically gets replaced at 12–15 years here, and plastic skylight panels crack on the first 1-inch stone.
  • Dry-wet-dry cycling. With 12 inches of precipitation and 30 inches of snow annually, wood doesn't rot the way it does in humid climates — it checks, splits, and pulls fasteners loose. Screws back out. Door jambs twist 1/4 to 1/2 inch out of square.
  • Expansive soil. Parts of Pueblo County sit on clay with real shrink-swell potential. A 4-inch unreinforced slab can heave 1–2 inches at a corner over a decade, and once the floor isn't flat, the doors never close right again.

> Sheds don't die from the walls in. They die from the roof down and the foundation up — and almost nobody budgets for either one.

Realistic Lifespan by Shed Type

Figures below assume Pueblo West conditions, average maintenance, and a shed that gets opened a few times a week.

  • Thin-gauge steel kit (Arrow-style, 26–30 gauge): 7–12 years. Failure point is the bottom 4 inches of panel, where splash and snowmelt sit. Once that perforates, there's nothing left to fasten to.
  • Resin/plastic (Lifetime, Keter, Rubbermaid): 10–18 years. The better double-wall resin with HDPE and UV inhibitors genuinely hits 15+. The $400 thin-wall units don't — expect 8–10 and a roof panel that cracks in a hailstorm.
  • Wood frame, OSB/T1-11 siding, asphalt roof: 18–25 years. You'll replace the roof once at year 12–15 for $600–$1,500 and that purchase buys you the second decade.
  • Wood frame, LP SmartSide or fiber cement: 25–35 years. Noticeably better here than OSB because it doesn't swell at cut edges.
  • Cedar/redwood board-and-batten: 30–40 years with a re-stain every 4–6 years at roughly $150–$350 in materials.
  • Steel or pole-barn building on a reinforced slab: 40–50+ years. The slab outlives everything.

The Five-Minute Test: Repair, or Done?

Walk out and check these in order. You're trying to separate cosmetic problems from structural ones.

1. Push the top corner of a wall sideways. If the whole building flexes more than an inch, the racking resistance is gone. That's structural. 2. Look at the bottom plate or base rail. Soft wood you can push a screwdriver into, or rust holes you can see daylight through, means the thing is no longer attached to anything. 3. Check the roof deck from inside on a bright day. Pinholes of light, sagging between rafters, or dark water staining on more than 20% of the sheathing means a full re-roof, not a patch. 4. Shut the doors. A 1/2-inch gap at the top that wasn't there five years ago is usually foundation movement. 5. Smell it. Musty plus visible black staining on the lower 12 inches of interior wall means moisture has been sitting there for seasons.

Two or more structural hits and you're putting money into a building with maybe three years left. One cosmetic hit and you should fix it.

What Extends a Shed's Life (Cheap Stuff That Actually Works)

  • Get it off the dirt. Four to six concrete blocks or a gravel pad with 4 inches of compacted 3/4-inch road base costs $120–$300 and routinely adds 5–8 years to a wood shed.
  • Anchor it properly. Real auger anchors or wedge anchors into concrete: $40–$90. This is the difference between a shed and a kite.
  • Re-caulk the roof-to-wall seams every 3 years. One $9 tube of polyurethane sealant.
  • Keep sprinklers off it. A rotor head hitting the same wall 15 minutes a day, 150 days a year, will destroy OSB siding in under a decade.
  • Add a ridge vent or two gable vents. Attic temps in a sealed shed hit 140–150°F in July here, which cooks shingles and warps resin panels. Venting costs under $75.

When Removal Is NOT Worth It — and Who Should Skip Us Entirely

This is the part most junk removal pages leave out, so here it is plainly. We'd rather you call us once for the right job than once for the wrong one.

  • If the only problem is the roof, don't demolish. A 10x12 shed re-roof runs $600–$1,500. Paying us $450–$900 to haul it off and then $3,500–$6,000 for a new shed to solve a roof problem is a bad trade. Call a roofer.
  • If it's a small resin or steel shed and you own a truck, do it yourself. An 8x10 resin shed comes apart with a socket set and a drill in about 2 hours. It's 3 pickup loads. At a local transfer station you're looking at roughly $30–$60 in minimum tipping fees. Total: under $100. Our minimum service charge is more than that, because a crew, a truck, fuel, insurance, and disposal have a floor. If your whole job fits in your own truck bed, you don't need us.
  • If you suspect asbestos or lead, we have to walk away. Sheds and farm outbuildings built before roughly 1980 sometimes have transite (asbestos-cement) roof or wall panels and lead-based paint. That's a licensed abatement job — figure $1,500–$4,000 — and no junk removal company should touch it. We'll tell you to stop and get it tested before we quote.
  • We can't take hazardous contents. Old gas cans, half-full pesticide jugs, solvents, propane cylinders, car batteries, and oil-based paint have to go to a household hazardous waste collection, not a landfill. If your shed is 40% old chemicals, the removal gets delayed until that's handled, and you'll be doing that part yourself.
  • The slab is a separate job and a separate price. Homeowners are regularly surprised by this. Breaking and hauling a 10x12 concrete pad is heavy work — it's several thousand pounds of material and often needs equipment. That's [concrete and rock removal](/services/concrete-rock-removal-pueblo), priced on its own. If you're leaving the pad for a new shed, say so up front and the quote drops.
  • If you're selling the house in under 6 months, think twice. A functional-but-ugly shed is storage a buyer may value. An empty gravel rectangle isn't. Ask your agent before you spend money erasing a feature.
  • Hantavirus is a real consideration out here. Southeastern Colorado has deer mice. A shed with heavy rodent nesting needs wetting-down and respirator precautions before anyone disturbs the material — including you. Don't dry-sweep it on a Saturday morning.
  • We don't repair sheds. If the answer is "new door track and three sheets of siding," we're the wrong call. A handyman at $60–$95/hour will serve you better.
  • One small shed alone is an inefficient job. If you've got a shed, a dead hot tub, and a pile of branches, getting them in one trip saves real money versus three separate minimum charges. Bundle it with [yard debris removal](/services/yard-debris-removal-pueblo) or a garage cleanout if you can.

Before You Rebuild in Pueblo West

Two local items worth confirming before the new shed shows up. In Colorado, a one-story detached accessory structure of 200 square feet or less is generally exempt from a *building* permit — but exemption from a building permit is not exemption from zoning, setbacks, or easements, and Pueblo West properties also sit under Metro District covenants. Verify current setback distances and accessory-structure rules with Pueblo Regional Building Department and the Metro District before you pour anything. A shed placed 3 feet from a property line that needed 5 is a shed you'll move twice.

And spend the money on the pad, not the paint. A $400 gravel or concrete foundation under a $2,500 shed is the highest-return decision in the whole project — it's the difference between 15 years and 28.
