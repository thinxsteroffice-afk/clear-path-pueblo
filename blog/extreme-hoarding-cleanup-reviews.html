EXTREME HOARDING CLEANUP REVIEWS

If you're searching "extreme hoarding cleanup reviews" in Pueblo West, star ratings won't tell you much. What matters is what the reviews describe. Good reviews of extreme hoarding cleanups mention three things: the crew showed up on the day they said, the final price matched the quote within about 10%, and they treated the homeowner with respect when things got hard. Bad reviews usually describe the opposite: a low phone estimate that doubled on-site, a crew that threw away things the family wanted to keep, or a company that left rodent waste and soiled flooring behind. This page covers how to read those reviews, what an extreme job costs here, and when you shouldn't hire a junk crew like us at all.

What "extreme" means before you read a single review

Reviews only help if the reviewer's job looked like yours. A 5-star review for a cluttered garage says nothing about a home where you can't reach the kitchen.

Many professionals use the Clutter-Hoarding Scale from the Institute for Challenging Disorganization, which has 5 levels:

  • Level 1–2: Clutter, but doors, stairs and appliances still work. Most families can handle this themselves over 2–3 weekends with a rented dumpster.
  • Level 3: One or more rooms can't be used for their purpose, there's visible pet or pest trouble, and odors are mild.
  • Level 4: Rodent activity, blocked exits, possible mold, and rotting food or dishes left for weeks.
  • Level 5: Heavy structural or sanitation problems. Examples include non-working plumbing, human or animal waste, and rooms filled to the ceiling.

"Extreme" means Level 4 or 5. When you read reviews, look for the ones that describe that level: "we couldn't open the back bedroom," "there were mice everywhere," "took three days." A company with 200 reviews about couch pickups may not have done a single Level 5 job.

Hoarding disorder affects roughly 2–6% of adults, according to the American Psychiatric Association's estimates. That makes it common enough that most Pueblo County crews have seen it. Far fewer have finished the hardest cases.

How to read hoarding cleanup reviews (the part other guides skip)

Hoarding reviews are unusual. Most people who hired a hoarding crew never leave a review, because they don't want a public record tying their name or their parent's name to a hoarded home. So the reviews you find are few, often anonymous, and usually posted by adult children or property managers rather than the person who lived there.

Here's what we'd look for, even when you're checking up on us:

  • Recency. Look for at least 2–3 reviews from the last 12 months. Crews turn over, and a great team from 2021 may not exist anymore.
  • Price language. Look for phrases like "came in at the quote" or "cheaper than estimated." If you see several reviews that say "the price changed on the day," skip that company.
  • Sorting language. Good reviews mention that the crew found things: cash, jewelry, documents, photos. In a Level 4–5 home, finding a birth certificate or a title is a normal part of the job. A crew that just shovels everything into the truck won't get reviews like that.
  • How the owner responds to bad reviews. A defensive reply that blames the customer is a warning sign. It tells you how they'll treat your family if something goes wrong.
  • Photos, carefully. A reputable company doesn't post recognizable photos of a client's home without permission. If a company's page is full of before-and-after shots showing house numbers or family photos on walls, ask yourself whether you'd want your home posted there.

> The most useful review of a hoarding cleanup is rarely the 5-star one. It's the 4-star one that explains what went wrong and how the company fixed it.

What an extreme cleanup costs in Pueblo West

Your total depends on volume, access, and contamination. For a typical Pueblo West ranch home on a half-acre to 1-acre lot, here's what Level 4–5 work usually looks like:

  • Volume: A 3-bedroom, 1,500–1,800 sq ft home at Level 4 usually produces 30–60 cubic yards of material. That's roughly 2–4 full truckloads.
  • Truckload pricing: Full-load junk removal in the Pueblo area generally runs $500–$750 per load. Heavy material like wet paper, soil, and mixed debris costs more because landfill fees are charged by weight.
  • Total job range: Most extreme residential cleanouts we see fall between $2,500 and $8,000. Jobs with a detached garage, shop building, or yard full of vehicles and scrap can go past $10,000.
  • Timeframe: Plan on 1–3 working days for the house itself with a 3–4 person crew. Add a day if you need to sort carefully for documents and keepsakes, which we recommend.

Pueblo West homes often have large outbuildings. The house may be Level 3 while the shop out back is Level 5. Ask for the quote line by line (house, garage, yard, outbuildings) so you can choose what to do now and what to do later. Our [garage cleanout pueblo](/services/garage-cleanout-pueblo) and [yard debris removal pueblo](/services/yard-debris-removal-pueblo) services are priced separately for exactly this reason.

An honest quote for Level 4–5 work should come from an on-site walkthrough, not photos texted over the phone. Photos miss how deep the piles go. If a company gives you a firm number for a Level 5 home without seeing it, expect that number to change.

When this ISN'T worth it — and who should not hire us

This is the section most junk removal websites leave out. Here are the situations where hiring an extreme hoarding cleanup crew is the wrong move, a waste of money, or unsafe.

1. The person who lives there hasn't agreed to it. This is the most common failure. When family members force a full cleanout on someone who's still living in the home, the house often fills back up within 6–12 months, and the relationship can be badly damaged. You'll have paid $5,000 to reset the clock. If the resident still lives there and is resisting, start with a mental health professional who treats hoarding disorder, not a truck. We'll tell you that on the phone, even though it costs us the job.

2. There's biohazard material we aren't equipped to handle. We haul junk. We're not a biohazard remediation company. If the home has an unattended death, large amounts of human waste, blood, or sharps (used needles) scattered through the debris, you need a certified biohazard or trauma cleanup company first. Some homeowners' insurance policies cover this. If a junk company says it'll "just handle it" without the right training, protective equipment, and disposal permits, that's a warning sign.

3. Heavy rodent infestation hasn't been treated. Colorado has one of the higher hantavirus case counts in the U.S., and deer mice are common around Pueblo West's open lots. The CDC recommends wetting droppings with a 1:10 bleach solution and letting it sit 5 minutes before cleanup. Nobody should be sweeping dry droppings. Pest control should come before the haul-out. Any crew that skips this step is putting its workers, and your family if you're helping, at risk.

4. It's animal hoarding. If there are live animals in numbers the home can't support, call Pueblo Animal Services first. A junk crew can't legally or ethically decide what happens to the animals.

5. It's really Level 1–2. If the rooms still work and it's just a lot of stuff, you probably don't need us. A roll-off dumpster for 7–10 days usually runs $400–$650 in this area, and a few relatives with gloves can do the rest. Save the money for the part you can't do yourself, like hauling the old appliances. For that, our [appliance removal pueblo](/services/appliance-removal-pueblo) service is a small, fixed-scope job.

6. You expect the house to be move-in clean when we leave. We leave the home broom-swept, not restored. After a Level 5 job you may still need carpet removal, drywall replacement, odor treatment, and possibly mold remediation. Budget another $2,000–$15,000 for those trades depending on damage. Any review that says "house was perfect afterward" from a Level 5 job either left something out or the company subcontracted the restoration and billed for it.

7. You need an ongoing solution. A cleanout is a one-time event. Hoarding disorder is a long-term condition. Without therapy, a professional organizer, or regular check-ins, relapse is common. We can't fix that part, and we won't pretend we can.

What a good extreme cleanup day actually looks like

A good day looks like this, so you can compare our reviews, and anyone else's, against it:

  • Before the day: A walkthrough, a written line-item quote, and a list of "search for" items from the family, such as wills, deeds, vehicle titles, jewelry, cash, firearms, medications, and specific photos.
  • Morning: The crew wears PPE (N95 or better, gloves, and Tyvek suits in Level 4–5 homes) and clears a path to every exit first.
  • During: Anything that might be valuable or personal goes into a set-aside area, not the truck. A family member or a person you name makes the final call on those items.
  • Donations and recycling: Usable furniture, metal, and appliances go to donation or scrap rather than the Pueblo landfill whenever their condition allows. In a heavily contaminated home, expect less than 10–15% of material to be reusable. Anyone promising "90% recycled" from a Level 5 home isn't being straight with you.
  • End of day: A walkthrough with you, photos of the empty rooms (for you, not our marketing), and a final invoice that matches the quote.

If the home belongs to someone who has died, the process overlaps with an [estate cleanout pueblo](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) job. The difference is pace: estates usually allow more time for sorting and appraisal.

Questions to ask before you book anyone in Pueblo West

Use these whether you call us or a competitor:

1. "Will you do an on-site walkthrough before quoting?" The answer should be yes for anything Level 3 and up. 2. "What happens if the job runs larger than quoted?" Get the per-load or per-hour overage rate in writing. 3. "How much is the deposit?" A deposit over 50% before any work starts is a red flag. 4. "Do you have liability insurance and workers' comp?" Ask to see the certificate. Level 5 work carries real injury risk, and you don't want an uninsured worker hurt on your property. 5. "What won't you take?" Paint, chemicals, propane tanks, tires, and biohazards all have special disposal rules. A crew that says "we take everything" hasn't thought it through. 6. "Can you work around a family member who's present?" Some residents want to be there. The crew should be ready for that and not rush them.
