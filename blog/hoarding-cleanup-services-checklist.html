HOARDING CLEANUP SERVICES CHECKLIST

A hoarding cleanup checklist for Pueblo West has six parts. First, get consent. The person whose home it is should agree to the cleanout, or someone with legal authority should sign off. Second, rate the severity so you know whether you need a junk crew, a biohazard firm, or both. Third, make a "find list" of documents, cash, medications and keepsakes before anything leaves the house. Fourth, protect everyone's health, including against hantavirus, which is a real risk in Pueblo West sheds and outbuildings. Fifth, get a quote based on volume, in writing, with disposal fees included. Sixth, plan what happens afterward, or the house may fill up again. The rest of this page covers each step with the numbers and questions that matter here.

Step 1: Consent and Authority Come Before Anything Else

Your checklist starts with permission, not trucks.

  • If the occupant is alive and competent: they need to agree. A cleanout done to someone usually fails. More on that below.
  • If you're acting for a parent or relative: have a durable power of attorney or guardianship paperwork in hand before you book.
  • If the owner has died: the personal representative named by the Pueblo County court needs to authorize the work. That usually means someone holding Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration. Our [estate cleanout pueblo](/services/estate-cleanout-pueblo) page covers that situation in more detail.
  • If it's a rental: a landlord can't just haul off a tenant's belongings. Colorado has specific rules about abandoned property, so talk to an attorney before anything goes to the curb.

Hoarding disorder affects about 2–6% of adults, according to estimates cited in the DSM-5. It's a recognized mental health condition, not a messy-house problem. That's why consent gets its own line.

Step 2: Rate the Severity Honestly

Two free scales help you describe the job accurately, so the quote you get is one you can trust:

  • The Clutter Image Rating (CIR): a 1–9 photo scale published through the International OCD Foundation. You match each room to a set of photos. Rooms at 4 or higher generally count as clinically significant.
  • The ICD Clutter-Hoarding Scale: a 5-level scale from the Institute for Challenging Disorganization. It covers sanitation, pests, structure and safety, not just how much stuff there is.

A rough guide to who you need:

| Severity | Typical signs | Who you need | |---|---|---| | Level 1–2 / CIR 1–3 | Cluttered but doors, exits and appliances work | Family, possibly a dumpster | | Level 3 / CIR 4–6 | Rooms unusable, paths only, some pests | Junk removal crew plus a family decision-maker | | Level 4–5 / CIR 7–9 | Human or animal waste, rot, structural damage, no working plumbing | Biohazard remediation first, then junk removal |

Take photos of every room, the garage and every outbuilding before you call anyone. Twenty photos give a crew enough to quote within about 10–15% of the final price. A vague phone description can be off by double that.

Step 3: Build a "Find List" Before Anything Leaves

The step most checklists skip is the one families regret most. Hoarded homes often hide cash, uncashed checks, savings bonds, jewelry, firearms and important papers inside piles of junk mail. Write down what you're looking for and give the list to the crew:

  • Vehicle titles, deeds, wills, insurance policies and tax records from the last 7 years
  • Cash and coins. Check books, coat pockets, freezers and envelopes.
  • Prescription medications. Don't throw these in the trash. Take them to a pharmacy take-back kiosk.
  • Firearms and ammunition. A family member should take custody, not the crew.
  • Photos, military records and items the occupant has named as precious

Ask any company you're considering: "Do you sort by hand, or do you shovel?" A by-hand sort with a paper box for suspicious items costs more labor. Budget roughly 30–50% more time than a straight haul-out. For a home where someone's whole life is in the piles, it's usually worth it.

> If the occupant is alive, let them pick a few "keep" categories in advance. Getting them to say yes to five categories goes better than asking them to rule on 5,000 objects one at a time.

Step 4: Health and Safety, Pueblo West Edition

Pueblo West has a risk that in-town Pueblo cleanouts see less often. Many homes sit on lots of a third of an acre to several acres, with sheds, metal storage containers, old campers and detached garages. Deer mice nest in those outbuildings, and deer mice carry hantavirus. Colorado reports hantavirus cases nearly every year.

The CDC's guidance for areas with rodent droppings:

  • Don't sweep or vacuum droppings dry. That kicks the virus into the air.
  • Soak droppings and nesting material with a 1:10 bleach-to-water solution, let it sit for about 5 minutes, then wipe it up.
  • Air out closed sheds for at least 30 minutes before working inside.
  • Wear gloves and an N95 or better respirator.

The indoor checklist:

  • Find and shut off gas to any buried or blocked appliances before moving piles
  • Check floors for soft spots. Years of weight and moisture can weaken subfloors.
  • Look for rattlesnakes in outdoor piles from April through October
  • Watch for sharps, spoiled food and pet waste, which change who should do the work (see the next section)

Disposal rules matter too. Since July 1, 2013, Colorado has banned TVs, computers, monitors and similar electronics from landfills, so those have to go to recycling separately. Refrigerators and freezers need their refrigerant recovered. Our [appliance removal pueblo](/services/appliance-removal-pueblo) page explains how that works.

When Hiring a Hoarding Cleanup Service Is NOT Worth It

Honestly, a junk removal crew is the wrong answer for some homes. Here are the cases.

1. The occupant hasn't agreed and still lives there. Forced cleanouts can traumatize the person and wreck family relationships, and the house often fills up again. Research on hoarding treatment consistently finds that clearing the house without dealing with the underlying behavior doesn't last. The proven approach is cognitive-behavioral therapy built for hoarding, which usually runs about 20–26 sessions. If your parent is refusing, spend your money on a therapist first, not on us. If there's an immediate safety emergency, such as blocked exits or no heat in January, call Pueblo County Adult Protective Services instead.

2. There's biohazard. That means human waste, an unattended death, large amounts of blood, or needles everywhere. A standard junk removal company, including us, isn't the right first call. You need a biohazard remediation company trained under OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard. We can come in afterward for the bulk haul-out. If a crew tells you they'll "just handle" a decomposition scene with contractor bags, that's a reason to hang up.

3. It's animal hoarding. Live animals in poor conditions are a Pueblo Animal Services matter before they're a cleanout matter.

4. It's only Level 1–2. If the doors open, the kitchen works and the clutter is mostly boxes and furniture, a motivated family with a weekend can do it. You'll usually pay about $400–$650 to rent a 20-yard roll-off dumpster on the Front Range for a week. Getting a crew could cost two or three times that. Our [faq](/faq) covers dumpster versus full-service in more detail.

5. You're expecting insurance to pay. Standard homeowners policies almost never cover hoarding cleanup, because it's considered gradual neglect rather than a sudden loss. Don't sign anything on the assumption that a claim will cover it.

6. The house has mold or structural problems. Removing the stuff will expose them, not fix them. Get a mold or structural inspection quote at the same time, so the cleanout isn't the only thing in your budget.

Other limitations to know about: we don't do mental health counseling, we don't appraise valuables, and nobody can promise to find every item on your find list in a home with 40+ cubic yards of material. A careful hand sort reduces the risk. It doesn't remove it.

Step 5: Get a Quote You Can Trust

Hoarding jobs are priced by volume and labor, not square footage. Reference points:

  • A standard junk removal truck holds about 15 cubic yards, roughly the space of a one-car garage packed from floor to ceiling.
  • A full truckload in the Pueblo area is commonly quoted around $500–$750, depending on how heavy the material is and the disposal fees.
  • A Level 3 hoarded home of about 1,500 square feet often runs 30–60 cubic yards, or 2–4 truckloads, before you count outbuildings.
  • Pueblo West outbuildings regularly add another 1–2 loads. Quote them separately so you can decide whether to clear them now or later.
  • Budget a 10–15% contingency. Hoarded homes almost always hold more than the photos show, because piles are deeper than they look.

Questions to ask every company:

  • Is the quote all-in, including landfill fees, electronics recycling, mattress fees and appliance handling?
  • Do you sort by hand and keep a separate box for papers and valuables?
  • What do you donate, and will you give me receipts?
  • Are you insured for work inside the home?
  • Can you work in sessions so the occupant can be part of it?

A Level 3 home usually takes 1–3 working days with a 3–4 person crew. A Level 4 home with remediation first can take one to two weeks from first call to an empty house. For the full scope of what we handle, see our [hoarding cleanout pueblo](/services/hoarding-cleanout-pueblo) service page.

Step 6: Plan What Happens After the Last Truck Leaves

An empty house only stays empty if something changes. Before the cleanout day, decide:

  • Who checks in, and how often. A family visit every 2 weeks for the first 6 months catches problems early, while things are still small.
  • Whether therapy or a peer support group starts before or alongside the cleanout. The Buried in Treasures workshop model is a good place to start.
  • What happens to outbuildings. Many Pueblo West families take down an unsafe shed after it's emptied, so it can't become the next storage spot. Our [shed demolition removal pueblo](/services/shed-demolition-removal-pueblo) page covers that.
  • What happens to the property. If it's being sold, list it within the season. An empty house in Pueblo West's spring winds collects tumbleweeds and break-in attempts.

The Short Version of the Checklist

1. Get consent or legal authority, in writing 2. Rate each room on the CIR or ICD scale and photograph everything 3. Write a find list and give it to the crew 4. Treat rodent areas with a 1:10 bleach solution. Never sweep droppings dry. 5. Hire biohazard remediation first if there's waste, sharps or an unattended death 6. Get an all-in, volume-based quote with outbuildings listed separately 7. Line up treatment and regular check-ins for the months after the cleanout
